INTRODUCTION
The United States files this emergency motion under Circuit Rule
27-3 for an injunction pending appeal to segregate—not to cancel—
ballots cast by ineligible noncitizen voters that are currently being
tabulated in the ongoing Federal election, contrary to Federal criminal
law. See 18 U.S.C. § 611. The Federal Government has identified over
twenty thousand individuals who are “(1) registered to vote in California,
(2) were sent a vote-by-mail ballot, and (3) are highly likely to be
noncitizen aliens.” CR 62-2 (Declaration of Nicolas Bartell (“Bartell
Decl.”) at para. 25; CR 62-3 (Declaration of Eric McLoughlin
(“McLoughlin Decl.”) at para. 25. But California refuses to act and
opposes even the simple step of segregating ballots by these individuals—
some of whom have now been criminally charged with violating 18 U.S.C.
§ 611, which prohibits noncitizen voting in federal elections. See Press
Release, Department of Justice 28 Foreign Nationals Arrested on Charges
that they Illegally Registered and Voted (October 19, 2026), available at
https://perma.cc/27AH-Y9TE.
What is more, Defendants (collectively, California) admit that Los
Angeles County alone has now “processed more than 331,000 [vote-by-

This is a case that must be resolved by November 3. “Every voter in
a federal primary election . . . has a right under the Constitution to have
his vote fairly counted, without its being distorted by fraudulently cast
votes.” Anderson v. United States, 417 U.S. 211, 227 (1974). Yet every
minute of processing that occurs without segregating the ballots of
suspected ineligible voters causes untraceable disenfranchisement and
dilution of the votes of United States citizens. Once an unlawful vote is
processed, it cannot be undone. Accordingly, this Court should
immediately issue an injunction pending appeal that orders California to
set aside vote-by-mail ballots from likely noncitizens identified by the

Federal Government—including ballots cast by the individuals charged
with 18 U.S.C. § 611 crimes this week—until the voter’s citizenship
status can be determined. The United States seeks this relief to prevent
unlawful dilution of American citizens’ votes and to protect the integrity
of our elections. See Anderson, 417 U.S. at 227.
STATEMENT
This case concerns the State of California’s refusal to segregate the
ballots of tens of thousands of individuals identified by the Federal
Government as highly likely to be ineligible to vote.
1. The Federal Government includes several agencies and sub-
agencies with authority to evaluate individuals’ citizenship. The United
States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) is responsible for
adjudicating immigrant visa and naturalization applications. See 6
U.S.C. § 271. The Fraud Detection and National Security Directorate
(FDNS) is an agency within USCIS that “safeguards the integrity of the
nation’s lawful immigration system by leading USCIS’ efforts to combat
fraud, detect national security and public safety threats, and maximize
law enforcement and Intelligence Community partnerships.” FDNS
Mission, available at https://perma.cc/4SHH-GAQX. Its expertise and
11
Case: 26-7070, 10/23/2026, DktEntry: 6.1, (17 of 43)
mission encompass noncitizen fraud in federal elections. See CR 62-2
(Bartell Decl.) at para. 13. Homeland Security Investigations (HSI),
which resides within Immigration and Customs Enforcement, serves as
the primary investigative agency enforcing America’s immigration laws
and maintains records and information on individuals who are being
investigated for violations of the United States’ immigration laws or
regulations. 6 U.S.C. § 252.
USCIS receives state voter rolls or information on state voter rolls
to review for potential aliens who unlawfully registered to vote or who
voted in a federal election. See CR 62-2 (Bartell Decl.) at para. 13. This
data gathering process is distinct from the Systematic Alien Verification
for Entitlements (SAVE) program. Id. at para. 15. When presented with
information on voter rolls from agencies like DHS, USCIS processes that
information to identify registered voters who are likely aliens. Id. at para.
14; see also CR 62-3 (McLoughlin Decl.) at para. 14.
USCIS begins its process by enhancing the voter data it receives
through a reliable third-party data analytics and investigative platform.
CR 62-2 (Bartell Decl.) at para. 14 . The raw voting record data are
combined with the enhanced data and then paired with an internal
12
Case: 26-7070, 10/23/2026, DktEntry: 6.1, (18 of 43)
USCIS alien number to identify the same individual. Id. at para. 16.
USCIS then scores and ranks the potential identity match to account for
incomplete, outdated, or mismatched information. Id.
Following a preliminary identity match, USCIS screens potential
matches against Department of State and Social Security Administration
data to remove individuals who can be identified as United States
citizens (or as deceased), and then it excludes those matches from further
review. Id. at para. 17. Afterwards, USCIS consolidates all match
evidence and assigns a confidence level for each match. Id. at para.18. A
high confidence match means the match between the voting record and
USCIS alien record is “strong and consistent with multifield evidence
supporting a strong identity link.” Id. At that point, USCIS has
completed its initial review.
Based on the results of that initial review, the FDNS investigators
within USCIS manually review every match to confirm the identity
match and determine which registered voters they believe are very likely
to be aliens based on USCIS’ citizenship data. Id. at para.19. Part of that
investigation includes verifying whether the registered voter could have
potentially derived citizenship based on history or through parentage. Id.
13
Case: 26-7070, 10/23/2026, DktEntry: 6.1, (19 of 43)
Investigators also confirm the match with Department of State systems
to identify if any documents (e.g., passports) might not have been linked
to USCIS systems, but which bear on the individual’s citizenship status.
Id. A second-level review of the match and citizenship status
determination occurs after all of that process, as an additional safeguard.
Id. After completion of the multilevel and additional-safeguard review,
USCIS makes a final determination on which matches to send to HSI for
further assessment. Id. at para. 20.
Once USCIS and FDNS complete their analysis and send their
work product, HSI performs an initial intake, which includes a quality
review that identifies duplicate entries, incomplete records, missing
identifiers, data quality issues, and the like. See CR 62-3 (McLoughlin
Decl.) at para. 16. After that intake, HSI prioritizes the records for review
and prepares the data for additional enrichment using approved analytic
capabilities. Id.
HSI’s enrichment process focuses on improving the completeness
and consistency of identification information and, where lawfully
permitted, identifying additional unique identifiers that may assist in
reliable matching of the voters to their immigration status. Id. at para.17.

HSI’s enrichment process can perform data matching across multiple
authorized data sources and consolidate disparate records into a unified
set of matching results. Id. The purpose of enrichment is to reduce the
risk that voters are mistakenly associated with the wrong immigration
status. Id. That process adds value by applying consistent quality
controls and enrichment processes before voter records are disseminated
as investigative referrals or routed for another authorized purpose. Id. at
para.18. Then, after its investigation, HSI may ask USCIS to perform
additional manual review of the relevant matches. Id. Once the HSI
review is completed, each individual data profile is referred to the
applicable HSI field office where it will be investigated for potential
criminal violations. Id. at para.19.

forwarded it to USCIS and FDNS on or around May 8, 2026, see CR 62-2

A. The Federal Government is likely to prevail on

1. Under the Supremacy Clause, the “Constitution, and the Laws of
the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof . . . shall be
the supreme Law of the Land[.]” U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2; Arizona v.
United States, 567 U.S. 387, 399 (2012) (“The Supremacy Clause
provides a clear rule that federal law” is supreme over state law). Federal
preemption principles ensure the constitutional supremacy of federal
law. Under those principles, contrary “state law must yield to the
regulation of Congress within the sphere of its delegated power.” Savage
v. Jones, 225 U.S. 501, 533 (1912). And federal law can preempt state law
“[e]ven without an express provision for preemption.” Crosby v. Nat’l
Foreign Trade Council, 530 U.S. 363, 372 (2000). That includes where
“state law stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of
the full purposes and objectives of Congress,” id. at 373 (citations
omitted) (cleaned up), or otherwise “directly conflict[s]” with federal law,
Kansas v. Garcia, 140 S. Ct. 791, 808 (2020) (Thomas, J., concurring).
23

State action must “give way” when it conflicts with “a considered
judgment” embodied in federal law. Arizona, 567 U.S. at 399, 405. 1
States cannot avoid preemption by facilitating a private party to
violate federal law. “It is . . . axiomatic that a state may not induce,
encourage or promote private persons to accomplish what it is
constitutionally forbidden to accomplish.” Norwood v. Harrison, 413 U.S.
455, 465 (1973). For example, if a state purported to subsidize federal
crimes, that would conflict with the considered judgment of Congress.
See, e.g., Musta v. Mendota Heights Dental Ctr., 965 N.W.2d 312, 321-27
& n.11 (Minn. 2021) (federal law preempted a requirement that an
employer subsidize the purchase of a federally prohibited drug), cert
denied, 142 S. Ct. 2834 (Mem.) (2022).
When evaluating the obstacle that state action poses to federal
aims, courts “examin[e] the federal statute as a whole and identify[] its
purpose and intended effects[.]” Crosby, 530 U.S. at 373. To illustrate,
when a State “authorizes [persons] to engage in conduct that the federal

[law] forbids, it ‘stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and

and Freezers Ass’n, Inc. v. Agricultural Marketing and Bargaining Bd.,
467 U.S. 461, 478 (1984) (quoting Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67
(1941)). At bottom, obstacle preemption exists wherever a state
“undermines the intended purpose and natural effect” of a federal
statute. Crosby, 530 U.S. at 373 (internal quotation marks omitted).
These preemption principles apply when state officials act in a
manner that obstructs or conflicts with federal law. “It has long been a
settled principle that federal courts may enjoin unconstitutional action
by state officials.” Puerto Rico v. Branstad, 483 U.S. 219, 228 (1987)
(emphasis added) (citing Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908)).
Accordingly, whether state and local officials “act[] through . . .
procedures rather than a court order or a general law . . . is irrelevant to
[the] analysis.” Golden State Transit Corp. v. City of Los Angeles, 475 U.S.
608, 614 n.5 (1986). Preemption does not turn on the form of state action.
It “turns on the actual content of [a state’s] policy and its real effect on
federal rights.” Livadas v. Bradshaw, 512 U.S. 107, 119 (1994) (citing and
adopting Livadas v. Aubry, 987 F.2d 552, 561 (9th Cir. 1991) (Kozinski,

Case: 26-7070, 10/23/2026, DktEntry: 6.1, (31 of 43)
J., dissenting) (“The point is, the Commissioner is applying state law and
his own policy in a way that impinges on protected federal rights.”)).
Courts have thus held that federal law preempts a wide range of
conflicting state actions. Where state officials operating a state
university had a “policy” of “preferential treatment” regarding “in-state
status” that the Supreme Court deemed inconsistent with federal
immigration law, that “policy” itself “violate[d] the Supremacy Clause.”
Moreno, 458 U.S. at 17. Preemption likewise applied when state officials
acted to “encourag[e] racial segregation” in violation of the Equal
Protection Clause. United States v. City of Jackson, 318 F.2d 1, 3–4 (5th
Cir. 1963). Or when California itself sought to pursue civil litigation in
state court to set emissions standards in violation of the Clean Air Act.
See Daimler Truck N. Am. LLC v. California Air Res. Bd., 2025 WL
3049944, at *17–*18 (E.D. Cal. Oct. 31, 2025). A “conflict in technique
can be fully as disruptive to the system Congress enacted as [a] conflict
in overt policy” written in a statute or regulation. Arizona, 567 U.S. at
406. After all, Ex parte Young, through which litigants there sought to
enjoin unconstitutional state action, does not focus on the validity of state
law; it focuses on whether “a state official who enforces that law ‘comes

B. The other injunction factors strongly favor the
government.
The United States is likely to succeed on the merits and suffer
irreparable harm. Critically, the public interest and balance of the
equities overwhelmingly support an injunction requiring Defendants to
segregate ballots of individuals known to the Federal Government and
Defendants to be highly likely ineligible to vote. Such an extraordinary
remedy is necessary to preserve the status quo, protect the integrity of
our elections, and prevent potentially tens of thousands of violations of
Federal criminal law.
Irreparable harm necessarily results from the enforcement of a
preempted state law. See New Orleans Pub. Serv., Inc. v. Council of the
City of New Orleans, 491 U.S. 350, 366–67 (1989) (irreparable injury may
be established “by a showing that the challenged state statute is
flagrantly and patently violative of . . . the express constitutional
prescription of the Supremacy Clause” (citation omitted)); Valle del Sol
Inc. v. Whiting, 732 F.3d 1006, 1029 (9th Cir. 2013) (irreparable harm
based on Supremacy Clause violation); Baird v. Bonta, 81 F.4th 1036,
33
Case: 26-7070, 10/23/2026, DktEntry: 6.1, (39 of 43)
1040 (9th Cir. 2023) (“It is well-established that the first factor is
especially important when a plaintiff alleges a constitutional violation
and injury. If a plaintiff in such a case shows he is likely to prevail on the
merits, that showing usually demonstrates he is suffering irreparable
harm no matter how brief the violation.”). This follows from the simple
fact that the United States suffers a “per se irreparable harm” when state
actions violate the Supremacy Clause. United States v. Texas, 557
F.Supp.3d 810, 821 (W.D. Tex. 2021) (collecting cases); Daimler, 2025 WL
3049944, at *17 (same). Indeed, the Supreme Court has repeatedly
recognized that the “inability to enforce its duly enacted [law] clearly
inflicts irreparable harm on” the United States. Abbott v. Perez, 585 U.S.
579, 602 n.17 (2018); see also Maryland v. King, 567 U.S. 1301, 1303
(2012) (Roberts, C.J., in chambers) (same).
The harm here is also quintessentially irreparable because
California’s mishandling of ballots cannot be undone. Under California
law, election officials open ballots––and then separate for all time––the
ballot from the envelope. Once this occurs, it cannot be undone. There is
no further way for officials to track or trace ballots cast by individuals
later determined to be ineligible to vote. Nor is there any way for Federal
34

law enforcement to trace these as part of criminal investigations. As we
write, California election officials are tabulating votes. The only way to
preserve the rapidly eroding status quo is for an injunction pending
appeal to require California to segregate the ballots Federal officials have
identified as associated with ineligible voters. Otherwise, what is done
can never be undone.
The balance of equities and the public interest likewise favor the
United States. Because the challenged laws are invalid under the
Supremacy Clause, they are not in the public interest. Am. Trucking
Ass’ns, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles, 559 F.3d 1046, 1060 (9th Cir. 2009)
(public interest represented by “the Constitution’s declaration that
federal law is to be supreme”). Indeed, it is emphatically contrary to the
public interest to allow ineligible voters to have their votes cast and
tabulated in a Federal election. This dilutes the votes of lawful voters. It
directly affects which individuals will serve in Federal offices. It also
facilitates violation of criminal law. 18 U.S.C. § 611. In particular, the
failure to at least segregate ballots of the identified individuals forever
impairs the ability of election officials to understand the effect of their
votes on the election. It also significantly inhibits Federal law

enforcement investigations into whether the identified individuals did in
fact violate Federal law by voting in a Federal election. Any comparable
burden on Defendants associated with segregating ballots is de minimis
in comparison. Segregating ballots is a targeted and narrow remedy, and
is something Defendants already do with provisional ballots. The public
interest and balance of the equities strongly support enjoining
Defendants to segregate ballots of individuals identified as ineligible to
vote.
CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons, this Court should grant an immediate
injunction pending appeal that requires California (1) to set aside vote-
by-mail ballots from persons identified by the Federal Government
likely to be noncitizens and (2) to independently—or in collaboration
with the Federal Government—verify their citizenship status before
tabulating their vote-by-mail ballot. The United States seeks this relief
to prevent unlawful dilution of American citizens’ votes and to protect
the integrity of our elections.
