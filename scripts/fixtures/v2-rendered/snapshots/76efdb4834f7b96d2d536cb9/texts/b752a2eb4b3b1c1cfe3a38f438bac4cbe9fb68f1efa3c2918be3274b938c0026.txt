INTRODUCTION
1. Federal law prohibits noncitizens from voting in federal elections. See 18
U.S.C. § 611(a). That law is an exercise of Congress’s clear constitutional power to
regulate the domestic conduct of noncitizens and to regulate the manner of federal
elections. Nothwithstanding the important role of the Federal Government to regulate the
manner of federal elections, States also play a part, but they—and their election officials—
cannot knowingly violate federal law when they administer federal elections.
2. For each election in California, the State automatically sends all registered
voters vote-by-mail ballots. Those ballots do not bifurcate between state, local, and federal
elections. Nor do they warn aliens that they are prohibited to vote in federal elections.
While California reviews signatures before tabulating cast ballots, there is a strong
presumption of validity even for ballots signed not with a name but with a simple “X.”
3. Based on a thorough analysis of voter rolls and federal data, agencies within
the Department of Homeland Security found thousands of likely noncitizens registered to
vote in California who would automatically be sent federal ballots under State law. Thus,
months before California started sending vote-by-mail ballots to all registered voters, the
Federal Government alerted the California Secretary of State that a preliminary review of
California’s voter role revealed that numerous likely noncitizens were registered to vote
in California. See Ex. A. The Federal Government asked California to work
collaboratively to identify any noncitizens registered to vote in the State. California made
no serious effort to do so, see Ex. B, and as required by State law, began sending vote-by-
mail ballots to all registered voters in early October—including, it seems, to those who the
Federal Government considered as likely noncitizens.
4. Days ago, the Federal Government reiterated this serious concern with
exacting details. It sent relevant California officials a list of over 20,000 names of people
the Federal Government believes are likely noncitizens who were sent a vote-by-mail
ballot from California to participate in the federal election. See Exs. C & D. The Federal
Government asked California to review the list and, in partnership with federal agencies,

called into question. This Court should enjoin California from presently tabulating vote-

be noncitizens and (2) to independently—or in collaboration with the Federal
Government—verify their citizenship status (beyond relying on unverified statements of

United States seeks this relief to prevent unlawful dilution of American citizens’ votes and
to protect the integrity of our elections.

LEGAL BACKGROUND
I. Federal Law Prohibits Noncitizens from Voting in Federal Elections
17. It is generally “unlawful for any alien to vote in any election held solely or in
part for the purpose of electing a candidate for the office of President, Vice President,
Presidential elector, Member of the Senate, Member of the House of Representatives,
Delegate from the District of Columbia, or Resident Commissioner.” 18 U.S.C. § 611(a).
18. A noncitizen may vote in such an election if (a) “the election is held partly
for some other purpose,” (b) a state or local law allows noncitizens to vote, and (c) voting
“is conducted independently of voting for a candidate for such Federal offices, in such a
manner that an alien has the opportunity to vote for such other purpose, but not an
opportunity to vote for a candidate for any one or more of such Federal offices.” Id.
19. A noncitizen who violates the voting ban may be fined or imprisoned. Id.

§ 611(b). Persons also can be in violation of federal law for aiding and abetting, or
otherwise facilitating, the federal crime of noncitizen voting. See 18 U.S.C. § 2.
20. A noncitizen who “express[es] [his] preference or opinion in a[n] . . . election
by ballot” violates the federal statutory ban on noncitizen voting, unless the noncitizen had
no opportunity to vote for a candidate seeking federal office. See Vote, Black’s Law
Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); 18 U.S.C. § 611(a); see also United States v. Knight, 490 F.3d
1268, 1271 (11th Cir. 2007) (finding 18 U.S.C. § 611 to be a general intent crime where
the government needs only to “prove that the defendant knowingly engaged in the conduct
prohibited” (citation omitted)).
II. California’s Vote-by-Mail Regime
A. California’s Vote-by-Mail Ballots
21. California has permitted voters to cast ballots by mail since 1922, see
Peterson v. City of San Diego, 34 Cal. 3d 225, 228-29 (1983), but it has dramatically
extended its vote-by-mail access in recent years.
22. Originally in an emergency response to the COVID-19 pandemic, the
California Legislature passed a bill requiring every county to implement an all-mailed-
ballot election system for the 2020 general election. See 2020 Cal. Stat. ch. 4 (codified as
amended at Cal. Elec. Code § 3000.5). After the election, the legislature made that system
permanent. See 2021 Cal. Stat. ch. 312 (codified at Cal. Elec. Code § 3000.5).
23. Under the current regime, state election officials are required to send
unsolicited ballots to “every registered voter” in the State, no later than 29 days before
“each election.” Cal. Elec. Code § 3000.5.
24. All vote-by-mail ballot identification envelopes that are sent out contain “[a]
warning plainly stamped or printed on it that voting twice constitutes a crime” but include
no such similar warning for the crime of voting as a noncitizen in a federal election. See
Cal. Elec. Code § 3011(a)(6); see also id. § 3011.
25. The California Legislature passed a law allowing counties to opt in to an all-
mailed-ballot election. Thus, counties can implement, writ large, the State’s permissive

mail-in voting procedures, which includes the lax signature verification requirements as
discussed further below. See California’s Voter’s Choice Act, 2016 Cal. Stat. ch. 832
(codified as amended at Cal. Elec. Code § 4005); see also Cal. Elec. Code § 3019.
B. Election Officials Count a Vote-by-Mail Ballot if They Verify the
Signature
26. For a vote-by-mail ballot to count in an election, the voter must sign the
ballot. Cal. Elec. Code § 3011(a)(2). California “shall [then] compare the signature”
between the vote-by-mail ballot received and the voter’s signature contained in its records
to determine whether the voter is an eligible voter. Id. § 3019.
27. California law requires election officials to “liberally construe[]” signature
verification of vote-by-mail ballots “in the favor of the voter.” Cal. Code Regs. tit. 2,
§ 20990(c) (2022). Indeed, “[a] presumption exists that the signature on the identification
envelope, signature verification statement, unsigned identification envelope statement, or
provisional ballot envelope is the voter’s signature and that the vote will be counted.” Cal.
Elec. Code § 3019(a)(2)(A); accord Cal. Code Regs. tit. 2, § 20960(c). Exact signature
matches are not required; signatures with similar characteristics suffice. Id. §
3019(a)(2)(B); accord id. § 20960(d)-(e).
28. Only a signature possessing “multiple, significant, and obvious differing
characteristics with all signatures in the voter’s registration record will be subject to
additional review by the elections official.” Id. § 20960(i). Even then, if an initial reviewer
identifies multiple, significant, and obvious differing characteristics, the vote will be
rejected only if “two different elections officials unanimously find beyond a reasonable
doubt that the signature differs in multiple, significant, and obvious respects from all
signatures in the voter’s registration record.” Id. § 20960(j). What is more, a signature
made using a mark, “such as an ‘X’ . . . is presumed valid” and should be counted. Id.
§ 20960(l).
29. California’s signature verification statement requires the voter to affirm that
she or he is a registered voter and is a resident of the precinct in which they voted. But the

Cal. Code Regs. tit. 2, § 20991(b).

I. Federal Agencies’ Process to Identify Likely Noncitizens on Voter Rolls in
Federal Elections
A. USCIS’ Processes to Determine Likely Noncitizens on Voter Rolls
33. USCIS regularly conducts investigative processes whereby lists of registered
voters or information about voters on voter rolls are analyzed to determine whether anyone
registered to vote in a federal election is a noncitizen. California’s voter roster containing
voter registration information is made available for purchase. See Cal. Elec. Code § 2188.1
34. To determine whether noncitizens are on voter rolls, USCIS compares
information about voter rolls with its internal data to identify potential matches between
registered voters and possible noncitizens. If USCIS identifies a registered voter in a
federal election who is also likely a noncitizen, it conducts additional investigative
processes to determine what level of confidence—high, medium, or low—it has that the
registered voter is a noncitizen. “High confidence” means that the match between voter
information on the voting roll and USCIS’ internal records is strong and consistent based
on multiple identifiers that support a strong identity link.
35. Voter rolls or information about voter rolls provided to USCIS typically do
not contain complete information on an individual. So, after receiving a voter roll or
information about voters on voter rolls, USCIS utilizes third-party software to identify
high, medium, or low confidence matches between voter information and information
about that individual compiled by the software program. Medium or low confidence
matches reflect incomplete, outdated, or mismatched data on the individual.
36. USCIS next takes the high-confidence match information and connects it
with an alien number in its system, which is informed by immigration-related interactions
between the individual and the agency. USCIS then produces a table of individuals who
are high-confidence matches from the voter rolls with a verified alien number.
37. USCIS next scours internal records for any evidence of naturalization and

CLAIM FOR RELIEF
Violation of the Supremacy Clause as Applied to California’s Actions of Knowingly
Sending Vote-By-Mail Ballots to Likely Noncitizens and Ultimately Counting Those
Ballots in Violation of 18 U.S.C. § 611
50. The United States incorporates all allegations stated above.
51. The Supremacy Clause in Article VI of the U.S. Constitution declares: “This
Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof
. . . shall be the supreme Law of the Land.” U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2.
52. The Elections Clause in Article I of the U.S. Constitution grants Congress
significant regulatory authority in federal elections because “Congress may at any time by
Law make or alter such Regulations, except as to the Places of chusing Senators.”
Otherwise, “The Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and
Representatives, shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof.” U.S. Const.
art. I, § 4, cl. 1.
53. Congress likewise has broad power to regulate the conduct of aliens in this
country, pursuant to its foreign-commerce and foreign-affairs powers. See Arizona v.
United States, 567 U.S. 387, 394–96 (2012); Toll v. Moreno, 458 U.S. 1, 10–11 (1982).
54. Under the Supremacy Clause, courts may find preemption where “state law
stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and
objectives of Congress.” Crosby v. Nat’l Foreign Trade Council, 530 U.S. 363, 373 (2000)
(citations omitted) (cleaned up). “What is a sufficient obstacle is a matter of judgment, to
be informed by examining the federal statute as a whole and identifying its purpose and
intended effects.” Id. “There is no ‘rigid formula or rule’ for determining when” a state
law creates such an obstacle that it is preempted by federal law. Chamber of Com. of the
United States of Am. v. Bonta, 62 F.4th 473, 482 (9th Cir. 2023).
55. If California passed a law that allowed noncitizens to vote for a candidate
12
Case 2:26-cv-12025 Document 1 Filed 10/15/26 Page 13 of 15 Page ID #:13
who is running for a seat in the U.S. Senate or the U.S. House of Representatives, that
state law would conflict with federal law, see 18 U.S.C. § 611(a), and would violate the
Supremacy Clause. After all, if a state law is contrary to an act of Congress or our Nation’s
Constitution, the state law must yield. See Mut. Pharm. Co. v. Bartlett, 570 U.S. 472, 479–
480 (2013) (“[I]t has long been settled that state laws that conflict with federal law are
‘without effect.’” (quoting Maryland v. Louisiana, 451 U.S. 725, 746 (1981)).
56. Because California cannot pass a statute allowing noncitizens to vote in a
federal election, it also cannot induce, encourage, promote, or knowingly facilitate
noncitizen voting for a candidate who is running for a seat in the U.S. Senate or the U.S.
House of Representatives. See Norwood v. Harrison, 413 U.S. 455, 465 (1973) (“It is also
axiomatic that a state may not induce, encourage or promote private persons to accomplish
what it is constitutionally forbidden to accomplish.”). Nor can California allow a
noncitizen to vote in the federal election when it has knowledge about the voter’s
citizenship status, as noncitizens cannot vote in federal elections. 18 U.S.C. § 611(a).
57. The Federal Government notified California months ago that numerous
noncitizens may be registered to vote in California. Despite knowing that California must
send vote-by-mail ballots to every registered voter, the State made no serious attempt to
work with the Federal Government to determine whether noncitizens are registered to
vote.
58. The Federal Government then provided California with a list of over 20,000
specific individuals who Federal agencies believe are registered to vote in federal elections
in California and are likely noncitizens. Despite knowing that California must count a
vote-by-mail ballot after verifying the signature—even a “signature” that says only “X”—
the State refused to independently verify the citizenship of people included on the list. Nor
would they agree to work with the Federal Government to verify citizenship status before
tabulating those voters’ votes.
59. In this context, California’s actions are preempted by 18 U.S.C. § 611(a)
insofar that they frustrate the very purpose and effect of the federal prohibition against
13

noncitizens voting in federal elections. The State knows that noncitizens might be
registered to vote, and it knows the names of such individuals. But it still intends to count
their vote without verifying the voter’s citizenship. California’s actions thus knowingly
facilitate unlawful voting.
60. To be sure, the United States actively investigates and prosecutes incidents
of unlawful voting. But it is not practicable or feasible for the United States to investigate
before the 2026 election concludes thousands, let alone tens of thousands, of individuals
that two federal investigative offices identified on the California voter roster as likely
being sent vote-by-mail ballots. California is thus knowingly imposing an obstacle on the
United States’ ability to enforce 18 U.S.C. § 611 by facilitating unlawful voting rather
than working with the Federal Government to prevent it.
61. Accordingly, California’s actions to send vote-by-mail ballots and ultimately
count those ballots, notwithstanding knowledge of noncitizen voting, are preempted as
applied to the individuals the Federal Government identified for California.
