Key Highlights The FAA certified Boeing's (NYSE:BA) 737 Max 7, the smallest model in the aircraft family, after nearly a decade of delays. The certification required key design improvements, including updates to flight-control software, the flightcrew alerting system and a redesigned engine anti-ice system. Southwest Airlines (NYSE:LUV) , the plane's launch customer, said the certification supports its plans to modernize its all-Boeing 737 fleet. Boeing shares rose more than 7% in afternoon trading Monday, with the company holding roughly 40 737-7 and 737 Max 10 aircraft already built and in inventory. A Milestone Nearly a Decade in the Making The Federal Aviation Administration certified Boeing's (NYSE:BA) 737 Max 7 on Monday, clearing the smallest model in the company's bestselling aircraft family for commercial service after a certification process delayed by close to a decade. The agency said the approval reflects years of sustained work to resolve complex technical issues and complete a thorough review of the airplane's design and supporting safety analyses. Carriers including Southwest Airlines (NYSE:LUV) had originally expected to begin flying the aircraft before the pandemic, but increased regulatory scrutiny following two fatal crashes involving the in-service 737 Max 8 in 2018 and 2019, along with subsequent safety and manufacturing crises and a required redesign of the engine anti-icing system, pushed certification back significantly. The FAA specified that the Max 7 was required to incorporate updates to flight-control software, the flightcrew alerting system and a redesigned anti-ice system engineered to prevent engine overheating. Southwest and Boeing Prepare for First Delivery Southwest Airlines said the certification represents an important step toward modernizing its all-Boeing 737 fleet with upgraded interiors and more fuel-efficient aircraft aligned with customer preferences and demand patterns. Boeing said it and Southwest are now preparing for delivery of the first aircraft, though airlines typically require several months to work newly certified planes into their operating schedules even after certification is granted. Boeing's president and chief executive of its commercial airplanes division said the certification validates the rigor of the aircraft's design and reflects the determination of the company's 737 Max development team throughout the extended review process. The company still awaits FAA certification of the 737 Max 10, the largest model in the family, which some carriers had originally expected to enter service as early as 2020, alongside continued anticipation for certification of the manufacturer's largest aircraft, the 777X. Certification Carries Meaningful Financial Implications for Boeing Boeing has approximately 40 737-7 and 737 Max 10 aircraft already built and sitting in inventory, and the newly granted certification could help convert that inventory into revenue, since manufacturers receive the bulk of an aircraft's purchase price upon delivery to the customer rather than at the time of manufacture. This dynamic makes certification milestones particularly consequential for near-term cash flow, given that completed but undelivered aircraft represent capital tied up without corresponding revenue recognition. Boeing shares rose more than 7% in afternoon trading Monday, reflecting investor recognition of the certification's potential financial impact. The approval builds on other recent progress in Boeing's relationship with the FAA, including a mid-July decision allowing the company to resume issuing its own airworthiness certificates for the 737 Max and 787 Dreamliner, an authority the agency had revoked following the Max 8 crashes that killed 346 people. Conclusion The certification of the 737 Max 7 marks a significant milestone in Boeing's broader effort to rebuild regulatory trust and stabilize production following years of safety and manufacturing crises. With inventory aircraft now positioned for delivery and a new production line recently opened in Everett, Washington, the certification could meaningfully support near-term cash flow as the company continues ramping up output of its 737 Max and 787 Dreamliner programs. Whether this momentum extends to the still-pending 737 Max 10 and 777X certifications is likely to shape the pace of Boeing's broader financial recovery in coming quarters. FAQs Q: Why did certification of the Boeing 737 Max 7 take nearly a decade? A: Certification was delayed by increased regulatory scrutiny following two fatal crashes involving the in-service 737 Max 8 in 2018 and 2019, along with subsequent safety and manufacturing crises. A required redesign of the aircraft's engine anti-icing system also contributed to the extended timeline. Q: How does this certification benefit Boeing financially? A: Boeing has approximately 40 737-7 and 737 Max 10 aircraft already built and in inventory, and manufacturers typically receive the bulk of an aircraft's price upon delivery rather than at the time of manufacture. Certification allows the company to begin converting this inventory into delivered aircraft and corresponding revenue. Q: What other Boeing aircraft models are still awaiting FAA certification? A: Boeing is still awaiting certification of the 737 Max 10, the largest model in the 737 Max family, as well as the 777X, the company's largest aircraft overall. Some carriers had originally expected the Max 10 to enter service as early as 2020.
