INTRODUCTION

Less than three weeks before Election Day, and with ballots already in the

hands of California voters, the U.S. Department of Justice (“DOJ”) asks this Court

to deny ballots to lawfully registered voters and set aside ballots already cast by

others. The targeted voters—over 20,000 in total—were identified on a list derived

through an error-prone matching process and delivered to local officials on a thumb

drive, with a password on a sticky note, which DOJ gave officials only 48 hours to

review before DOJ filed its lawsuit seeking to compel California to do its bidding.

No federal law authorizes DOJ’s extraordinary demand, which would upend

California’s elections and would result in an unprecedented intrusion of the federal

government into state-run election administration. The Constitution assigns States

the authority to set and enforce voter qualifications and administer their elections,

subject to certain safeguards enacted by Congress and designed to protect voters

during federal elections. Among them, the National Voter Registration Act

(“NVRA”) bars systematic programs to remove ineligible voters from the rolls in

the 90 days before an election, because that is when mistakes based on flawed data

are most likely to disenfranchise eligible voters. 52 U.S.C. § 20507(c)(2)(A).

DOJ’s demand, which arrives well inside that window, seeks an end-run around

that statutory protection and constitutional structure—all in pursuit of strong-

arming California into aligning its election procedures with the preferences of the

President. Put simply, DOJ seeks to enlist this Court in an effort to take over

California’s elections and decide who gets to have their votes count. DOJ’s effort

is lawless.

(“SIREN”), and Mi Familia en Acción (“MFA”)—are nonprofit organizations that

represent and serve California voters. They move to intervene as Defendants under

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 24 to protect their interests and the interests of the

BACKGROUND

I.

Federal law assigns election administration responsibilities to

States, consistent with the Constitution’s separation of powers.

The Constitution’s Elections Clause “invests the States with responsibility

for the mechanics of congressional elections,” subject only to Congress’s power to

“preempt state legislative choices.” Foster v. Love, 522 U.S. 67, 69 (1997). Even

then, Congress’s power is strictly limited to preempting State laws on the “Times,

Places and Manner of holding Elections.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1. Nothing in

that limited grant of authority empowers the federal government to decide who gets

to vote; the Constitution commits the power to set and enforce voter qualifications

to the States. See Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council of Ariz., Inc., 570 U.S. 1, 16–17

(2013). This structure is deliberate: since the founding, the Elections Clause “has

constitutionally prevented the centralization of election management in the

Executive.” Weber, 816 F. Supp. 3d at 1175.

Congress has enacted certain laws governing voter registration, but nothing

in those laws empowers the federal government to create a list of registered voters

whom it “believes” to be noncitizens and then demand that States deny them access

to the ballot box. Compl. ¶ 45. To the contrary, the NVRA charges States with the

“administration of voter registration for elections for Federal office.” 52 U.S.C.

The NVRA also imposes strict procedural safeguards on removing voters

from the rolls, which DOJ seeks to circumvent here. Most notably, the NVRA

requires States to “complete, not later than 90 days prior to the date of a primary or

general election for Federal office, any program the purpose of which is to

programs targeting suspected noncitizens. See Mi Familia Vota, 129 F.4th at 715–

erroneous “maintenance” is most likely to disenfranchise entirely eligible voters,

who might have little or no time to correct the error. See Arcia v. Fla. Sec’y of State,

772 F.3d 1335, 1346 (11th Cir. 2014). DOJ’s eleventh-hour demand here to have

California refuse to send ballots to some voters and refuse to count ballots cast by

others is a blatant attempt to make an end-run around this requirement.

LEGAL STANDARD

A party seeking to intervene as of right must satisfy four requirements:

(1) the applicant must timely move to intervene; (2) the

applicant must have a significantly protectable interest

relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the

action; (3) the applicant must be situated such that the

disposition of the action may impair or impede the party’s

ability to protect that interest; and (4) the applicant’s interest

must not be adequately represented by existing parties.

Arakaki v. Cayetano, 324 F.3d 1078, 1083 (9th Cir. 2003).

Rule 24(a)(2) “traditionally receives liberal construction in favor of

applicants for intervention,” id., and the Ninth Circuit requires “interpret[ing] these

requirements broadly in favor of intervention,” guided by “practical considerations,

not technical distinctions.” W. Watersheds Project v. Haaland, 22 F.4th 828, 835

(9th Cir. 2022) (quoting Citizens for Balanced Use v. Mont. Wilderness Ass’n, 647

F.3d 893, 897 (9th Cir. 2011)). Courts must “take all well-pleaded, nonconclusory

allegations in the motion to intervene, the proposed complaint or answer in

intervention, and declarations supporting the motion as true absent sham, frivolity

or other objections.” Sw. Ctr. for Biological Diversity v. Berg, 268 F.3d 810, 820

(9th Cir. 2001).

“A motion for permissive intervention pursuant to Rule 24(b) is directed to

the sound discretion of the district court.” San Jose Mercury News, Inc. v. U.S. Dist.

Ct., 187 F.3d 1096, 1100 (9th Cir. 1999). Only two factors must be satisfied where,

The Ninth Circuit has also identified additional considerations the Court may

consider in weighing permissive intervention requests. See Callahan v. Brookdale

Senior Living Cmtys., Inc., 42 F.4th 1013, 1022 (9th Cir. 2022). Those

considerations—including Proposed Intervenors’ interests, the inadequacy of the

representation of those interests, and the intervenors’ contribution to the factual and

legal issues, see id.—buttress the case for intervention here. Proposed Intervenors

have significant interests at stake, see supra Argument § I.B, and their involvement

will center the rights of voters who are most likely to be impacted by DOJ’s attempt

to interfere with California’s voting procedures. See, e.g., In re Exch. Union Co.,

No. 24-mc-91645, 2025 WL 894652, at *3 (D. Mass. Mar. 24, 2025) (granting

permissive intervention to protect “privacy interest”).

In sum, because Rule 24 is read “broadly in favor of intervention,” W.

Watersheds Project, 22 F.4th at 835, and because Proposed Intervenors’

participation will assist rather than prejudice efficient development and resolution

of this matter, the Court should grant permissive intervention in the event it does

not grant intervention as of right.

Proposed Intervenors respectfully request that this Court grant intervention
as of right, or, in the alternative, grant permissive intervention.
