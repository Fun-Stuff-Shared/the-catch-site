INTRODUCTION
Appellant seeks an injunction from this Court before the District Court has
even adjudicated its request for a temporary restraining order (“TRO”), even
though argument on that request is set for October 27. The sought-after TRO is the
Appellant’s demand that certain mail ballots be set aside, because Appellant
suggests without substantiation that they may have been returned by non-citizens.
The purported basis for the present “emergency” is that California law (since 2021)
allows the processing of mail ballots upon receipt. Cal. Elec. Code §15101.
Setting aside that Appellant’s attempt to interfere in California’s midterm elections
is entirely meritless (for reasons addressed in the County’s contemporaneouslyfiled opposition), the “emergency” Appellant seeks to use to leapfrog the District
Court is a situation entirely of Appellant’s own making.
Appellant created its own purported deadline of noon, October 22, 2026, for
the District Court to rule on its “Ex Parte Application for Temporary Restraining
Order and Order to Show Cause Why a Preliminary Injunction Should Not Issue,”
Dkt.62, a request that was filed days after the complaint. Even if the absence of a
ruling from the District Court by this self-created deadline constituted a
constructive denial of Appellant’s “Ex Parte Application,” this Court would lack
jurisdiction to hear this interlocutory appeal because Appellant requested only a
TRO and a hearing, and denial of neither one is appealable. Regardless, the record

-1-

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 34.1,
36.1, (9 of 21)

shows that the District Court was moving expeditiously to a ruling on Appellant’s
request for injunctive relief when Appellant filed the notice of appeal. The fact
that the District Court had not yet ruled does not have the practical effect of
refusing injunctive relief, so there has been no constructive denial. Accordingly,
the Court should immediately dismiss the appeal.
STATEMENT
The one-count complaint here was filed on October 15—just 19 days before
Election Day. Under California law (in place for several election cycles, creating
no surprise for Appellant), counties “may begin to process vote by mail return
envelopes … not … later than 29 days before the election,” Cal. Elec. Code.
§15101(a); such processing is already underway across the state.
After suing, Appellant waited four days to request preliminary relief, which
it did on October 19 by filing an ex parte application for leave to file an overlength
motion for a temporary restraining order and order to show cause on a preliminary
injunction. Dkt.22-1. The memorandum attached to Appellant’s motion
“request[ed] that [the district court] grant … relief by November 3, 2026, before
the end of voting in the 2026 federal election.” Dkt.22-2 at 4. Appellees promptly
opposed Appellant’s pursuit of preliminary relief. Dkts.31, 32. Within hours of
the case’s assignment to Judge Carter on October 21, Judge Carter set a hearing on
Appellant’s request for preliminary relief on October 27. Dkts.53, 54, 55, 64.

-2-

Later on October 21, Appellant demanded even more expedited treatment,
Dkt.67, pointing to the County’s statement that “[b]allots have been mailed,
returned, and processed, and election officials are working toward fixed deadlines
for counting and certification,” Dkt.32-1 at 8. This statement merely
acknowledged that Appellants were processing mail ballots according to
California’s Election Code. See Cal. Elec. Code. §15101. Nevertheless, Appellant
claimed that this statement created an emergency, as “ballot processing practices
render votes anonymous and permanently untraceable once opened,” Dkt.67 at 2,
Appellant demanded a temporary restraining order by noon the following day, id.
When that deadline passed, Appellant noticed this appeal claiming that its TRO
request had been constructively denied. Dkt.74. After noticing this appeal,
Appellant filed an ex parte application for a preliminary injunction pending appeal.
Dkt.76. Appellees opposed the ex parte application. Dkts.79, 96.
ARGUMENT
I.

THE APPEAL SHOULD BE DISMISSED FOR LACK OF APPELLATE
JURISDICTION
“[T]he appellant has the burden of establishing that the appellate court has

jurisdiction to hear the case,” In re Ozenne, 841 F.3d 810, 814 (9th Cir. 2016). But
Appellant’s notice of appeal invokes only the “critical and exigent nature of the
relief sought” and declares that the order on appeal is the constructive denial of its
sought-after TRO purportedly effected by the District Court. Dkt.74 at 1.
-3-

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 34.1,
36.1, (11 of 21)

Appellant’s failure to identify a basis for this Court’s jurisdiction is sufficient
ground to dismiss the appeal.
In any event, there is no basis for appellate jurisdiction. The only relief
Appellant sought by its self-created deadline of October 22 was an ex parte TRO.
Thus, there was not—and could not have been—any “constructive denial” of a
preliminary injunction giving rise to appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C.
§1292(a)(1). Nor was the federal government’s motion constructively denied. The
District Court promptly ordered briefing and set a hearing on the motion for
October 27. Parties may not manufacture constructive denials by imposing
arbitrary ultimatums on district courts, especially in circumstances like these,
where: (1) Appellant’s original motion did not seek a decision until November 3;
and (2) the apparent basis for the claimed emergency is that the County is
processing returned mail ballots, consistent with longstanding California law and
which it has been doing since October 11 (before this case was filed). The appeal
should be dismissed for lack of appellate jurisdiction.
A.

Appellant’s own filings below make clear that there has been no

constructive denial of a preliminary injunction, which is a necessary predicate to
this Court having appellate jurisdiction here. Appellant styled its motion below as
an “Ex Parte Application for Temporary Restraining Order and Order to Show
Cause Why a Preliminary Injunction Should Not Issue.” Dkt.62. Appellant’s

-4-

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 34.1,
36.1, (12 of 21)

proposed order confirmed that it only sought a TRO, proposing that Defendants be
enjoined “PENDING HEARING on the below Order to Show Cause.” Dkt.62-6 at
6. The only other relief Appellant requested was an order to show cause that
would set a briefing-hearing schedule on whether to issue a preliminary injunction.
Id. at 7.
Appellant’s subsequent “Request for Expedited Ruling,” filed late on
October 21, similarly did not seek a preliminary injunction, but only an expedited
ruling “by noon on October 22, 2026” on Appellant’s “application for temporary
restraining order to preserve the status quo.” Dkt.67 at 1–2. On appeal, Appellant
acknowledges this, i.e., that “the United States requested expedited ruling on the
temporary restraining order by noon October 22.” Mot.22 (emphasis added).
Accordingly, the request Appellant now claims was constructively denied was
solely for a TRO that would be “pending hearing” on whether the Court should
issue a preliminary injunction. The Court promptly scheduled a hearing. Dkt.64.
Appellant has not shown that awaiting the scheduled hearing forecloses
effective appellate review. Appellant’s asserted interest in this litigation is
investigation and prosecution of 18 U.S.C. §611(a), which renders it a crime for
noncitizens to vote in federal elections. See, e.g., Dkt.1 ¶¶1, 9-10, 17-20. But
separating mail ballots from their associated envelopes does not prevent federal
investigation or prosecution, because the envelopes continue to reflect who voted.

-5-

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 34.1,
36.1, (13 of 21)

Processing mail ballots only prevents Appellant from determining how specific
Californians voted—but how someone voted is irrelevant to whether they violated
section 611(a). Accord, Dkt.1 ¶20 (“A noncitizen who expresses his preference or
opinion in an election by ballot violates the federal statutory ban on noncitizen
voting, unless the noncitizen had no opportunity to vote for a candidate seeking
federal office.” (cleaned up)).
The narrow scope of Appellant’s requested relief below is fatal to its implicit
assertion that appellate jurisdiction exists. Section 1292(a)(1) permits appeals
from specified interlocutory orders concerning injunctions, but an appeal ordinarily
does not lie from the denial of a TRO. See Wilson v. U.S. Dist. Ct. for N. Dist. of
Cal., 161 F.3d 1185, 1187 (9th Cir. 1998). That rule prevents premature appeals
and “uneconomical piecemeal appellate review.” Religious Tech. Ctr. v. Scott, 869
F.2d 1306, 1308 (9th Cir. 1989) (quoting Kimball v. Commandant Twelfth Naval
Dist., 423 F.2d 88, 89 (9th Cir.1970)). To be sure, a TRO denial may be
appealable if it is “tantamount to a denial of a preliminary injunction.” Id. But
that exception does not apply. Appellant—which, as noted, has the burden to
establish jurisdiction—makes no argument that constructive denial of its request
for a TRO amounted to denial of a preliminary injunction.
This Court’s decision in Wilson is instructive. There, the Court reviewed a
TRO through mandamus rather than an interlocutory appeal—and denied relief.

-6-

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 34.1,
36.1, (14 of 21)

161 F.3d at 1187. Acknowledging that the district court had scheduled a
preliminary-injunction hearing less than three weeks away, this Court emphasized
that the resulting order would therefore be “fully reviewable on appeal and
expedited proceedings may be requested.” Id. The same opportunity remains
available here, on a far shorter schedule: Appellant’s application is set for a
hearing on October 27 with expedited briefing to be completed by October 25.
Dkts. 64, 77. Appellant cannot bypass those proceedings merely because it wants
an immediate answer—after waiting months to file suit and weeks to seek
preliminary relief. See OPM v. AFGE, 473 U.S. 1301, 1305–1306 (1985) (Burger,
C.J., in chambers) (rejecting appellate jurisdiction over TRO denial where district
court expressly contemplated a prompt preliminary-injunction hearing).
Appellant’s apparent failure before it filed its TRO request to understand the ballot
processing procedures set forth California’s Election Code does not allow it to
bypass section 1292’s limitations on this Court’s appellate jurisdiction.
B.

Even if the Court could somehow construe Appellant’s request for a

TRO pending hearing as a request for a preliminary injunction under section 1292,
the District Court did not constructively deny that request. To be sure, an order
having the practical effect of refusing an injunction may be appealable, but only
upon a showing of serious, potentially irreparable consequences and that effective

-7-

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 34.1,
36.1, (15 of 21)

challenge requires an immediate appeal. See Carson v. American Brands, Inc., 450
U.S. 79, 84 (1981). Appellant cannot show that this standard is met.
This action was filed on October 15; and, as noted, it asserts that California
state election law is preempted by a federal criminal statute. Dkt. 1. Appellant’s
initial, October 19 request for preliminary relief sought a determination before the
end of voting on November 3, 2026. Dkt.22-2 at 4; Dkt.62-1 at 4. Responsive to
this request, the district court scheduled a hearing for October 27, a week before
Appellant’s requested decision date. Dkt.64.
But late in the evening on October 21, Appellant filed an “emergency”
request, demanding a ruling by noon the next day—only three business hours later.
Dkt.67 at 2. The purported basis for this deadline was that the County was
processing mail ballots in accordance with California law—a fact that should have
been readily ascertainable to Appellant before this action was filed, as it is set forth
in the state Election Code. Cal. Elec. Code §15101(a).
Were there any doubt that there has been no constructive denial of relief, the
district court’s expedited treatment of this case dispels it. Upon assignment of the
case, Judge Carter immediately scheduled argument on Appellant’s motion, and
the court’s October 23 order now requires additional briefing over the weekend,
ahead of the October 27 hearing. Dkt.77 at 1. Those accelerated proceedings
confirm that the court is diligently working toward a decision. See Fideicomiso de

-8-

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 34.1,
36.1, (16 of 21)

la Tierra del Caño Martín Peña v. Fortuño, 582 F.3d 131, 134–135 (1st Cir. 2009)
(dismissing appeal where further interlocutory relief remained available and
district court was proceeding toward ruling).
This Court should recognize Appellant’s posturing for what it is: an
impermissible attempt to “achieve[] [an appeal] simply by asserting that the trial
court has failed to act promptly as wished by [the] party seeking an injunction,”
Amazon.com Services LLC v. National Labor Relations Board, 151 F.4th 221,
227–228 (5th Cir. 2025) (dismissing interlocutory appeal). Appellant’s lack of
“diligence in seeking to expedite,” including its delay in moving for any
preliminary relief and its inconsistent “requests for a ruling by a specific date”
further weigh against finding that the district court effectively refused relief. Id. at
227.
A.A.R.P. v. Trump does not dispense with the requirement to show that the
District Court’s actions had the “practical effect of refusing an injunction.” 605
U.S. 91, 94 (2025) (quoting Carson v. American Brands, Inc., 450 U.S. 79, 84
(1981)). There, the plaintiff detainees faced imminent removal to foreign custody
under circumstances where Appellant had disclaimed any ability to return
detainees once removed. Id. at 92-93. The Court’s reasoning turned on the
“extreme urgency” and “imminent threat of severe, irreparable harm” in that
situation, which meant that the district court’s actions “had the practical effect of

-9-

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 34.1,
36.1, (17 of 21)

refusing an injunction” to the detainees. Id. 94–97 (2025). Here, Appellant has
not demonstrated anything close to that level of harm, because ballot processing
does not alter its ability to investigate purported violations of section 611(a). See
supra pp.5-6. As explained in the County’s accompanying opposition to
Appellant’s motion for an injunction pending appeal, Appellant’s asserted
irreparable injury is entirely illusory and self-inflicted.
Put simply, Appellant’s (unstated) argument for appellate jurisdiction is that
the district court failed to rule by Appellant’s imposed deadline. But the vehicle
for seeking an order compelling a district court ruling is mandamus. See
Fideicomiso, 582 F.3d at 134–135. Appellant has not requested that extraordinary
relief, undoubtedly because mandamus requires a “clear and indisputable”
entitlement to the writ. Cheney v. United States District Court for District of
Columbia, 542 U.S. 367, 380–381 (2004). Appellant cannot establish such an
entitlement to an overnight decision while the district court is proceeding toward a
prompt hearing on expedited briefing.
CONCLUSION
Appellant’s appeal should be dismissed for lack of appellate jurisdiction.
