                                  22   III.   LEGAL STANDARD
                                  23          In order for the Court to grant a preliminary injunction, Alibaba must establish that: (1) it

                                  24   is likely to succeed on the merits, (2) it faces a likelihood of irreparable harm in the absence of a

                                  25   preliminary injunction, (3) the balance of equities weighs in its favor, and (4) the injunction is in

                                  26   the public interest. Winter v. Nat. Res. Def. Council, Inc., 555 U.S. 7, 20 (2008); All. for the Wild

                                  27   Rockies v. Cottrell, 632 F.3d 1127, 1131 (9th Cir. 2011). In the Ninth Circuit, “if a plaintiff can

                                  28   only show that there are serious questions going to the merits – a lesser showing than likelihood of
                                                                                          7

                                                                          <pageID>



                                   1   success on the merits – then a preliminary injunction may still issue if the balance of hardships tips

                                   2   sharply in the plaintiff’s favor, and the other two Winter factors are satisfied.” Friends of the Wild

                                   3   Swan v. Weber, 767 F.3d 936, 942 (9th Cir. 2014) (citation modified). “Where, as here, the party

                                   4   opposing the injunctive relief is a government entity, the third and fourth factors – the balance of

                                   5   equities and the public interest – merge.” Hubbard v. City of San Diego, 139 F.4th 843, 854 (9th

                                   6   Cir. 2025) (citation modified).

                                   7          The plaintiff’s likelihood of success on the merits “is the most important (and usually

                                   8   decisive) [Winter factor] in cases where a plaintiff brings a constitutional claim[.]” Baird v.

                                   9   Bonta, 81 F.4th 1036, 1041 (9th Cir. 2023). “If a plaintiff bringing [a claim based on a

                                  10   constitutional violation] shows he is likely to prevail on the merits, that showing will almost

                                  11   always demonstrate he is suffering irreparable harm as well.” Id. at 1042. “A plaintiff’s

                                  12   likelihood of success on the merits of a constitutional claim also tips the merged third and fourth



 United States District Court
                                  13   factors decisively in his favor.” Id.

                                   3                           a.      Colorable First Amendment Claim
                                   4           To raise a colorable claim, Alibaba has the burden to demonstrate Section 4663 implicates

                                   5   the First Amendment. First Amendment scrutiny is triggered when the government seeks to

                                   6   “proscrib[e] speech . . . or expressive conduct[.]”8 R. A. V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, 382

                                   7   (1992); see also TikTok, 604 U.S. at 68 (citation omitted). Alternatively, First Amendment

                                   8   scrutiny may be appropriate for a regulation of “activity with no expressive component” that

                                   9   “impose[s] a disproportionate burden upon those engaged in protected First Amendment

                                  10   activities.” TikTok, 604 U.S. at 68. On the other hand, a law is not subject to First Amendment

                                  11   scrutiny if it is a “general[ly] applicabl[e]” regulation that incidentally causes a First Amendment

                                  12   burden. Arcara v. Cloud Books, Inc., 478 U.S. 697, 705 (1986).



 United States District Court
                                  13           To establish that its First Amendment rights had been infringed, a plaintiff does not need to

                                  14   establish that it has been completely silenced. “The distinction between laws burdening and laws

                                  15   banning speech is but a matter of degree.” United States v. Playboy Ent. Grp., Inc., 529 U.S. 803,



Northern District of California
                                  16   812 (2000). “The First Amendment protects [the] right not only to advocate [a petitioner’s] cause

                                  17   but also to select what they believe to be the most effective means for so doing.” Meyer v. Grant,

                                  18   486 U.S. 414, 425 (1988) (emphasis added).

                                  19           The Court finds that First Amendment scrutiny applies because Section 4663 regulates

                                  20   speech. By restricting Alibaba’s ability to retain lobbyists, Section 4663 functionally restricts its

                                  21   political speech. “Discussion of public issues” is “pure speech” that is at “the core of the

                                  22   protection afforded by the First Amendment[.]” McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm’n, 514

                                  23   U.S. 334, 345-46 (1995); see also Meyer, 486 U.S. at 422 (“[C]ommunication concerning political

                                  24   change [] is appropriately described as ‘core political speech.’”). The First Amendment also

                                  25   protects “[t]he right to petition,” that is, “the right of the people to inform their representatives in

                                   7          Alibaba also urges that the Court find Section 4663 facially overbroad. The Court finds

                                   8   that Alibaba has not established a likelihood of success as to its facial challenge, because it has not

                                   9   made the requisite showing under Moody v. NetChoice, 603 U.S. 707 (2025). See NetChoice, LLC

                                  10   v. Bonta, 170 F.4th 744, 758 (9th Cir. 2026).

                                  11          The Supreme Court has cautioned that “facial challenges threaten to short circuit the

                                  12   democratic process,” and that it “has therefore made [them] hard to win.” Moody, 603 U.S. at 723



 United States District Court
                                  13   (citation omitted). In First Amendment cases, the question is whether a substantial number of “the

                                  14   law’s unconstitutional applications substantially outweigh its constitutional ones.” Id. (noting that

                                  15   this is a less demanding, “though still rigorous[,]” standard in the First Amendment context to



Northern District of California
                                  16   provide “breathing room for free expression[]”) (citation omitted). A facial challenge requires

                                  17   considering: (1) the scope and reach of the law, that is, “[w]hat activities, by what actors, do the

                                  18   laws prohibit or otherwise regulate”; and (2) “which of the laws’ applications violate the First

                                  19   Amendment, and [measuring] them against the rest.” Id. at 724, 745. “A facial challenge thus

                                  20   demands record development on third-party speech and the full scope of a law’s potential

                                  21   applications.” NetChoice, 170 F.4th at 758 (citation modified).

                                  22          At this preliminary stage, the Court finds that Alibaba’s facial challenge fails because there

                                  23   is insufficient information about the statute’s scope. Because this is an overbreadth claim, the full

                                  24   scope of applications must be considered. Otherwise, there is the risk of “premature interpretation

                                  25   of statutes on the basis of factually barebones records.” Wash. State Grange v. Wash. State

                                  26   Republican Party, 552 U.S. 442, 450 (2008) (citation omitted). Alibaba urges that this statute

                                  27   must be applied in the same way to every CMC. Mot. at 18. However, Alibaba may not simply

                                  28   rely on “applications of the statute to its own speech.” Project Veritas v. Schmidt, 125 F.4th 929,
                                                                                         21

                                                                        #: <pageID>



                                   1   961 (9th Cir.), cert. denied sub nom. Project Veritas v. Vasquez, 146 S. Ct. 90 (2025). It is

                                   2   conceivable that every CMC may be subject to the same restriction in speech, but the record has

                                   3   not been developed to show whether that burden would be constitutionally permissible as applied

                                   4   to other CMCs. The Court recognizes that Alibaba’s ability to obtain information regarding other

                                   5   applications may have been limited at the preliminary injunction stage.15 However, Alibaba

                                   6   carries the burden at both steps outlined in Moody. The failure to do so treats a facial challenge

                                   7   more like as-applied claims. Moody, 603 U.S. at 724. Because Alibaba did not meet its burden,

                                   8   the Court rejects Alibaba’s facial challenge at this time. Project Veritas, 125 F.4th at 961; see

                                   9   also Prichett v. Bonta, 818 F. Supp. 3d 1059, 1078-79 (N.D. Cal. 2025) (declining to consider

                                  10   First Amendment facial challenge to statute for lack of factual information).

                                  11          B.      Irreparable Harm
                                  12          To be irreparable, harm must be “imminent and not remote or speculative.” Boardman v.



 United States District Court
                                  13   Pac. Seafood Grp., 822 F.3d 1011, 1022 (9th Cir. 2016). Typically, irreparable harms are not

                                  14   quantifiable and, thus, “cannot be fully remedied with a financial award.” Novedades Y Servicios,

                                  15   Inc. v. Fin. Crimes Enf. Network, 181 F.4th 961, 977 (9th Cir. 2026). By contrast, “[t]he



Northern District of California
                                  16   possibility that adequate compensatory or other corrective relief will be available at a later date, in

                                  17   the ordinary course of litigation, weighs heavily against a claim of irreparable harm.” Sampson v.

                                  18   Murray, 415 U.S. 61, 90 (1974).

                                  19          It is “relatively easy” to establish irreparable harm when First Amendment rights are

                                  20   violated: “The plaintiff ‘need only demonstrate the existence of a colorable First Amendment

                                  21   claim.’” Council for Educ. & Rsch. on Toxics, 29 F.4th at 482 (quoting Brown v. Cal. Dep’t of

                                  22   Transp., 321 F.3d 1217, 1225 (9th Cir. 2003)); see also Roman Catholic Diocese of Brooklyn v.

                                  23   Cuomo, 592 U.S. 14, 19 (2020) (“The loss of First Amendment freedoms, for even minimal

                                  24   periods of time, unquestionably constitutes irreparable injury.” (quoting Elrod v. Burns, 427

                                  25   U.S. 347, 373 (1976) (plurality opinion))).

                                   6   V.     SCOPE OF RELIEF
                                   7          The Court has broad powers in shaping injunctive relief. It is well established that “the

                                   8   scope of a preliminary injunction must be no broader than necessary to provide complete relief to

                                   9   the named plaintiffs.” Thakur v. Trump, 176 F.4th 1187, 1206 (9th Cir. 2026) (citation modified).

                                  10   While the Court may award complete relief, “[c]omplete relief is not a guarantee – it is the

                                  11   maximum a court can provide.’” Trump v. CASA, Inc., 606 U.S. 831, 854-54 (2025)).

                                  12          In its preliminary injunction motion, Alibaba requested that the Court order any of the



 United States District Court
                                  13   following: (1) require Defendants to remove Alibaba from the 1260H List, (2) enjoin Defendants

                                  14   from enforcing Section 4663 against any entity, or (3) enjoin Defendants from enforcing Section

                                  15   4663 against entities that contract with Alibaba’s lobbyists. Mot. at 22. However, Alibaba only



Northern District of California
                                  16   moved for interim relief based on its First Amendment claim. Removal from the 1260H List is

                                  17   relief that is not addressed through Alibaba’s First Amendment claim, but rather its APA claims.

                                  18   Because of the mismatch between Alibaba’s asserted claim and its requested remedy, the Court

                                  19   must deny Alibaba’s request that the Court vacate its 1260H designation. Garcia v. Google, Inc.,

                                  20   786 F.3d 733, 745 (9th Cir. 2015).

                                  24   Alibaba’s APA claims and would disturb the Department’s foreign policy and national security

                                  28   ECF No. 39-1. In both cases, the D.C. Circuit and district court considered the plaintiffs’ APA
