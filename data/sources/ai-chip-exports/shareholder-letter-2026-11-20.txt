Re: Who decides whether Jensen Huang returns
Dear Mr. Jones and Members of the Board:
I am an NVIDIA shareholder and have been for nearly 15
years. I write to ask the board to place one decision—
whether Jensen Huang returns, and on what terms—in the
hands of new directors elected by shareholders at a special
meeting. This request takes no position on whether Mr.
Huang should return. It is about who decides. It should not
be you.
Directors should not decide a question when one answer
stands to benefit them personally more than the other.
NVIDIA’s Code of Conduct, which binds its directors,
requires anyone with an actual or potential conflict to
remove themselves from the decision. That rule governs
here. Each of you is a defendant in Vanegas v. Huang, a
pending shareholder derivative lawsuit that, among other
things, faults the board for deferring too much to Mr.
Huang. Keeping him out strengthens your personal defense
in that lawsuit; reinstating him weakens it. And the question
before you is not just any question. It is likely the most
material decision made in NVIDIA’s history.

The November 11 lawsuit gives each of you a
personal stake in keeping Mr. Huang out
Whatever you decide about Mr. Huang becomes evidence in
the case against you. On November 8, the board announced
an independent investigation, said it had “no reason to
believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were
compromised,” and kept Mr. Huang in charge, expressing
confidence in him. On November 10, the independent
directors retained Wachtell Lipton to conduct the
investigation. On November 11, the Vanegas complaint was
filed. One of its charges is that the board “left Mr. Huang in
command” (¶ 81) while it investigated him. The same day,
Mr. Huang was out.
A Reuters article said the quiet part out loud: if the board
forces him out, that “could provide legal protection for the
board in the derivative lawsuit by showing directors took
action.” Keeping Mr. Huang out preserves that protection.
Bringing him back would hand the plaintiff the opposite
argument.
Neither indemnification nor insurance moots the conflict.
The complaint asks the court to declare that each of you
breached your duties—a finding neither can erase. Because
the suit is brought on NVIDIA’s behalf, Delaware law bars
the company from indemnifying you for any judgment or
settlement. The complaint also seeks disgorgement of stock-
sale profits from six of you—a majority of the board, Mr.
Jones among them. No charter can excuse that claim under
Delaware law, and insurance typically does not cover it.
Avoiding that exposure, protecting your reputations, and
strengthening your defenses are personal benefits distinct
from NVIDIA’s interests. How you decide the question of
Mr. Huang’s return bears on those personal benefits.

Delaware law treats this kind of stake as an interest in the
decision: in Rales v. Blasband, for example, the Delaware
Supreme Court recognized a directorial interest “where a
corporate decision will have a materially detrimental impact
on a director, but not on the corporation and the
stockholders.” In Revlon, Inc. v. MacAndrews & Forbes
Holdings, Inc., the Delaware Supreme Court upheld an
injunction against a lock-up that secured too little for
shareholders and whose “by-product” was to protect the
directors against “a perceived threat of personal liability.”
Here, the threat you face is not merely perceived. It is a
filed complaint bearing each of your names. In In re Fitbit,
Inc. Stockholder Derivative Litigation, the Court of
Chancery concluded that a director could not impartially
decide an action because the positions that decision would
cause the company to take “would likely compromise his
defenses” in a pending suit. The same concern arises here:
the stance you cause NVIDIA to take on Mr. Huang’s future
bears on your defenses in Vanegas.
Mr. Huang’s return under appropriate safeguards may be
the best course for NVIDIA and the worst for your defense.
In deciding whether to reinstate Mr. Huang, each of you
faces two questions: (1) What is best for NVIDIA? (2) What
is safest for my own defense?
NVIDIA shareholders deserve a decision from directors who
face only the first.

Lesser measures could achieve accountability,
redress, and protection, but Mr. Huang’s exile
stands to benefit you personally more
The board’s own announcement calls Mr. Huang’s
contributions “immeasurable.” And the market agrees.
NVIDIA has lost more than a trillion dollars in market value
since November 8, most of it after Mr. Huang’s departure
was announced. Dan Ives, the Yorkville Ives analyst, put the
difference at a quarter of the company: $300 a share with
Mr. Huang as CEO, $225 without him.
The reported facts behind the board’s concerns are serious.
But NVIDIA’s own Code provides ways to take them
seriously short of exile: enhanced controls and changed
protocols. Lesser measures can achieve accountability,
redress, and protection against recurrence. The board has
already adopted one such measure: on November 17, it
formed a standing National Security and Export Control
Oversight Committee, which can oversee Mr. Huang as
readily as it can oversee his successor. Similar measures
can be crafted to address other issues. The board could also
narrow Mr. Huang’s role to address its concerns while
preserving his contributions. Each measure keeps exile in
reserve: the board could remove him the day one failed.
Whether exile or a more measured response better serves
NVIDIA’s shareholders is not a judgment that can be made
reflexively. And it should be made by directors with no stake
in the answer. This board has made it anyway, with no sign
of having adequately considered the range of possibilities
between doing nothing and exiling him: it has ruled out Mr.
Huang’s return as CEO, and NVIDIA has said he will not
work with it in any capacity.
If lesser measures suffice, exile buys NVIDIA nothing and
costs its shareholders everything Mr. Huang would

contribute. Finding out costs little: an interim CEO is in

Even if the lawsuit has not influenced you,
NVIDIA’s Code requires you to step aside
Shareholders do not have to show that the lawsuit has in
fact influenced any of you—though it would be only human
if it had. It is enough that, while the lawsuit is pending, one
answer to the question of Mr. Huang’s fate stands to benefit
you personally more than the other. Your Code covers any
conflict, actual or potential, between personal gain and
obligations to NVIDIA, and it directs that when such a
conflict arises, “we immediately remove ourselves from any
decision-making role in the matter.” The Code does not wait
for a court to find one. The Financial Team Code of
Conduct, which also binds the board, adds that where there
is “even the potential for conflict or ambiguity” about what
is permitted, the conduct must be avoided.
Mr. Jones said on November 17 that Mr. Huang had
resigned after “personal behavior inconsistent with the
company’s code of conduct” came to light.
The board must now apply to itself the same Code it applied
to Mr. Huang.

Only new directors chosen by shareholders, not by
the defendants, can settle the question of Mr.
Huang’s return
You may be right in your decision to deny reinstating Mr.
Huang. You may be wrong. What you cannot be is impartial.
This decision is important enough that shareholders deserve
the benefit of a process that cannot be impeached.
Handing the decision to new directors accomplishes this. It
asks only what your Code already requires: that you give up
control over one decision in which you have a personal
stake. That is not much to ask, given how much is at stake.
Delaware law provides a template. In Zapata Corp. v.
Maldonado, a board whose directors faced a derivative suit
over their stock options seated new directors and made
their determination final, and the Delaware Supreme Court
upheld their power to act for the company. If a dispute over
stock options warranted that step, deciding the return of
the founder who led NVIDIA for 33 years surely does.
A committee drawn from the sitting board cannot do that
work: every sitting director is a defendant, and every one
sat on the board that kept Mr. Huang in charge on
November 8 and ruled out his return on November 17. In In
re Oracle Corp. Derivative Litigation, the Court of Chancery
refused to defer to a committee of two directors the board
had chosen for their independence, because their ties to the
defendants left that independence in doubt. Having
shareholders, rather than the defendant directors, choose
the new directors removes that doubt from the start.

The board should hire no permanent CEO until
directors elected by shareholders decide Mr.
Huang’s return
I ask the board to take three steps. First, extend no offer for
permanent CEO, and take no other step that would
foreclose Mr. Huang’s return, until the new directors
decide. Second, add three seats to the board, resolve that
shareholders will fill them, and call a special meeting now,
nominating no candidates and making no recommendation.
Third, give the three new directors sole and final authority
to decide whether Mr. Huang returns, in what role, and
under what safeguards. The appendix sets out suggested
mechanics for these steps and for two supporting ones:
withdrawing the statements that ruled out Mr. Huang’s
return, and moving oversight of the investigation away from
directors whose defense depends on its findings.
The first step must take effect immediately. Once the board
hires a permanent CEO, the question of Mr. Huang’s return
is preempted, and answered by the directors who should
not answer it. A pause costs little: the board has already
said repeatedly that it has full confidence in interim CEO
Colette Kress, and it expects NVIDIA’s operations “to
continue without interruption.”
Because this question belongs to all of NVIDIA’s owners, I
am making this letter public. I would welcome a
conversation with Mr. Jones or any other director, and I ask
for a response within ten days.
If the board has waived the Code’s conflict-of-interest
provision for its own members, I ask it to disclose the
waiver and the reasons for it, as the Code provides and as
Nasdaq’s rules require within four business days. Under
those rules, only the board itself or one of its committees

Appendix: The five steps and suggested
mechanics
Step 1. Preserve the committee’s options
 • Until the committee described in Step 3 decides, or releases the
   board from this step, extend no offer for permanent CEO, enter
   no agreement that would foreclose or materially constrain its
   consideration of Mr. Huang’s return, and settle no derivative
   claim on terms that bear on his return.
Step 2. Seat three independent, shareholder-elected
directors
 • Add three seats to the board and, as NVIDIA’s Charter and
   Section 19 of its Bylaws permit, resolve that shareholders will
   fill them.
 • Call a special meeting now so that the shareholders may elect
   those directors.
 • Open nominations for 15 days after the meeting is called to any
   shareholder or group of shareholders that has held at least $1
   billion of NVIDIA stock for at least one year, each of which may
   nominate up to three candidates. Amend the deadline in Section
   6(f) of the Bylaws as needed, and include every properly
   nominated candidate in the company’s proxy statement and on a
   universal proxy card. If more than nine candidates are
   nominated, include the nine whose nominators hold the most
   shares.
 • Nominate no candidates and make no recommendation.
 • Ask the SEC staff to shorten the ten-day preliminary filing
   period, if necessary.
 • Hold the meeting on the earliest date the proxy rules then allow,
   and no later than 60 days after it is called.
 • The candidates should be independent of Mr. Huang and the
   incumbent directors, have no personal interest in the defense of
   the pending claims, and have made no commitment concerning
   the outcome.

Step 3. Empower the new directors to decide and implement
their decision
Constitute the new directors as a special committee with sole
authority, to the extent permitted by law, to decide whether Mr.
Huang returns, in what role, and under what safeguards, and to
implement that decision without further approval by the full board.

 • Resolve, as the Zapata board did, that the committee’s decision
   is final and not subject to review by the full board.
 • A committee cannot fill a board seat under Article V of the
   Charter; only the full board or the shareholders can. Resolve
   now that if the committee decides Mr. Huang should sit on the
   board, the shareholders, not the sitting directors, will vote on
   his seat.
 • Commit publicly not to revoke or narrow the committee’s
   authority or alter its membership to influence its decision, not to
   reverse that decision once it is made, and to keep both
   commitments through the 2027 annual meeting.
 • Provide counsel and advisers of the committee’s own choosing,
   at NVIDIA’s expense, together with full access to the
   investigation’s findings and supporting materials.
 • Ask the committee to decide within 30 days after the later of its
   seating and its receipt of the investigation’s final report.
Step 4. Reset the public record
State publicly that the November 17 statements and the
spokesperson’s November 12 statement ruling out any role for Mr.
Huang no longer represent the company’s position, that his return
remains an open question, and that the committee will decide it.

 • Make the statement by press release and on Form 8-K.
Step 5. Protect the independence of the investigation
In the interim, remove from oversight of the investigation any
director whose stock sales the complaint challenges, keep any
officer named in the complaint from directing it or receiving its
reports, and transfer Wachtell Lipton’s reporting line to the new
committee once it is seated.
