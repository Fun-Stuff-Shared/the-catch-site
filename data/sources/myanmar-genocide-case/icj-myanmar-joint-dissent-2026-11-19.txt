I. INTRODUCTION
1. We have no doubt that Myanmar’s security forces committed serious human rights
violations against the Rohingya during the clearance operations in 2016 and 2017 in northern
Rakhine State. If, and to the extent that, the hostilities between the armed forces of Myanmar (the
“Tatmadaw”) as well as other security forces of Myanmar and the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army
(“ARSA”) constituted an armed conflict at the relevant time, we likewise have no doubt that members
of Myanmar’s security forces committed serious violations of the law of armed conflict at several
locations, which may amount to war crimes. Furthermore, it is also possible that the atrocities
perpetrated against Rohingya in northern Rakhine State formed part of a widespread and/or
systematic attack directed against a civilian population and accordingly that crimes against humanity
were committed. The Court, however, has no jurisdiction to make findings on any of those questions,
since its jurisdiction in the present case is based exclusively on Article IX of the Convention on the
Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (the “Convention”).
2. The central question before the Court is whether the conduct of the security forces of
Myanmar during the 2016 and 2017 clearance operations in northern Rakhine State amounted to
genocide. The majority answers that question in the affirmative. We respectfully disagree. The main
reason for our disagreement is that the evidence before the Court does not establish, under the

-2applicable stringent standard of proof, which requires “fully conclusive evidence” 1, that Myanmar
acted with genocidal intent at the relevant time. As the Judgment correctly states, a finding that
genocide has been committed requires the Court to be fully convinced of the existence of each
constituent element of that crime, including genocidal intent. Unlike the majority, we are not at all
convinced, and even less “fully” convinced, that this is the case here. In this opinion, we explain
why.
3. Before turning to our legal analysis, we wish to acknowledge the terrible suffering endured
by those who were subjected to violence, displacement, destruction of homes and other grave abuses.
Nothing in our opinion should be taken as diminishing the reality of that suffering or the dignity of
those who were subjected to it. But the Court’s task is necessarily confined to the application of the
relevant  in this context, stringent  legal standards to the evidence before it.
4. According to Article II of the Convention, genocidal intent consists of the intent to destroy,
in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such. We share the majority’s
view that, prior to the 2016 and 2017 clearance operations, the Rohingya in Myanmar constituted an
ethnical group within the meaning of the Convention. We also agree that the relevant question in the
present case is not whether Myanmar intended to destroy the Rohingya population in its entirety, but
whether it intended to destroy a “part” of that protected group. Like the majority, we hold that the
relevant part must be “substantial” and that, in the present case, the relevant substantial part is the
approximately 815,000 Rohingya who lived in northern Rakhine State prior to the 2016 and 2017
clearance operations.
5. Our starting-point is therefore the same as that of the majority. To conclude that Myanmar
acted with genocidal intent during its 2016 and 2017 clearance operations, it must be established that
it intended to destroy the group of approximately 815,000 Rohingya who lived in northern Rakhine
State prior to the clearance operations.
II. THE LEGAL STANDARD FOR ESTABLISHING GENOCIDAL INTENT
6. In Bosnia, the Court emphasized the distinction between physical destruction of the
protected group and mere dissolution of the group, and determined that the word “destroy” does not
cover the latter 2. In Croatia, the Court confirmed its determination in Bosnia and found that, for the
purposes of genocidal intent, the term “destroy” in Article II of the Convention means physical or

-3biological destruction 3. International criminal tribunals have consistently adopted the same
interpretation 4, and neither Party has invited the Court to depart from it.
7. Physical destruction of a group, in whole or in part, can occur through the killing of the
members of at least a substantial part of the group. Biological destruction can be brought about by
eliminating the reproductive capacity of at least a substantial part of the group so that its biological
survival is no longer ensured. Preventing a group, in whole or in part, from preserving its language
and culture and other elements which together constitute the group’s identity is not a form of physical
and biological destruction. The same is true for the destruction of a geographical unity or political
cohesion that a protected group may possess. In that respect, we recall the Court’s jurisprudence that
forcible displacement of a group cannot, by itself, amount to the physical or biological destruction
of that group 5.
8. Our analysis of genocidal intent proceeds from the Court’s settled interpretation of the term
“destroy” as denoting the physical or biological destruction of a protected group. While the Judgment
refers to that interpretation without expressly departing from it, certain formulations give rise to
concern. In particular, the reference to “the dispersal of at least a part of the group” (Judgment,
para. 290) may be understood as suggesting that dispersal, in and of itself, can amount to the
destruction of the group, in whole or in part, for the purposes of the crime of genocide.
9. Moreover, as explained further below (paragraphs 22-25), apart from introducing
uncertainty as to its faithfulness to the established interpretation of the term “destroy”, the Judgment,
by attaching considerable evidentiary significance to discrimination, expulsion, and the destruction
of the “foundations” of a group’s social life in inferring genocidal intent, effectively broadens the
concept of destruction through the backdoor. In practical terms, this substantially weakens the
distinction between the destruction of a group and its forcible removal, as affirmed in Bosnia and
reaffirmed in Croatia (see further paragraphs 83-88 below). The result is a blurring of the boundary
between genocide and other crimes under international law.

-411. With respect to the establishment of genocidal intent by circumstantial evidence, we recall
the Court’s clear statement in its 2007 Judgment in Bosnia:
“The dolus specialis, the specific intent to destroy the group in whole or in part,
has to be convincingly shown by reference to particular circumstances, unless a general
plan to that end can be convincingly demonstrated to exist; and for a pattern of conduct
to be accepted as evidence of its existence, it would have to be such that it could only
point to the existence of such intent.” 6
12. We share the view expressed in the Judgment (para. 385) that the term “‘particular
circumstances’ . . ., as used in paragraph 373 of the 2007 Bosnia Judgment, cannot be understood as
a ground for inferring genocidal intent based on conduct carried out at only a small number of specific
locations”.
13. In Croatia, the Court further elaborated upon paragraph 373 of the Bosnia Judgment:
“The Court recalls that, in the passage in question in its 2007 Judgment, it
accepted the possibility of genocidal intent being established indirectly by inference.
The notion of ‘reasonableness’ must necessarily be regarded as implicit in the reasoning
of the Court. Thus, to state that, ‘for a pattern of conduct to be accepted as evidence
of . . . existence [of genocidal intent], it [must] be such that it could only point to the
existence of such intent’ amounts to saying that, in order to infer the existence of
dolus specialis from a pattern of conduct, it is necessary and sufficient that this is the
only inference that could reasonably be drawn from the acts in question.” 7
14. The Gambia has submitted that this test involves, first, an assessment of whether genocidal
intent constitutes a reasonable inference from the relevant pattern of conduct and, secondly, whether
that inference is the only reasonable one. The Judgment proceeds on the same basis. This, however,
is not what the Court said in Croatia. Instead, in that Judgment, in order to indirectly find genocidal
intent, the Court first identified a relevant pattern of conduct attributable to the State concerned.
Then, it asked whether the existence of genocidal intent was the only inference that can reasonably
be drawn from that pattern of conduct. We do not see a compelling reason to depart from this
jurisprudence. On the contrary, the distinction between the findings of genocidal intent being a
reasonable inference and being the only reasonable inference is not only artificial but also tends to
implicitly shift the burden on the respondent to show that the existence of genocidal intent was not
the only reasonable inference to be drawn, because at least one other inference is reasonable.
Unfortunately, the reasoning in the Judgment reveals how easily this possibility can materialize.

-5III. THE RELEVANT CIRCUMSTANCES, IN PARTICULAR
THE RELEVANT PATTERN OF CONDUCT

15. In the absence of direct evidence, The Gambia mainly relied on the inference it invites the
Court to draw from an alleged pattern of conduct. We therefore begin our analysis by asking whether
a relevant pattern of conduct of the security forces of Myanmar can be identified and, if so, how to
properly define this pattern. The Gambia has submitted that, in the present case, the relevant pattern
of conduct is to be identified by reference to seven indicators set out by the Fact-Finding Mission
(the “FFM”) in its 2019 Report, which states as follows:
“[F]irst, the Tatmadaw’s extreme brutality during its attacks on the Rohingya;
second, the organized nature of the Tatmadaw’s destruction; third, the enormity and
nature of sexual violence perpetrated against women and girls during the ‘clearance
operation’; fourth, the insulting, derogatory, racist and exclusionary utterances of
Myanmar officials and others prior, during and after the ‘clearance operations’; fifth,
the existence of discriminatory plans and policies . . .; sixth, the Government’s tolerance
for public rhetoric of hatred and contempt for the Rohingya; and seventh, the State’s
failure to investigate and prosecute gross violations of international human rights law
and serious violations of international humanitarian law, both as they were occurring
and after they occurred.” 8
16. Myanmar, for its part, has submitted that the approach advanced by The Gambia departs
from the methodology adopted by the Court in Bosnia and Croatia.
17. In this respect, the present Judgment states (para. 397):
“[T]he Court will base its assessment of the existence of genocidal intent in the
present case on the following factors: (a) the general context, including the perpetration
of other culpable acts systematically directed against the Rohingya; (b) the selective
targeting of the Rohingya; (c) the perpetration of acts that violate the foundations of the
Rohingya group; and (d) the systematic nature and the scale of Myanmar’s actions.”
18. In our view, this element of the Judgment, while containing a reference to the Court’s
Judgment in Croatia, does not faithfully reflect that jurisprudence.
19. In Croatia, the Court first summarized Croatia’s argument as follows:
“Croatia thus sets out a series of 17 factors which it believes, individually or taken
together, could lead the Court to conclude that there was a systematic policy of targeting
Croats with a view to their elimination from the regions concerned: (1) the political
doctrine of Serbian expansionism which created the climate for genocidal policies

attacks on groups of Croats far exceeded any legitimate military objective necessary to

20. The Court then observed:
“[O]f the 17 factors suggested by Croatia to establish the existence of a pattern
of conduct revealing a genocidal intent, the most important are those that concern the
scale and allegedly systematic nature of the attacks, the fact that those attacks are said
to have caused casualties and damage far in excess of what was justified by military
necessity, the specific targeting of Croats and the nature, extent and degree of the
injuries caused to the Croat population (i.e. the third, seventh, eighth, tenth and eleventh
factors identified in paragraph 408 above).” 10
21. The significance of this passage lies not merely in the factors that the Court chose to
emphasize, but also in those it did not take up. Confronted with a list of seventeen proposed
indicators, the Court focused the inquiry to five “most important” factors. In so doing, the Court
made it clear that the remaining indicators were not considered to possess sufficient probative weight
to support, either individually or cumulatively, an inference of genocidal intent.
22. The Judgment departs from the Court’s jurisprudence in Croatia in two significant
respects. First, the factor “acts that violate the foundations of the Rohingya group” (Judgment,

of genocidal intent to be drawn from grave misconduct without a sufficiently rigorous explanation
of how such misconduct evidences an intent to physically or biologically destroy a group, in whole
or in part. Second, while the general context may assist in interpreting the significance of proven
acts, it cannot dispense with the need to demonstrate, with precision, how those acts support an
inference of genocidal intent. Yet, the Judgment fails to demonstrate this with the required precision.
We discern no compelling reason for this departure from established jurisprudence. Indeed, the
Court’s new approach leads to a holistic assessment that is, in reality, so indeterminate that it borders
on arbitrariness.
23. As regards the factors considered relevant in the Judgment, we do not believe that
Myanmar’s discriminatory practices and policies towards the Rohingya, the derogatory language
employed by members of the Tatmadaw, and the toleration of derogatory public speech are capable
of pointing in any meaningful way to the existence of genocidal intent on the part of Myanmar at the
relevant time. Historical experience demonstrates that such practices and policies may pave the way
for the eventual formation of genocidal intent; we therefore do not deny that they may constitute
indicators of a risk that such an intent might eventually crystallize. That, however, is quite different
from what is to be established here, namely that genocidal intent was indeed formed. Unfortunately,
discrimination against minorities and derogatory speech directed at them or tolerated by State
authorities are not uncommon phenomena. Fortunately, however, in most cases, such conduct does
not culminate in the formation of genocidal intent.
24. We agree with the majority that the restrictions on the Rohingya’s access to food in
northern Rakhine State, as alleged by The Gambia, should not be regarded as forming part of the
relevant pattern of conduct. Myanmar has responded to The Gambia’s allegation by contending that
The Gambia has failed to establish any deliberate restrictions on food which were systematic or of a
general nature. We note that there is no witness testimony before the Court which would deal in any
detail with that particular allegation. The most specific information which forms part of the evidence
before the Court is contained in a 2018 Report by Amnesty International 11. However, without taking
a position on the evidentiary value of this report, we note that the information contained therein does
not describe in any detail the geographic scope of the restrictions in question or whether their effect
was broadly the same throughout northern Rakhine State. Furthermore, we note that witnesses
reported to Amnesty International that the restrictions caused many Rohingya to flee, which confirms
that flight remained an available (and, as will be seen, perhaps even intended) route to safety. Given
the past experiences with Rohingya departing for Bangladesh, Myanmar could assume that those
escaping would again find a safe place there. Thus, even if the accounts contained in the Amnesty
International Report are taken at their highest, they do not support an inference that Myanmar
imposed the restrictions with the aim of starving the Rohingya population in northern Rakhine State
to death or of thereby contributing to a pattern of conduct reflecting an intent to destroy that
community.
25. We also concur with the majority not to include measures aimed at regulating the
maximum number of births within the Rohingya families in northern Rakhine State into the relevant

-8pattern of conduct. It is undisputed that between 2005 and 2018 the local government in Maungdaw
Township enacted an order to limit the number of children per family, without, however, stating a
specific limit. It is also undisputed that the local authorities, when approving marriages, required
couples to agree to have no more than two children. We do not doubt the possibility that, together
with other circumstances adversely affecting the Rohingya family life in northern Rakhine State, the
regional order had the potential of dissuading Rohingya couples from having more than two children.
The testimony of Witness NJ underscores that possibility. In our view, however, this does not as such
and necessarily support an inference that Myanmar intended the biological destruction of the
Rohingya in northern Myanmar. There is a material distinction between an intent to limit the growth
of a substantial part of a group and an intent to destroy that part biologically; the former cannot,
without more, be equated with the latter.
26. In our view, the principal factors which constitute the relevant pattern of conduct in the
present case are (i) the scale and systematic nature of the attacks by Myanmar’s security forces
against the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State during the 2017 clearance operations; (ii) the fact
that those attacks went far beyond what was justified by the necessity of confronting the ARSA
insurgency; (iii) the specific targeting of Rohingya civilians; and (iv) the nature, extent and degree
of the injuries inflicted upon the Rohingya population of northern Rakhine State, particularly women
and children.
27. Based on that pattern of conduct, however, we are not convinced that the only reasonable
inference to be drawn from the evidence is that Myanmar acted with genocidal intent. Our principal
reasons for reaching that conclusion are as follows.
28. First, and as a preliminary matter, we consider that no significant weight should attach to
the operations conducted in October and November 2016 for the purpose of establishing genocidal
intent on the part of Myanmar. The Judgment, wisely, does not do so. Indeed, there is good reason
not to consider the 2016 operations as forming part of the relevant pattern of conduct: the evidence
in that regard is insufficient and, at any rate, it does not point to the existence of genocidal intent.
That the operations involved human rights violations is in little doubt. We note that, in its August
2017 Final Report, the Advisory Commission on Rakhine State referred to allegations of “serious
human rights violations by the security forces” 12. Reports by non-governmental organizations
forming part of the evidentiary record likewise suggest that human rights violations may indeed have
been committed by Myanmar in 2016 13. The evidence before the Court, however, falls short of the
standard of full conclusiveness which is required to establish those allegations. Even if they were
established, they relate to conduct of relatively limited geographical scope. Above all, they include
allegations of temporary detentions on a significant scale rather than a pattern of conduct capable of
bringing about the physical or biological destruction of the protected group or a substantial part
thereof.

-929. On the evidence before the Court, the 2017 clearance operations also do not give rise to a
pattern of conduct from which the existence of genocidal intent can be identified as the only
reasonable inference. In our explanation, we begin with the three locations on which The Gambia
placed the greatest emphasis in these proceedings: namely the three villages of Min Gyi, Chut Pyin
and Maung Nu.
30. We have no doubt that Myanmar’s security forces committed a large number of acts
constituting forms of the actus reus of genocide, as described in Article II of the Convention at Min
Gyi, Chut Pyin and Maung Nu, and that many of those acts went far beyond what was necessary to
effectively confront the ARSA insurgency. On the basis of the evidence before the Court, we are
fully convinced that acts perpetrated in these three villages included not only killings within the
meaning of Article II (a), notably including the targeting and killing of children, but also acts causing
serious bodily and mental harm within the meaning of Article II (b) of the Convention. We are in
full agreement, too, with the findings made in the Judgment that the conduct of Myanmar’s security
forces in Min Gyi and Chut Pyin included rape and other forms of sexual violence against women,
in several instances carried out with abhorrent brutality.
31. Those findings establish conduct with similar characteristics at the three locations
concerned. They do not, without more, establish that similar conduct formed a pattern extending
across northern Rakhine State or directed against the entire Rohingya population there, that is against
the entire substantial part of the protected group, as identified by the Court. Min Gyi, Maung Nu and
Chut Pyin are three out of a total of 509 Rohingya villages in that region. Crucially, the evidence
does not convincingly demonstrate that the salient features of the conduct established at those three
locations were replicated on a significantly broader scale.
32. This line of analysis is consistent with the Court’s finding of genocide in the Bosnia case.
It is true that in this case the Court determined that genocide was committed in a single town, namely
Srebrenica. In that case, however, the Court did not see any reason to disagree with the findings of
the Appeals Chamber of the ICTY in Krstić, which were based on the fact that the “Bosnian Muslims
of Srebrenica” were “emblematic of the Bosnian Muslims in general” and that therefore the
importance of “that community” was “not captured solely by its size”. This was due, in particular, to
Srebrenica’s prominence in the eyes of the international community. In the present case, the relevant
substantial part of the protected group is formed by the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State. There
has been no suggestion  and rightly so  that “the Rohingya of Min Gyi, Chut Pyin and Maung
Nu” were emblematic of the Rohingya in Myanmar.
33. We turn, then, to locations other than Min Gyi, Chut Pyin and Maung Nu.
34. Most of the evidence submitted by The Gambia which documents specific brutalities is
concentrated on the three aforementioned villages. However, The Gambia does not place reliance on
these locations alone. The second pillar of its case, in so far as the allegations of a pattern of conduct
are concerned, rests on the assertion that, in at least 54 locations, acts including but extending well
beyond the destruction of property were perpetrated as part of the same “modus operandi”. In support
of that contention, The Gambia’s expert testified that a common pattern of conduct was replicated in
“54 documented instances”, including, in particular, “surprise attacks on civilian villages”,

- 10 “often from multiple angles”, “[i]ndiscriminate shooting from the get-go”, “executions”, “shooting
at refugees”, and “elements of rape”.
35. Myanmar, for its part, has strongly contested this claim.
36. The Judgment finds that acts constituting the forms of actus reus described in Article II (a)
and (b) of the Convention were committed at six additional locations beyond those identified above
(Judgment, paras. 226-247).
37. The Gambia’s submissions in relation to the set of 54 locations and the assertions of its
expert rely principally if not entirely on the following passage from the 2018 FFM Report:
“‘Clearance Operations’ following the same modus operandi took place in
numerous other Rohingya villages across northern Rakhine State. The Mission verified,
through multiple interviews and other information, ‘clearance operations’ similar to
those detailed above in a total of 54 separate locations, and it received first-hand
accounts of additional ‘clearance operations’ in a further 22 locations.” 14
38. The Judgment likewise refers to this factual assertion (Judgment, para. 248). This factual
assertion is indeed a significant one in that it purports to establish a pattern from 54 (or even 76)
locations, rather than being confined to no more than 10 (or even as few as 3) in number. We have
serious doubts about the considerable evidentiary weight accorded by the majority to the FFM
reports, and particularly to the 2018 Report, as well as its approach to the corroboration of the factual
assertions contained therein. We do not, however, consider it necessary to address those evidentiary
issues in the present opinion, because the assertion that the same “modus operandi” replicated itself
across 54 (or 76) villages is made in the report in conclusory fashion, without supporting evidence,
and even without making clear, for example in a footnote, the specific locations the FFM had in mind
when it made its rather categorical declaration.
39. Even if The Gambia’s case were taken at its highest, and the factual assertions contained
in the 2018 FFM Report were accepted as proven to the required standard, they would still fail to
establish a broadly similar pattern of conduct involving acts under Article II of the Convention. Since
the FFM’s assertion of “the same modus operandi” is not accompanied by an identification of the
54 locations, we undertook a close review of the specific locations that can be identified from other
elements of the 2018 Report. This review does not yield a common “modus operandi” spanning
54 locations, much less 76. Our review indicates that widespread killing is recorded in 12 additional
locations, while individual (non-widespread) incidents of such conduct are identified in a further
eight locations. In the remaining 28 locations, the Report records no killings. Similarly, allegations
of mass rape concern 11 additional locations, while isolated incidents are reported in 3 others; no
allegations of rape are recorded in the remaining 34 locations. Thus, even if the factual assertions
contained in the 2018 Report were all accepted as proven, they would show that the security forces

kept its border open to these refugees. Yes, there might have been a few Tatmadaw
soldiers who did not interfere with those border crossings. But they were the exceptions
rather than the rule. The fact that so many Rohingya made it to Bangladesh was not due
to the Tatmadaw’s kindness, but to the sheer volume of the refugee flow, which made
it impossible to stop.”
41. Myanmar has categorically denied The Gambia’s claim.
42. In this respect, the Judgment makes the following findings (Judgment, paras. 269-270):
“269. The Court observes that the record also contains credible statements from
numerous witnesses who encountered security forces on their journey to Bangladesh
but were permitted to proceed unimpeded . . . Some Rohingya interviewed by the FFM
who were not attacked reported being ordered by the security forces they encountered
during the journey to leave or they would be killed . . .
270. Based on the evidence before it, the Court is convinced that, in addition to
the extreme hardship of the journey, the Myanmar security forces systematically shot at
Rohingya as they were fleeing the clearance operations in villages, and that a significant
number of Rohingya were subjected to killing, rape and other attacks or threats of attack
during the flight to Bangladesh.”
43. We note that the Judgment draws a distinction between the shooting of Rohingya “as they
were fleeing the clearance operations in villages” and the “killing, rape and other attacks or threats
of attack during the flight to Bangladesh”. That distinction is important. The shooting of Rohingya
during the clearance operations should be kept separate from the distinct factual question of whether
the relevant pattern of conduct also included systematic attacks of Rohingya by Myanmar’s security
forces during their flight to Bangladesh. It is noteworthy that the Judgment uses the term
“systematically” only in relation to the attacks during clearance operations in certain villages. With

- 12 respect to the flight of Rohingya to Bangladesh, it states that a “significant number of Rohingya”
were subjected to killing, rape and other attacks or threats of attack during their flight to Bangladesh.
44. In examining the relevant evidence, we focus exclusively on the period during which
hundreds of thousands of Rohingya fled to Bangladesh, to avoid considering for a second time the
evidence concerning shootings that occurred during the clearance operations themselves.
45. On this point too, The Gambia has relied primarily on the 2018 FFM Report. In this
respect, however, the Report largely consists of accounts indicating that individual Rohingya were
shot, killed or injured while attempting to flee, or that “many” were shot during their flight, without
demonstrating that such attacks were representative or systematic. We note that no more than 28 out
of those 875 persons interviewed by the FFM for its 2018 Report reported attacks on Rohingya
en route to Bangladesh. While the FFM Report records instances of the laying of landmines to be
attributed to Myanmar, it neither gives a geographical or quantitative account of this activity nor does
it allege that its purpose was to attack fleeing Rohingya, rather it suggests that this was “to prevent
or dissuade Rohingya from returning” 15.
46. The Gambia, relying on the 2018 FFM Report, appears to characterize attacks on fleeing
Rohingya as systematic, suggesting that they constituted the rule rather than the exception. However,
a closer examination of the underlying source cited by The Gambia  paragraph 764 of the
Report — reveals a significantly more nuanced account than presented by The Gambia. Rather than
describing a systematic resort to lethal force against those attempting to flee, the passage records a
range of different situations. Many individuals succeeded in crossing safely, others tragically
drowned, and still others were reportedly shot. In our view, the FFM account does not, by itself,
support the broader proposition that Myanmar’s security forces systematically attacked Rohingya
throughout their flight to Bangladesh.
47. Finally, The Gambia has also referred to the assessment put forward by Médecins Sans
Frontières that “13.4 percent of violent deaths to the Rohingya occurred during the period between
displacement from their village and arrival in Bangladesh” 16. In the absence of precise knowledge
about the underlying methodology, however, we find it difficult to attach significant evidentiary
value to this statement. In any event, it does not support a finding that Myanmar’s security forces
systematically subjected the Rohingya to lethal attacks throughout the period concerned.

52. Thus far, our assessment concerning Rohingya fleeing across the border to Bangladesh has
focused on allegations of killings and of causing serious bodily harm, leading us to conclude that
such acts do not, on the evidence before the Court, constitute a pattern. There may however be room
for a clarification of the Court’s jurisprudence on genocidal intent as regards the longer-term
biological consequences, for the group, of causing mental harm within the meaning of Article II (b)
of the Convention to members of at least a substantial part of a protected group (paragraph 7 above).
Therefore, if the security forces of Myanmar caused serious mental harm within the meaning of
Article II (b) of the Convention to those hundreds of thousands of Rohingya who fled to Bangladesh,
this type of injury might as well have to be included in the relevant pattern of conduct. Thus, we shall
now examine whether the flight of hundreds of thousands of Rohingya to Bangladesh in connection
with the 2017 clearance operations led to serious mental harm within the meaning of Article II (b) of
the Convention.
53. The Gambia has claimed that the mass exodus of Rohingya to Bangladesh resulted in
lasting mental harm to those now forced to live in refugee camps.
54. Myanmar has denied that claim and responded that The Gambia has not supported it by
sufficient evidence.
55. The Judgment, referring particularly to the Judgment of the Appeals Chamber of the ICTY
in Tolimir, states that forcible displacement can cause “serious mental harm” for the purposes of the
actus reus of genocide when coupled with circumstances such as in the present case (Judgment,
para. 328).
56. We agree that serious mental harm under Article II (b) of the Convention must involve a
grave and long-term disadvantage to the ability of the human being concerned to lead a normal and
constructive life (Judgment, para. 325). We also concur with the Judgment’s general proposition that
forcible displacement, while not of itself being an act listed in Article II, can occur under such
circumstances that it causes serious mental harm to the displaced persons. We disagree, however,
with how the majority applies this general standard in the present case.
57. As the ICTY’s Appeals Chamber stated in Tolimir, the causing of serious mental harm
within the meaning of Article II (b) of the Convention requires “proof of a result, i.e. that serious

- 15 mental harm was inflicted” 20. The Chamber emphasized the importance of demonstrating lasting
consequences, noting that the absence of “evidence of any long-term psychological trauma”
precluded a finding of serious mental harm 21. In that respect, the Appeals Chamber accepted that the
Trial Chamber distinguished between the displacement campaigns that took place in Srebrenica, on
the one hand, and in Žepa, on the other hand, and that, with respect to the displaced Bosnian Muslims
in Srebrenica, the Trial Chamber attached significant importance to “the painful separation process
from their male family members at Potočari, the fear and uncertainty as to their fate and that of their
detained male relatives” in addition to other conditions present in that case 22. In relation to Žepa, the
Appeals Chamber observed that “[e]ven though the emotional pain and distress inflicted upon Žepa’s
Bosnian Muslims was irrefutably grave, no evidence of any long-term psychological trauma was
cited in the Trial Judgement” 23. In the absence of references to such evidence, the Appeals Chamber
accepted that the Trial Chamber had justifiably denied a finding that the Bosnian Muslims forcibly
displaced from Žepa had suffered serious mental harm within the meaning of Article II (b) of the
Convention 24.
58. We concur with the ICTY’s Appeals Chamber in Tolimir that it is necessary to pose an
exacting threshold for establishing serious mental harm within the meaning of Article II (b). To
proceed otherwise would seriously risk undermining the settled jurisprudence of the Court, as
confirmed in the Judgment (para. 407), that forcible displacement does not, of itself, fall under any
act listed in Article II of the Genocide Convention.
59. In the present case, the evidence before the Court establishes that a significant number of
those Rohingya who fled from northern Rakhine State to Bangladesh experienced fear, anguish and
emotional distress in circumstances of violence and insecurity. The legal question, however, is
whether the evidence establishes fully convincingly that those experiences caused serious mental
harm within the meaning of Article II (b). We do not deny that forcible displacement may, in
principle, result in serious mental harm, depending on the circumstances. In the present case,
however, we are not fully convinced by the evidence that the demanding threshold of serious mental
harm within the meaning of Article II (b) of the Convention has been reached for the vast majority
of those Rohingya who fled to Bangladesh. In particular, the body of witness testimony before the
Court does not suggest that most of these Rohingya have suffered serious mental harm within the
meaning of Article II (b) of the Convention because of their flight. Nor does it permit the Court
reliably to distinguish harm caused by conduct attributable to Myanmar from that associated with
subsequent conditions in the refugee camps, prolonged uncertainty and other intervening
circumstances.

The Gambia placed great reliance is far from providing fully conclusive evidence in the relevant
respects. The report by Fortify Rights records information provided by high percentages of the
Rohingya survey participants of symptoms which the report describes to be “indicative” of

61. At this point of the analysis, we wish to emphasize that, for the purpose of establishing
Myanmar’s genocidal intent, it is not necessary to prove that the destruction of the relevant
substantial part of the Rohingya population in northern Rakhine State in fact occurred. What must
be established is the intention to bring about such destruction. In the absence of direct evidence of
such intent, however, the issue is whether the conduct of the security forces of Myanmar included
the intentional infliction of serious mental harm within the meaning of Article II (b) of the
Convention on hundreds of thousands of Rohingya because of their flight to Bangladesh. For, if
established, such harm could have constituted an element of the pattern of conduct from which the
existence of genocidal intent at the relevant time might have reasonably been inferred. For the reasons
just given, the evidence before the Court does not convincingly establish that the flight of hundreds
of thousands of Rohingya to Bangladesh — even assuming that Myanmar intended to bring about
their displacement — caused serious mental harm within the meaning of Article II (b) of the
Convention to those who fled or at least a vast majority of them. Accordingly, even assuming that
there may be room for clarifying the Court’s jurisprudence concerning the significance, for genocidal
intent, of the longer-term biological consequences of serious mental harm inflicted upon members of
at least a substantial part of a protected group, the mental pain caused by the mass exodus of
Rohingya from northern Rakhine State resulting from the 2017 clearance operations cannot be
regarded as forming part of a relevant pattern of conduct supporting a reasonable inference of an
intent to destroy the relevant substantial part of the protected group.
62. In conclusion, the relevant pattern of conduct in the present case consists of the commission
by Myanmar’s security forces of a large number of acts falling within Article II (a) and (b) of the
Convention at Min Gyi, Chut Pyin and Maung Nu. Those acts went far beyond what was necessary
to confront the ARSA insurgency effectively and included rapes and other forms of sexual violence
against women, often carried out with atrocious brutality, as well as egregious violence against
children. Even if The Gambia’s case were accepted at its highest, however, and even if all the specific
factual assertions contained in the 2018 FFM Report were treated as established, that pattern of
conduct would extend only to widespread killings in 12 additional locations and widespread rape in
11 additional locations. Accordingly, even on that assumption, it would involve the widespread
commission of physical attacks against a relatively small proportion of the Rohingya population in
northern Rakhine State. Whatever the precise number of victims, those subjected to documented

- 17 physical attacks constituted a very small minority of that population. The relevant pattern of conduct
is thus considerably more limited in scope and scale than The Gambia has argued and the Court has
found.
IV. WHETHER GENOCIDAL INTENT IS THE ONLY REASONABLE INFERENCE
63. We now turn to the question whether the existence of genocidal intent on the part of
Myanmar at the relevant time is the only reasonable inference to be drawn from the pattern of conduct
identified above. In our view, the relatively limited geographical scope of this pattern of conduct and
the comparatively limited scale of destruction within the meaning of Article II of the Convention do
not preclude a finding of genocidal intent. However, these two elements, especially if taken together,
prima facie point away from the conclusion that the existence of such intent is the only reasonable
inference to be drawn.
64. In Croatia, the Court examined the pattern of conduct from two perspectives to determine
whether genocidal intent was the only reasonable inference to be drawn from it. First, it considered
“the context in which the acts constituting the actus reus of genocide within the meaning of
subparagraphs (a) and (b) of the Convention were committed” 25. Secondly, it asked whether the State
concerned did not avail itself of opportunities to destroy a substantial part of the protected group 26.
65. As regards the context in this case, the Judgment appears to attach significance to media
reports concerning three speeches delivered by the then Senior General, Min Aung Hlaing. In
particular, the Judgment mentions that the Senior General was reported to have said that “[t]he
Bengali problem was a long-standing one which has become an unfinished job despite the efforts of
the previous governments to solve it. The government in office is taking great care in solving the
problem.” (Judgment, para. 157.)
66. Assuming the accuracy of the media report, the statement by the then Senior General of
Myanmar’s armed forces cannot be assessed in isolation, but must be interpreted in the context of
the full speech of which it formed a part. Elsewhere in the reported speech, the Senior General
denounced the “Bengali terrorists” for their “well-planned terrorist acts” beginning, according to
those reports, on 25 August 2017. He also attributed attacks on “the local ethnics” to the “Bengalis”
more generally. The reports further indicate that he stated that “[t]he Bengalis calling themselves
Rohingya has become a national cause” and asserted that, according to the Government, “absolutely,
our country has no Rohingya race”. He also referred to events in 1942, stating that “Bengalis
attacked, murdered and coerced” Rakhine ethnics “into leaving their homes”, and declared that the
Government “will never let such a terrible occurrence happen again”. In that context, he stated that
“the entire people must defend the country with strong patriotism”. At the same time, the reported
speech also emphasized that “the only measure that [the] security forces are taking is to ensure
everything is within the framework of the law” and that “[p]eople living in Myanmar must obey its

- 18 laws”. Referring to the violence of October 2016, the Senior General further observed that “[a]s the

speech also states that “[t]here must be cooperative efforts to ensure that the people of Rakhine State
have peaceful living conditions” and that “[t]he local people for their part need to return home and

as possible while efforts must be made for regional development.”
67. We consider certain aspects of this speech to be reprehensible and capable of fuelling
hostility towards the Rohingya in Myanmar, and in northern Rakhine State in particular. We are
nevertheless unable to read the reported reference to an “unfinished job” and to the Government’s
determination “to solve the problem” as expressing a policy of destroying the Rohingya, whether in
Myanmar generally or in northern Rakhine State in particular. On the contrary, when the speech is
read as a whole, the more conciliatory passages point in a different direction.
68. We have also looked closely at the two other speeches, reportedly made by the then Senior
General, and as relied upon by The Gambia. Here again, we have been unable to find language which,
if reported with accuracy, could reasonably be interpreted as expressing the policy goal of destroying
the Rohingya in Myanmar or in northern Rakhine State.
69. With respect to what The Gambia has characterized as a Tatmadaw campaign of hate
speech and propaganda on Facebook, we reach the same conclusion. Much of the relevant material
reflects Myanmar’s long-standing official historical narrative and its concern that an “alien Bengali
race” might, through demographic growth, outnumber what they regarded as the “national races” in
Rakhine State. Certain posts went further and used dehumanizing language against the Rohingya.
While such posts are abhorrent and wholly unacceptable, they cannot, without more, be equated with
a policy of the State of destroying the population concerned.
70. Furthermore, the activities of ARSA at the material time must be considered as part of the
broader context in which the relevant pattern of conduct occurred. In this respect, the Judgment
contains a number of findings with which we concur. It finds that, on 9 October 2016, ARSA attacked
three Border Guard Police headquarters (Judgment, para. 175); that, by August 2017, ARSA had
acquired the capacity to carry out multiple co-ordinated violent attacks (Judgment, para. 174); that,
on 25 August 2017, ARSA launched co-ordinated attacks on at least 30 security force outposts across
northern Rakhine State (Judgment, para. 177); that ARSA activity occurred in areas near Min Gyi
beginning on 25 August 2017 (Judgment, para. 187); that ARSA attacked several outposts in the
general vicinity of Chut Pyin on 25 August 2017 (Judgment, para. 202); that three ARSA attacks in
the immediate vicinity of Maung Nu on 25 August 2017 were reported to the FFM (Judgment,
para. 215); and that the ARSA commander-in-chief issued a message ordering the burning of
non-Rohingya villages (Judgment, para. 178). More generally, the findings in the Judgment broadly
reflect Myanmar’s position that both the 2016 and the 2017 clearance operations were preceded by
ARSA attacks and that each operation ended relatively soon after Myanmar’s security forces had
eliminated the threat posed by ARSA.

- 19 71. We now turn to the question whether Myanmar did not make use of opportunities to destroy
the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State. As the Court held in Croatia, the fact that a State does not
avail itself of existing opportunities to destroy the relevant part of the protected group constitutes a
factor weighing against the inference that the State possessed genocidal intent at the material time.
In that Judgment, the Court also took into account the number of Croat deaths alleged by Croatia in
relation to the size of the targeted part of the protected group, observing, that the number of deaths
was small relative to the overall size of that group. This was one of the main factors leading the Court
to conclude that genocidal intent was not the only reasonable inference available27.
72. The Judgment acknowledges that Myanmar “did not take every opportunity it had to
destroy members of the Rohingya group” (Judgment, para. 435). We agree. However, the
formulation that Myanmar did not take “every” opportunity tends to obscure what the evidence
before the Court in fact demonstrates, namely that Myanmar’s security forces did not make use of
highly significant opportunities to destroy the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State (paragraphs 48-50
above).
73. The Judgment nevertheless concludes that Myanmar’s failure to exploit these opportunities
does not preclude an inference of genocidal intent. The sole reason advanced in support of this
conclusion is that “the claimed purpose of Myanmar’s actions was to undertake counter-insurgency
operations in response to ARSA attacks, which imposed geographic and other constraints on the
opportunities that were available to Myanmar” (Judgment, para. 435).
74. We disagree. There can be little doubt that, given their overwhelming presence, the security
forces of Myanmar possessed ample opportunities to destroy the Rohingya throughout northern
Rakhine State, well beyond Min Gyi, Chut Pyin, and Maung Nu, as well as the six additional
locations examined in the Judgment. Most importantly, Myanmar’s security forces possessed ample
opportunities to destroy Rohingya during their flight to Bangladesh. In our view, it is erroneous to
suggest, as the Judgment does, that the practical difficulty which Myanmar’s security forces might
have faced in portraying their exploitation of those opportunities as comprising legitimate
counter-insurgency operations in areas where ARSA was no longer present somehow limited the
opportunities themselves. The absence of ARSA may have made the exploitation of those
opportunities harder to portray as counter-insurgency, but it did not diminish the opportunities
themselves.
75. The Judgment seeks to support its reasoning by relying on a statement of the Appeals
Chamber of the ICTY in Krstić concerning the events at Srebrenica. In that case, only the able-bodied
Muslim men of Srebrenica were killed, while the remainder of the community was forcibly displaced.
In explaining why the Bosnian Serb forces nevertheless acted with genocidal intent, the Appeals
Chamber stated:
“The decision not to kill the women or children may be explained by the Bosnian
Serbs’ sensitivity to public opinion. In contrast to the killing of the captured military

27 Ibid., p. 127, para. 437.

- 20 men, such an action could not easily be kept secret, or disguised as a military operation,
and so carried an increased risk of attracting international censure.” 28
76. The context in which this statement was made must be borne in mind. Before making this
observation, the Appeals Chamber had already explained why it considered genocidal intent to have
been established in the case before it. Its central reasoning was that the relevant substantial part of
the protected group had, in its entirety, been subjected to physical and biological destruction through
the combined effect of the killings and the separation of the men from the women and children.
Against that background, the fact that the Bosnian Serb forces had not taken the opportunity to
destroy the entire Muslim community of Srebrenica by killing all of its members was immaterial to
the finding of genocidal intent. The statement relied upon by the Krstić Judgment was therefore an
obiter dictum. This context also explains why the Appeals Chamber saw no need to determine
whether the Bosnian Serb forces had in fact refrained from killing all Muslims in Srebrenica to avoid
international censure. It merely observed with due restraint that this may have been an explanation.
77. The present Judgment, by contrast, proceeds without due restraint. Its reasoning rests on
the assumption that, had ARSA been present in the vicinity, Myanmar would have taken the
opportunity to destroy the hundreds of thousands of Rohingya fleeing to Bangladesh while presenting
such destruction as a legitimate element of counter-insurgency operations. The Judgment thus
reasons in a circle: it assumes, rather than demonstrates, the very conclusion it seeks to prove.
78. For these reasons, we consider that the broader context in which the relevant pattern of
conduct occurred does not support an inference that Myanmar acted with genocidal intent at the
relevant time. The fact that the security forces of Myanmar eschewed ample opportunities they
possessed to destroy the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State speaks against such an inference.
79. The Judgment’s approach to the criterion of opportunity represents a further departure
from the Court’s prior jurisprudence. In Croatia, the fact that available opportunities were not
exploited was treated as a feature of the pattern weighing against an inference of genocidal intent. In
the present Judgment, the Court acknowledges that Myanmar’s security forces left unused multiple
opportunities available to them to destroy members of the Rohingya group, but it deprives that
circumstance of significant weight by invoking “geographic and other constraints” (para. 435). The
Judgment does not say what these “other” constraints, within the area of the clearance operations
themselves, are. Considering the immediately preceding reference to the Krstić Appeals Chamber’s
discussion of international public opinion, however, what the Judgment evidently has in mind is
Myanmar’s perceived need to portray its conduct as part of a legitimate counter-insurgency
operation. One may accept that such a need could, in theory, cause perpetrators possessing genocidal
intent to refrain from seizing opportunities to destroy members of the group. The Judgment does not,
however, demonstrate that this supposed constraint existed, still less that it accounted for the fact that
Myanmar left these specific and highly relevant opportunities unused. In so far as those opportunities
are concerned, the Court’s reasoning is pure supposition. The result is that a feature of the pattern of

28 ICTY, Prosecutor v. Radislav Krstić, IT-98-33-A, Appeals Chamber Judgement, 19 April 2004, para. 31.

- 21 conduct which, under the Court’s prior jurisprudence, would weigh against an inference of genocidal
intent is neutralized by an unproved theory consistent with that intent. Put differently, the respondent
State is effectively required to disprove a hypothesis capable of preserving the Court’s inference of
genocidal intent. In that respect, the practical operation of the burden of proof is reversed, making
the indirect establishment of genocidal intent materially easier.

80. In addition to the above-mentioned factors that speak against an inference of genocidal
intent (paragraphs 63-79 above), we are of the view that the relevant pattern of conduct reasonably
permits two alternative inferences. As will be seen, each of these is, on its own, incompatible with
the existence of genocidal intent. In this regard, we emphasize that the question is not whether either
alternative inference is, on balance, more persuasive than an inference of genocidal intent. The test
laid down in the Court’s jurisprudence is the “only reasonable inference”, not a “more reasonable
inference”. Accordingly, if even only one alternative inference, inconsistent with genocidal intent,
can reasonably be drawn from the evidence, the inference of genocidal intent cannot be sustained.
81. In our view, the first reasonable alternative inference is that the relevant pattern of conduct
formed part of an effort pursued by Myanmar of forcibly displacing the vast majority of the Rohingya
from northern Rakhine State. The fact that this pattern of conduct included the widespread
commission of acts falling within Article II (a) and (b) of the Convention in a relatively small number
of Rohingya villages does not preclude such an inference. On the contrary, it is at least reasonably
plausible that the widespread commission of such acts, in a subset of locations, was intended to
compel the vast majority of the Rohingya population, including in other locations, to flee northern
Rakhine State. The widespread commission of acts falling within Article II (a) and (b) of the
Convention in a subset of locations is thus entirely consistent with an objective of forced
displacement. The same is true of indications in the case file that Myanmar imposed restrictions on
Rohingya’s access to food in northern Rakhine State and that these restrictions contributed to the
departure of many Rohingya to Bangladesh (see paragraph 24 above).
82. In this respect, we recall that, notwithstanding the factual differences between that case
and the present one, the Court in Croatia likewise found that genocidal intent had not been
established even though the relevant pattern of conduct included the systematic commission of acts
prohibited by Article II of the Convention. The Court concluded
“that the acts committed by the JNA and Serb forces essentially had the effect of making
the Croat population flee the territories concerned. It was not a question of
systematically destroying that population, but of forcing it to leave the areas controlled
by these armed forces.” 29

29 Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (Croatia v. Serbia),

Judgment, I.C.J. Reports 2015 (I), p. 127, para. 435.

- 22 83. This conclusion was significant because it recognized that the systematic commission of
acts falling within Article II does not, without more, eliminate forcible displacement as a reasonable
explanation of a pattern of conduct. The Court examined the purpose underlying the violent acts and
concluded that they were intended to force the relevant population to leave, not to destroy it. The
present Judgment invokes this jurisprudence. But then it takes a markedly different approach. It relies
on evidence of violence preceding and, to a lesser extent, accompanying the flight of hundreds of
thousands of Rohingya to dismiss forcible displacement as a possible reasonable explanation of the
pattern of conduct. It does so without adequately considering whether the relevant violence against
Rohingya in a subset of locations may itself have been committed with the purpose of terrorizing the
vast majority of the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State into leaving and preventing their return. By
proceeding in that way, the Judgment in effect substantially weakens the distinction between
genocide and forcible displacement which the Court had established in its previous jurisprudence.
84. The Judgment advances three reasons for rejecting the inference that Myanmar intended
to forcibly displace, rather than destroy, the vast majority of the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State.
First, it observes that nothing in the case file suggests that Myanmar’s security forces facilitated the
mass exodus to Bangladesh, for example by organizing transportation (Judgment, paras. 458-459).
Secondly, it relies on its finding that, in many instances, the Myanmar’s security forces attacked
Rohingya during their flight to Bangladesh (Judgment, para. 460). Thirdly, it considers that “[a]
number of aspects of the clearance operations, including the brutal manner in which they were
conducted, . . . are not consistent with an intent to forcibly displace the population” (Judgment,
para. 462).
85. In our view, none of these considerations is persuasive. First, the fact that a State neither
facilitates nor organizes the displacement of a population does not militate against the inference that
it intended forcibly to displace that population. Northern Rakhine State had witnessed earlier mass
departures of Muslim populations, and of the Rohingya in particular, without any organization or
facilitation by State authorities. Myanmar therefore had good reason to expect that the systematic
commission of acts falling within Article II of the Convention in a relatively small number of
Rohingya villages would instil fear throughout the Rohingya community and prompt a mass exodus.
In any event, forcible displacement may take a variety of forms; the absence of organized transports
or other efforts of facilitating the transfer does not make the inference of forcible displacement
unreasonable.
86. Secondly, as we have set out before (see paragraphs 45-50 above) and as the Judgment
itself acknowledges, although there is evidence before the Court that Myanmar’s security forces, on
several occasions, attacked Rohingya during their flight to Bangladesh, the evidence does not
establish that such acts occurred as a policy or even that such attacks were frequent. It therefore is
reasonably possible that these incidents constituted exceptions to a broader policy aimed at forcibly
displacing, rather than destroying, the Rohingya population. To hold, as the Judgment suggests, that
an alternative inference can only reasonably be drawn if it can explain, without any exception, the
“totality of the evidence” (Judgment, para. 443) has no basis in the Court’s previous jurisprudence
and, in fact, departs from it. If this criterion however were to be applied, it would, by its own, exclude
the possibility of inferring genocidal intent in the first place. For, as we have shown above, there
exist a number of elements of the evidence, for example the mostly unhindered flight of the vast
majority of Rohingya from northern Rakhine State to Bangladesh (paragraphs 74-79), which cannot
be convincingly explained on the basis of an inference of genocidal intent.

- 23 87. Thirdly, we note that the reference in the Judgment to “[a] number of aspects of the
clearance operations”, which are allegedly “not consistent with an intent to forcibly displace the
population”, is so vague that it is no more than an assertion. In so far as the Judgment singles out
“the brutal manner in which they were conducted” as an aspect of the clearance operations allegedly
not consistent with an intent to forcibly displace the relevant population, we disagree. For, as we
mentioned before (paragraph 83 above), the Judgment thereby ignores the possibility that the
relevant brutality may itself have been employed with the purpose of terrorizing the vast majority of
the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State into leaving and preventing their return.
88. As the Judgment correctly finds, the brutal manner in which the clearance operations were
conducted, included a number of documented incidents in which Rohingya houses were burned while
inhabitants were still present inside, including incidents in which persons were allegedly locked
inside, forced into or prevented from leaving burning houses. The 2018 FFM Report characterizes
the evidence before it as having “verified a pattern” of such conduct. However, the specific incidents
identified in the Report in support of that characterization concern a limited number of locations 30.
The evidence does not establish that the burning of houses while the inhabitants were still present
was a systematic feature of the destruction of the Rohingya villages more broadly, nor does the
satellite evidence concerning the widespread destruction of villages establish that inhabitants were
present at the time of burning. In fact, the record also contains evidence indicating that “burning has
occurred in [Rohingya] villages, long after the Rohingya fled them” 31. These burnings support an
inference of an intent to forcibly displace the vast majority of the Rohingya population in northern
Rakhine State rather than to destroy it. The destruction of homes, by itself, does not amount to
physical or biological destruction of the protected group. This remains true even where such
destruction renders the return of displaced members of a protected group constituting a substantial
part thereof difficult or impossible. In our view, the widespread destruction of Rohingya villages can
therefore not be regarded as one of the factors forming part of a pattern of conduct capable of
revealing genocidal intent.
89. The second possible inference is “collective punishment” inflicted above all to intimidate
and thereby deter. In our view, on the evidence, it is impossible to exclude a reasonable inference
that Myanmar systematically committed acts falling within Article II (a) and (b) of the Convention
in a limited number of Rohingya villages in northern Rakhine State with the intention of inflicting
“collective punishment” and this with the goal of intimidating the Rohingya population throughout
the region to deter it from supporting ARSA in the future. In this regard, we recall that the relevant
pattern of conduct followed the ARSA attacks of 25 August 2017 and ended very soon after the threat
posed by ARSA had been neutralized (see paragraph 66 above). The Judgment acknowledges that
ARSA mobilized several thousand villagers to participate in its attacks (Judgment, para. 174).
Viewed against this background, it is reasonably possible to infer that Myanmar conducted its 2017
clearance operations in a manner that far exceeded the limits of a lawful counter-insurgency

90. The Judgment does not directly address the possibility of an alternative inference of
intimidation and deterrence. With respect to an intention of punishment, the Judgment concludes that
it does not constitute a reasonable alternative inference and advances three considerations in support
of that conclusion. First, it observes that an intent to punish may coexist with genocidal intent and
that “the two intentions may not always be capable of meaningful separation for the purpose of
drawing alternative inferences” of the latter (Judgment, para. 464). Secondly, it states that the Court’s
finding in Croatia — that the ill-treatment of prisoners at the Ovčara camp following the fall of
Vukovar was intended to punish rather than to destroy — was based on a substantially distinct set of
facts (Judgment, para. 465). Thirdly, it concludes that “the scale and nature of Myanmar’s actions
and the methods it employed went well beyond what would be required to punish the population”
(Judgment, para. 466).
91. In our view, none of these considerations undermines the reasons for concluding that the
infliction of “collective punishment” with the ultimate objective of intimidation and deterrence
constitutes a reasonable alternative inference in the present case. First, we do not disagree with the
proposition that an intent to punish may coexist with genocidal intent and that the two intentions
“may not always be capable of meaningful separation for the purpose of drawing alternative
inferences”. That general proposition, however, does not address the specific alternative inference
advanced here. The inference described in the preceding paragraph is not merely, and not even
primarily, one of punishment. Rather, it is that the violence inflicted upon certain Rohingya
communities, independently from serving as “collective punishment”, primarily served the purpose
of intimidating the wider Rohingya population with a view to deterring future support for ARSA.
Such an objective provides an explanation for the relevant pattern of conduct that is, in the words of
the Judgment, “meaningfully separable” from and in fact incompatible with an intent to destroy the
Rohingya in northern Rakhine State at the relevant time.
92. Secondly, we do not dispute that the factual circumstances surrounding the events
following the fall of Vukovar differ from those of the present case. Our reasoning, however, does not
depend upon any close factual analogy between the two situations. The significance of the Court’s
reasoning in Croatia lies in its general recognition that acts falling within Article II of the Convention
may, depending on the circumstances in which they occur, reasonably be explained by an objective
other than the destruction of the protected group, in whole or in part.
93. Thirdly, the Judgment’s assertion that “the scale and nature of Myanmar’s actions and the
methods it employed went well beyond what would be required to punish the population” does not
provide a convincing reason for rejecting the alternative inference under consideration. The relevant
question is not what would have been necessary “to punish the population”. The question is whether
the widespread commission by Myanmar’s security forces of acts falling within Article II (a) and (b)
of the Convention in a limited number of Rohingya villages in northern Rakhine State can be
reasonably explained by an intention to inflict “collective punishment” with the goal of intimidating
the Rohingya population throughout the region, and thereby deterring it from supporting ARSA in
the future. In our view, this is the case. Indeed, the severity of the violence is not inconsistent with

- 25 such a purpose: particularly harsh “punishment” of Rohingya in a limited number of villages may
reasonably be understood as a means to magnify the intimidating and deterring effect of the
“punishment” inflicted. Therefore, there is no reason to exclude such an inference in favour of
genocidal intent.
94. For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the broader context in which the relevant
pattern of conduct occurred does not support an inference that Myanmar acted with genocidal intent
at the relevant time. The fact that it did not use the ample opportunities it possessed to destroy the
Rohingya in northern Rakhine State speaks against such an inference. On the evidence, at least two
alternative inferences that are incompatible with the existence of genocidal intent are reasonably
available, namely that Myanmar pursued a policy of forcibly displacing the vast majority of the
Rohingya from northern Rakhine State; and/or that it systematically committed acts falling within
Article II (a) and (b) of the Convention against certain Rohingya communities as a form of
“collective punishment” designed, above all, to intimidate the wider Rohingya population and to
deter it from supporting ARSA in the future. Since either of these inferences may reasonably be
drawn from the evidence, it cannot be said that the only reasonable inference available is that
Myanmar acted with genocidal intent.
V. CONCLUDING OBSERVATIONS
95. We return to the point from which this opinion began. The Court’s jurisdiction is limited
to the Genocide Convention. Our conclusion is that the commission of genocide by Myanmar has
not been established. This neither excludes the possibility that Myanmar committed other
internationally wrongful acts nor diminishes the terrible suffering endured by the Rohingya,
particularly those living in northern Rakhine State.
96. To our regret, the Judgment’s contrary conclusion is not the result of applying the legal
framework for indirectly proving genocidal intent within the meaning of the Genocide Convention,
as articulated by the jurisprudence of the Court. It is rather the product of altering several components
of this legal framework: by broadening the scope of considerations treated as sufficiently probative
of genocidal intent; by weakening the connection between acts of violence and the goal of physical
and biological destruction; by discounting the evidentiary significance of available, but not used
opportunities to destroy; by artificially dividing the identification of genocidal intent into the two
steps of drawing first a reasonable inference of the existence of such an intent and then concluding
that this intent is the only reasonable inference because of the absence of any alternative reasonable
inference that could explain the “totality of the evidence”. Each step makes a finding of genocide
easier compared with the previously established test. Taken together, these steps substantially lower
the standard for indirectly proving genocidal intent articulated in Bosnia and Croatia while
purporting to remain faithful to those precedents. By proceeding in that way, the Judgment not only
fails to convince, but also ignores the Court’s previously stated principle that it will not depart from
settled jurisprudence unless there are “very particular reasons” 32 for doing so.
97. Few determinations entrusted to this Court carry consequences as grave as a finding that a
State has committed genocide. Such a determination requires the most thorough judicial scrutiny.
