INTRODUCTION

       1.       Dan S. Sullivan (“Senator Sullivan”) has represented the State of Alaska in

the United States Senate since 2015. He is running for reelection as a Republican in

November 2026, in what most observers believe will be a close race against Democrat

Mary Peltola.

       2.       Daniel J. Sullivan decided to run for Senator in 2026 as a Republican with

the intention of misleading supporters of Senator Sullivan into voting for him. The sole

purpose of his candidacy is to benefit the Peltola campaign by reducing the number of votes

for Senator Sullivan.

       3.       Congress has tasked state officials with “protect[ing] the integrity of the

electoral process,” 52 U.S.C. § 20501(b)(3), and the Supreme Court has long recognized

that states “ha[ve] an interest, if not a duty, to protect the integrity of its political processes

from frivolous or fraudulent candidacies.” Bullock v. Carter, 405 U.S. 135, 145 (1972);

see also Jenness v. Fortson, 403 U.S. 431, 442 (1971) (“There is surely an important state

interest in … avoiding confusion, deception, and even frustration of the democratic process

at the general election.”).

       4.       Should Alaska certify any electoral results tainted by the inclusion on the

ballot of Daniel J. Sullivan—whose candidacy, Defendant Beecher found, is “for the

purpose of confusing or misleading voters and compromising the fairness of the ballot” 1—

the State and its officers will fail in its obligation to protect the integrity of its election.

                                         STANDING

       16.     The United States has a substantial and legally cognizable interest in

maintaining the integrity of elections. See 52 U.S.C. §§ 20501(b)(3), 20510(a). The United

States suffers an injury in fact whenever federal law is violated, Vermont Agency of Nat.

Res. v. United States ex rel. Stevens, 529 U.S. 765, 771 (2000), including when remedying

such a violation is necessary to safeguard the sacred right of its citizens to vote, see United

States v. Raines, 362 U.S. 17, 27 (1960). The United States likewise suffers an injury when

the certification of the election for a federal office is tainted by a frivolous candidacy

because that undermines confidence in the make-up and workings of the federal

government.

       17.     Defendants are each substantially involved in the administration of elections

in Alaska, including the 2026 election for Senator, as well as the collection, counting, and

certification process following the election.

       18.     An order declaring a certification of an election result prejudiced by Daniel

J. Sullivan’s inclusion on the ballot unlawful and enjoining defendants from allowing such

a certification to occur would redress the United States’ injuries.

BACKGROUND

   A. Daniel J. Sullivan’s Senate Campaign

       19.    On or about May 16, 2026, Senator Sullivan filed to run for reelection to the

United States Senate.

       20.    On or about May 29, 2026, Daniel J. Sullivan filed to run for election to the

Senate. Daniel J. Sullivan had never previously run for political office.

       21.    Daniel J. Sullivan took several measures designed to dupe Alaska’s voters

into believing that he is Senator Sullivan, as detailed below, prompting complaints to the

Alaska Division of Elections.

       22.    On June 15, the Alaska Division of Elections (in a letter issued by Defendant

Beecher) found that Daniel J. Sullivan’s declaration of candidacy was not properly filed

with the Division because it was not filed in order to declare an actual good-faith candidacy

for the office of United States Senator but was instead filed with a purpose to confuse or

mislead and to thereby compromise the ballot’s fairness or neutrality.” Ex. A, at 2.

                 Sullivan”—the same name used by Senator Sullivan. Daniel J. Sullivan

                 did not register to vote under the name “Dan Sullivan.”

              b. According to the Division, Daniel J. Sullivan “requested to be designated

                 on the ballot as affiliated with the Republican Party”—the same party as

   B. Daniel J. Sullivan’s campaign threatens to mislead Alaska’s voters in the general
      election.

       26.    Alaska uses a two-step process for electing Senators and other officials.

       27.    In the primary election, all candidates appear on the ballot, regardless of

political party. With irrelevant exceptions, “only the four candidates who receive the

greatest number of votes for any office shall advance to the general election.” AK Stat. §

15.25.010.

       28.    The general election is “conducted by ranked-choice voting.” AK Stat. §

15.15.350(c). Voters rank their candidates in order of preference and do not have to rank

all candidates.

29.    “The election board shall initially tabulate each validly cast ballot as one vote

for the highest-ranked continuing candidate on that ballot or as an inactive ballot. If a

candidate is highest ranked on more than one-half of the active ballots, that candidate is

elected and the tabulation is complete.” A.S. § 15.15.350(d). Otherwise, “votes cast for

the defeated candidate shall cease counting for the defeated candidate and shall be added

to the totals of each ballot’s next-highest-ranked continuing candidate.” § 15.15.350(d)(2).

       30.    For example, consider a citizen who lists Candidate A as his first choice,

Candidate B as his second choice, Candidate C as his third choice, and Candidate D as his

fourth choice. If Candidate A receives the fewest first-place votes, then he is eliminated,

and the citizen’s vote is transferred to Candidate B. If Candidate B now has the most first-

place votes, then that candidate wins the election.

       31.    Alaska held its primary election on August 28. Peltola received 49.5% of the

vote, Senator Sullivan received 41.4% of the vote, Daniel J. Sullivan received 2.5% of the

vote, and Republican Gerald Heikes received 1.1% of the vote. Those candidates will be

on the ballot in the November general election.

                                  CLAIM FOR RELIEF

                                       Count One

       35.    The United States incorporates all allegations stated above.

       36.    “The Attorney General may bring a civil action . . . for such declaratory or

injunctive relief as is necessary to carry out” Chapter 205 of Title 52 of the U.S. Code,

which covers the NVRA.

       37.    The NVRA imposes a variety of duties on States for the purpose of

“protect[ing] the integrity of the electoral process.” 52 U.S.C. § 20501(b)(3); see id. §§

20503-20509.

       38.    The Supreme Court has long recognized that an important aspect of

safeguarding the “integrity of [a state’s] political processes” is keeping “frivolous . . .

candidacies” from the ballot. Bullock, 405 U.S. at 145; see Lindsay v. Bowen, 750 F.3d

1061, 1064 (9th Cir. 2014) (explaining that a contrary rule would require a state to let

ineligible candidates “clutter and confuse our electoral ballot”).

       39.    To be sure, Bullock involved including or excluding candidates from the

ballot. 405 U.S. at 145. However, the State was “understandably and properly seek[ing]

to prevent the clogging of its election machinery, avoid voter confusion, and assure that the

winner is the choice of a majority, or at least a strong plurality, of those voting, without the

expense and burden of runoff elections.” Id. When the ballot contains a frivolous

candidate, the same state interests remain after ballots have been cast through certification

so as to assure that the State’s “election machinery” is not “clogg[ed]” by frivolous

to “assure that the winner [of the Alaska Senate seat] is the choice of a majority, or at least

a strong plurality, of those voting,” id., is to ensure that the certification is not impacted by

Daniel J. Sullivan’s frivolous candidacy.

       40.    Defendant Beecher has already made the determination, in her capacity as

Director of the Division of Elections, that Daniel J. Sullivan’s candidacy is frivolous in that

his “declaration of candidacy . . . was not filed in order to declare an actual good-faith

candidacy for the office of the United States Senator, but was instead filed with a purpose

to confuse or mislead and to thereby compromise the ballot’s fairness or neutrality.” Ex.

A, at 1. That determination was reached after considering the evidence before her as she

thoroughly laid out in Exhibit A, at 1-2.

       41.    Given the substantial likelihood that the inclusion of Daniel J. Sullivan will

confuse or mislead voters looking to cast a vote for Senator Sullivan, and thus distort the

will of Alaskan voters, there is a substantial likelihood that a certification of the election

will reflect an electoral process that lacked integrity.

                                  PRAYER FOR RELIEF

          WHEREFORE, the United States requests that this Court grant the following
relief:

          43.   Declare that certification of any result that may be impacted by voter

confusion, as defined above, would violate Defendants’ duties under the NVRA to “protect

the integrity of the electoral process.” 52 U.S.C. § 20501(b)(3).

          44.   Defendants from certifying any result that may be impacted by voter

confusion, as defined above, pending an investigation to determine whether votes cast for

Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan and any further procedures

or relief this Court deems necessary and appropriate to determine what the result would

have been absent Daniel J. Sullivan’s inclusion on the ballot.

          45.   Award any other relief it deems just and proper.
