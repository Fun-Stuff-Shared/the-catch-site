# WEDGEWOOD POLLING PUBLIC STATEMENT

WEDGEWOOD POLLING, an experimental project to demonstrate the vulnerabilities, lack of oversight, and overall low barrier to entry in the modern political polling industry, makes this public statement.

# Why We Did This

Having previously worked in the polling and research sector, we watched as the public's trust in polling eroded alongside a rise in unverified, low-quality methodology. We wanted to test a simple hypothesis: How easy is it for an entirely fabricated polling firm to gain traction, be taken seriously, and expose how little scrutinization exists in media and public commentary?

The short answer: Far too easy.

# How Far It Went

Despite having no history, formal credentials, or real data behind the vast majority of our releases, Wedgewood Polling was added to major polling aggregators, including The New York Times, FiftyPlusOne, and VoteHub, and repeatedly treated as a legitimate data source.

Our fabricated numbers were given instant credibility across national media platforms—including being cited on air and in reporting by outlets such as MS NOW. The ease with which unverified figures were absorbed into mainstream political narratives highlights a systemic breakdown in media vetting and data aggregation.

# The Scope of the Project

- The Experiment: Most data published under the Wedgewood Polling name was generated to test whether media outlets, aggregators, and commentators would report on data without verifying the source, methodology, or underlying firm legitimacy.
- The Exception: Out of curiosity and for our own internal baseline, we did conduct one fully legitimate, crowdfunded Nebraska poll during this run. That single poll utilized standard, rigorous methodology to give us a real baseline to compare against synthetic data.

# Moving Forward

Wedgewood Polling was never intended to sway an election or permanently mislead the public; it was designed as a wake-up call. If a fictional entity run by a small group of industry veterans can be integrated into major polling averages and cited by major news organizations, the standards for how polling data is vetted, aggregated, and reported must change.

We hope this experiment sparks a necessary conversation about data integrity, media literacy, and accountability across the political research landscape.
