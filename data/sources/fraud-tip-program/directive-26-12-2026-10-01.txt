To:          All Fraud Division Personnel

Re:          Directive 26-12: Corporate Enforcement in the Fight Against Fraud

       The Fraud Division is committed to combating the ongoing and rampant fraud
against the United States and American taxpayer dollars. To carry out this fight,
Division prosecutors will take an aggressive, all-tools approach to investigating and
prosecuting our health care, government, tax, and trade fraud priorities. This broad-
based enforcement strategy is critical to protecting and furthering American interests
and holding fraudsters fully accountable for their crimes.

       Today, I am issuing the following directives to ensure that the Fraud Division’s
corporate enforcement work efficiently and fairly advances our mission, provides
transparency to stakeholders, appropriately incentivizes the disclosure of
misconduct, rewards those who cooperate with the government, holds individuals and
corporate criminals accountable, and promotes the recovery of fraudulent proceeds
for the American people.

   I. Principles of Corporate Enforcement

       The Department of Justice has a long history of prosecuting companies for
fraud committed by their employees, particularly when a company’s wrongdoing is
serious and pervasive, and its compliance policies, internal controls, and history of
misconduct, among other factors, establish that the company caused or encouraged
the offense. An effective corporate enforcement practice is a necessary component to
advancing the mission of the Department, because it holds criminal entities
accountable, furthers individual prosecutions, promotes a culture of compliance and
integrity, and protects and fosters honest and good-faith markets and institutions. At
the same time, the Department must protect law-abiding companies and

       The Fraud Division’s corporate enforcement program will be finely tuned to
meet these objectives. We will zealously prosecute corporate actors that defraud
taxpayers and the United States of America. And while we aggressively target these
bad corporate actors, we will also firmly guard against overbroad corporate
enforcement—rightly dividing between shades of corporate malfeasance—lest we
interfere with legitimate business operations.

      To discharge these principles, Fraud Division prosecutors shall follow the
Department’s policies concerning corporate enforcement, including the Principles of
Federal Prosecution of Business Organizations and the Department-wide Corporate
Enforcement and Voluntary Self-Disclosure Policy (CEP). See, e.g., Justice Manual
(JM) § 9-28.000.1 Fraud Division prosecutors should familiarize themselves with the
Department’s policies regarding the prosecution of business organizations before
opening or joining any corporate investigation or prosecution.

   II. The Fraud Division’s Corporate Enforcement Section

       The Division’s anti-fraud work covers a wide range of industries and white-
collar offenses. With prosecutors deployed across the country, working closely with
our U.S. Attorney partners, the Division will consistently and fairly apply the
Department’s policies to all corporate matters, while also accounting for important
differences in subject matter areas such as health care, government, tax, and trade
when determining an appropriate course of action in a corporate case.

        Earlier this year, I announced the creation of the Division’s Corporate
Enforcement Section to lead the Division’s work to ensure the consistent, fair, and
successful prosecution of corporate crime across the Division’s entire fraud portfolio.
The Corporate Enforcement Section shall actively support our other sections,
counseling, litigating, negotiating, and otherwise driving success for our Division in
our corporate enforcement efforts. Accordingly, Fraud Division prosecutors shall
work closely with the Corporate Enforcement Section at all phases of corporate
investigations, from case intake through completion of any corporate resolution or
litigation. This approach will maximize efficiency, consistency, and results in the
Division’s corporate enforcement portfolio, leveraging the unique toolbox of our
corporate enforcement specialists.

To start, within seven days of the issuance of this directive, Fraud Division

       The Corporate Enforcement Section will also have primary responsibility for

resolution. In particular, the Corporate Enforcement Section will lead the Division’s
work evaluating a company’s efforts to implement or enhance its compliance
program, ensuring adequate reporting under any disclosure obligations, and
responding to other issues that may arise during the term of the agreement. This
division of labor will free up resources in the prosecuting sections to pursue additional
individual and corporate cases and allow corporate enforcement experts to assess
compliance with corporate resolutions consistently across the Division.

   III. Fraud Division Corporate Investigations and Enforcement Actions

       The Principles of Federal Prosecution of Business Organizations sets forth a
non-exhaustive list of factors to be considered when deciding how to treat a
corporation throughout the lifecycle of a corporate case. See JM § 9-28.300. To
promote transparency and ensure Fraud Division prosecutors are appropriately and
efficiently advancing our corporate enforcement mission, I am directing the following.

      In opening and conducting a corporate investigation, Fraud Division
prosecutors should prioritize:

      1. Fraud schemes involving the health care industry, including health care
         fraud, distribution of controlled substances, and violations of the Federal
         Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act;
      2. Fraud schemes involving the public trust or financial integrity of
         Americans and markets related to procurement, government contracts, and
         other government functions;
      3. Fraud schemes involving significant evasion of internal or external
         revenue; and

       When recommending appropriate outcomes in corporate matters, Division

to bring charges and negotiating plea or other agreements:

      1. Knowledge of or involvement in fraud scheme by corporate management;
      2. Efforts to conceal fraud from government agencies or auditors or otherwise

      3. Conduct that furthers the scheme lasting three years or more;
      4. Actions that threaten the safety or security of Americans, including

      5. Conduct that causes substantial financial hardship to a taxpayer funded

      6. Conduct that affects multiple taxpayer funded programs or government

      7. Conduct that affects three federal districts or more;
      8. Conduct that results in financial harm to twenty-five or more victims or $25
          million or more in loss;
      9. Conduct that involves the exfiltration of American dollars to support
          foreign adversaries; and
      10. Conduct that involves immigration offenses.

       These factors are a non-exhaustive list of considerations for prosecutors when
applying the Principles of Federal Prosecution of Business Organizations to their
cases. A prosecutor may consider any other relevant factor, consistent with the
Justice Manual, and place appropriate weight on such factor in deciding a course of
action in a corporate case. 3 In all circumstances, Fraud Division prosecutors shall
also follow and implement the CEP.

   IV. Promoting Disclosure of Misconduct to the Department

      Leveraging an infusion of resources, state-of-the-art technology, and data
analytics through the National Fraud Detection Center and partner components, the
Fraud Division is proactively generating leads and opening new individual and
corporate fraud investigations at a rapid pace. Such efforts to root out fraud can be
aided by the assistance of individuals and companies who seek to do the right thing
and bring criminal fraud out from the shadows. Indeed, the Department has
