QUESTION PRESENTED
    Thirty years ago, Congress enacted the Illegal
Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility
Act of 1996 (“IIRIRA”), Pub. L. 104–208, 110 Stat.
3009-546, with distinct detention provisions for
individuals in different circumstances. One section, 8
U.S.C. § 1226(a), governs detention during removal
proceedings for noncitizens who entered the country
and allows for their release on bond or conditions. A
different detention provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1225(b)(2)(A),
imposes mandatory detention without the possibility
of bond for noncitizens who have not yet entered the
country and are “seeking admission” into the United
States.
    The question presented is whether the court of
appeals correctly determined that Respondent—a
long-time resident who entered the country years ago
and was arrested inside the United States—is subject
to the detention and bond regime under § 1226(a), and
not mandatory detention under § 1225(b)(2)(A).

INTRODUCTION
     This case concerns the government’s recent
radical reinterpretation of the Immigration and
Nationality Act (“INA”) to require detention without
the possibility of bond of Respondent Ricardo
Aparecido Barbosa da Cunha, a long-time resident
who entered the United States without inspection
years ago and has since established a life and family
here. For nearly three decades after the enactment of
the current version of the INA, the government
interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1226(a) to permit noncitizens
like Mr. Barbosa da Cunha to be released on bond
while their removal proceedings are pending. But in
2025, the government suddenly reversed its
interpretation, asserting that the immigration laws
require detaining such noncitizens without a bond
hearing under a different statute, 8 U.S.C.
§ 1225(b)(2). The government then arrested Mr.
Barbosa da Cunha—who had made himself known to
the government because he voluntarily came forward
to file an asylum application—and kept him in custody
with no opportunity for bond.
    At no point has the government claimed that Mr.
Barbosa da Cunha poses a flight risk or danger. After
the district court ordered a bond hearing, an
immigration judge found that he presented neither a
danger nor a flight risk and ordered his release.
Instead, the government’s sole justification for Mr.
Barbosa da Cunha’s detention is its novel and
countertextual reading of the INA, which no
presidential administration or court adopted before
2025: that § 1225(b)(2)(A), mandates Mr. Barbosa da
Cunha’s detention even though that provision applies

             STATEMENT OF THE CASE
I.   STATUTORY BACKGROUND
     A. The Immigration Detention Statutes
     This case concerns the relationship between two
civil immigration detention statutes that Congress
enacted simultaneously in 1996 as part of a
comprehensive overhaul of the immigration laws: 8
U.S.C. § 1226 and § 1225(b)(2)(A). See IIRIRA, Pub. L.
No. 104-208, 110 Stat. 3009 (1996).
    Section 1226.    Section 1226 governs the
detention of individuals who have effected an entry
into the United States and are “present in the
country” pending their removal proceedings. Jennings
v. Rodriguez, 583 U.S. 281, 288 (2018). The default
rule provides that “[o]n a warrant issued by the
Attorney General, 2 an alien may be arrested and
detained pending a decision on whether the alien is to
be removed from the United States” and allows their
release on bond. 8 U.S.C. § 1226(a).3 By regulation,
noncitizens may seek a bond hearing before an
immigration judge, who makes individualized


2 Congress transferred this authority from the Attorney General

to the Secretary of Homeland Security following relocation of
immigration enforcement functions to the Department of
Homeland Security in 2002 and 2003. See 6 U.S.C. § 557; 8 U.S.C.
§ 1003(a)(1).
3 A separate provision of the Act provides for warrantless arrests

in certain circumstances. See 8 U.S.C. § 1357(a)(2).


                                4

determinations of flight risk and danger. See 8 C.F.R.
§§ 1003.19(a), (h)(2), 1236.1(c)(8), (d).
    Some noncitizens are not eligible for release under
§ 1226(a)’s default rule. See 8 U.S.C. § 1226(a)
(providing for detention and release “[e]xcept as
provided in subsection (c)”). Section 1226(c), as
enacted in IIRIRA, provides that four enumerated
categories of noncitizens “shall” be detained pending
removal proceedings and prohibits their release on
bond. These are individuals who fall under certain
criminal and national security grounds. See 8 U.S.C.
§ 1226(c)(1)(A)–(D).
    In 2025, Congress passed the Laken Riley Act,
Pub. L. 119-1, 139 Stat. 3 (2025), which amended
§ 1226(c) to mandate detention for an additional
category of individuals who are inadmissible,
including for being present without admission, and
have other criminal history. See 8 U.S.C.
§ 1226(c)(1)(E).
    Section 1225.      In IIRIRA, Congress also
enacted § 1225, a statute governing inspection of
noncitizens and a new “expedited removal” procedure.
Section 1225(a)(1) provides that a noncitizen “present
in the United States who has not been admitted or
who arrives in the United States . . . shall be deemed”
an “applicant for admission.” Id. § 1225(a)(1).
     Section 1225 also includes provisions for
detention that are entirely distinct from § 1226.
Paragraph 1225(b)(1) authorizes the mandatory
detention of noncitizens placed into expedited removal
proceedings—“arriving” noncitizens as well as certain
other applicants for admission. Id. § 1225(b)(1)(A)(i),
(b)(1)(A)(iii), (b)(1)(B)(ii)    &     (b)(1)(B)(iii)(IV).

Specifically, the Executive has the option of applying
expedited removal to noncitizens present in the
country without admission who have been present for
less than two years. See id. § 1225(b)(1)(A)(iii). As the
name suggests, expedited removal proceedings are
designed to summarily remove certain noncitizens
who lack documentation or seek an immigration
benefit through fraud or misrepresentation, and who
have been present for limited time periods. See also
id. §§ 1182(a)(6)(C), 1182(a)(7) (setting forth
inadmissibility grounds that may trigger expedited
removal).
     The detention provision at issue here,
§ 1225(b)(2)(A), applies to “other” applicants for
admission who are “seeking admission” to the country,
id., and are not subject to expedited removal, see id.
§ 1225(b)(2)(B)(ii) (stating that § 1225(b)(2)(A) “shall
not apply to an alien to whom paragraph [1225(b)](1)
applies”). This includes a number of categories, such
as noncitizens who present at ports of entry and are
charged with inadmissibility on criminal grounds.
Section 1225(b)(2)(A) provides that:
    in the case of an alien who is an applicant for
    admission, if the examining immigration
    officer determines that an alien seeking
    admission is not clearly and beyond a doubt
    entitled to be admitted, the alien shall be
    detained for a [removal] proceeding under
    section 1229a of this title.
Noncitizens subject to detention under § 1225(b)—
either (b)(1) or (b)(2)—have no access to bond. Instead,
the only option for release is through a discretionary
grant of parole by the Department of Homeland

    B. Congress’s Choice in IIRIRA to Preserve
       the Availability of Bond Generally for
       Noncitizens in the United States
     In IIRIRA’s detention statutes, Congress largely
preserved the preexisting detention scheme:
Noncitizens arrested inside the country, including
those who had entered without admission, remained
eligible for release on bond under § 1226(a). Those
apprehended at the border continued to be detained
without possibility of bond under § 1225(b)(2)(A).
     Congress maintained this detention scheme,
treating those present in the United States differently
from those apprehended near the border, even as
IIRIRA made changes to the removal process. Before
IIRIRA, the immigration statute tracked a bifurcated
system for removing noncitizens: “Deportation”
applied to everyone placed in proceedings within the
United States—both those who had entered lawfully
but were subject to removal (e.g., due to criminal
convictions) and those who had entered the United
States without inspection and were later
apprehended. See 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1) (1995).
“Exclusion” proceedings applied to people encountered
at the border during the inspection process, but who
had not yet “enter[ed]” the country. See id. §§ 1225(a)–
(b) (1995), 1226(a) (1995). For people in deportation
proceedings, the statute authorized but did not
require arrest and detention “[p]ending a

                           7

---

<!-- page 16 -->
determination of deportability” and permitted release
on bond. Id. § 1252(a)(1) (1995). But for noncitizens
apprehended at the border and placed in exclusion
proceedings, the statute mandated detention without
bond. Id. § 1225(b) (1995).
   IIRIRA changed the removal process in some
important ways:
    x   IIRIRA eliminated the bifurcated system of
        deportation and exclusion, combining them
        into a single “removal” procedure that applies
        to everyone, whether they have previously
        entered the United States or were stopped at
        the border. See IIRIRA § 304, 110 Stat. at
        3009-587–93; see also 8 U.S.C. § 1229a.
   x    IIRIRA also made grounds of removal depend
        not on physical entry into the United States,
        but instead on “admission,” defined as a
        “lawful entry” after inspection by an
        immigration officer. 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A).
        Now, a noncitizen who has been “admitted” to
        the country is “deportable,” see id.
        § 1229a(e)(2)(B), whereas someone who
        entered the country without inspection and
        has not been admitted or paroled is
        “inadmissible,” see id. § 1229a(e)(2)(A). See
        also id. §§ 1182, 1227.
    x   IIRIRA also “deemed” certain categories of
        noncitizens “applicant[s] for admission,”
        including noncitizens who are “present in the
        United States [without having] been
        admitted” as well as noncitizens who “arrive[]
        in the United States” either at or outside a
        “designated port of arrival.” Id. § 1225(a)(1).

This put noncitizens who entered without
        inspection on the same footing in removal
        proceedings as other “applicants for
        admission,” confirming that they are both
        subject to inadmissibility and thus bear the
        burden of showing that they are “entitled to
        be admitted and [are] not inadmissible.” Id.
        § 1229a(c)(2)(A).
     Even as Congress made these changes, it retained
the core of the pre-IIRIRA detention scheme. Congress
made § 1226—the new detention statute governing
individuals apprehended inside the country—
applicable to both inadmissible and deportable
noncitizens, rather than just deportable noncitizens.
Compare 8 U.S.C. § 1226(a) (authorizing detention
and bond “pending a decision on whether the alien is
to be removed,” (emphasis added)), with id.
§ 1252(a)(1) (1995) (authorizing detention and bond
“[p]ending a determination of deportability”). In this
way, the new § 1226 “restate[d]” the provisions in the
INA that previously authorized “release on bond” for
individuals who entered without inspection. H.R. Rep.
No. 104-828, at 210 (1996) (Conf. Rep.); see also H.R.
Rep. No. 104-469, pt. 1, at 229 (1996) (same). And in
§ 1225(b)(2)(A), Congress retained the prior rule of
detaining, without possibility of bond, individuals who
are “seeking admission” at the border and are
determined      to    be   inadmissible.     8   U.S.C.
§ 1225(b)(2)(A).
    Congress enacted these detention provisions
against the backdrop of the long-established
constitutional distinction between noncitizens who
have entered the United States (even if without
authorization), and noncitizens stopped at the border

                          9

---

<!-- page 18 -->
seeking to enter. See, e.g., Shaughnessy v. United
States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 212 (1953) (“[A]liens
who have once passed through our gates, even
illegally, may be expelled only after proceedings
conforming to traditional standards of fairness
encompassed in due process of law.”).

    C. IIRIRA and Mandatory Detention
    IIRIRA also subjected certain groups of
noncitizens inside the country to mandatory
detention, without a hearing to assess flight risk and
danger. But Congress did so surgically, requiring the
Executive to use limited detention resources on
targeted classes of noncitizens, including recent
entrants who are subject to the new expedited removal
proceedings, 8 U.S.C. § 1225(b)(1), and certain
noncitizens with final orders of removal awaiting
deportation, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(2).
    Most importantly, Congress enacted § 1226(c) as
a “special rule for aliens who have committed certain
dangerous crimes and those who have connections to
terrorism.” Nielsen v. Preap, 586 U.S. 392, 396 (2019).
The provision required detention pending removal
proceedings of “a limited class” of noncitizens, Demore
v. Kim, 538 U.S. 510, 518 (2003), whom Congress
found to pose a “heightened risk” of flight or danger.
Preap, 586 U.S. at 398; see also Demore, 538 U.S. at
513, 518–21 (discussing Congress’s findings of bail
risk). These include both inadmissible and deportable
noncitizens. 8 U.S.C. § 1226(c)(1).
    To account for § 1226(c)’s anticipated new
demands on detention capacity, Congress enacted a
safety-valve provision, which allowed the Attorney
General to delay implementing new § 1226(c) for up to

                           10

---

<!-- page 19 -->
two years while the Executive Branch added more
detention beds. See H.R. Rep. No. 104-469, pt. 1, at
120, 123, 207; IIRIRA § 386(a), 110 Stat. at 3009-653.
Congress estimated that § 1226(c)’s new detention
mandate would require the detention of 45,000
noncitizens subject to removal on criminal grounds
each year. See H.R. Rep. No. 104-469, pt. 1, at 118,
120, 123. The Executive Branch promptly invoked the
safety-valve provision, citing lack of detention
resources. 4 Congress also directed the agency to add
500 beds to the existing 8,500-bed system. IIRIRA
§ 386(a), 110 Stat. at 3009-653.

§ 1225(b)(2)(A)’s detention mandate warranted any
implementation delay or increase in detention
capacity, as it did for § 1226(c). This was so even

III. THE GOVERNMENT’S REVERSAL
    In the summer of 2025, the government adopted a
novel interpretation of the detention statutes. DHS, in
coordination with the Department of Justice (“DOJ”),
“determined” in an unpublished guidance document,
without reasoning or explanation, “that section [1225]
of the [INA], rather than section [1226], is the
applicable immigration detention authority for all
applicants for admission,” including anyone who

entered without inspection. 5 DHS instructed its
officers to subject any noncitizen present in the United
States without being admitted or paroled to
mandatory        detention     under    § 1225(b)(2)(A),
explaining that these “applicants for admission”
would now be “treated in the same manner that
‘arriving aliens’ have historically been treated.” 6
Three months later, the BIA adopted DHS’s new
interpretation in a published decision, Matter of
Yajure Hurtado, 29 I. & N. Dec. 216 (BIA 2025).

§ 1225(b)(2)(A) and deny bond hearings not only at the
border, but also to noncitizens who entered without
admission apprehended anywhere within the United
States, regardless of how long they have lived here.
Millions of noncitizens could be affected by the
government’s new interpretation. 7

that practice.” Pet. App. 10a. In particular, the court

  REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION
    The government asks this Court to hold this case
for Raycraft v. Lopez-Campos. Cert. Pet. 11–13. But
the Court should instead grant review here or,
alternatively, in the pending petition in Buenrostro-
Mendez v. Blanche, No. 26-43 (cert. pet. filed July 2,
2026). For the same reasons discussed in the petition
in Buenrostro-Mendez, either this case or Buenrostro-
Mendez presents the best vehicle for resolving the

statutory question presented because both squarely
present the issue and do not raise a separate
constitutional issue that may never need to be decided
by this Court. Lopez-Campos, by contrast, adds a
separate due process question that involves no circuit
split, and that this Court need not reach if Respondent
prevails on his statutory arguments.
    1. The parties agree that the statutory question
of which detention statute governs noncitizens like
Respondent warrants this Court’s review. The
statutory question has produced a 7-2 circuit split and
is exceptionally important and ready for review.
Supra at 16–17.
    2. The separate due process question presented
in Lopez-Campos, however, is not independently
certworthy. Unlike the statutory issue, the due
process question has produced no circuit split, and no
circuit other than the Sixth Circuit has held that
applying    § 1225(b)(2)(A)   to   noncitizens     like
Respondent violates due process. Furthermore, if
Respondent here or the petitioners in Buenrostro-
Mendez prevail on the statute, there will be no need to
decide the due process question at all.
    The constitutional question in Lopez-Campos is
also unsuitable for review because it substantially
overlaps with the due process question already before
the Court in Genalo v. Black, No. 25-886 (cert. granted
June 15, 2026). Genalo concerns an as-applied due
process challenge to mandatory detention under
another provision of the INA, § 1226(c). The Court’s
decision there likely will inform any constitutional
question that remains if the government ultimately
prevails on the statutory issue here. For instance, in
