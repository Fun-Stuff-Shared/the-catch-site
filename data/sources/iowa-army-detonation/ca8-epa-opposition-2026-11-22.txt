                                 INTRODUCTION

      Sixty-eight drums of degraded lead azide, a primary explosive used in

detonators, sit in two earth-covered magazines at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant

(the “Facility”). They could explode at any time. The lead azide has been stored in

drums for decades and has recrystallized. It is now so sensitive to temperature,

friction and shock that it cannot be safely moved, opened, or treated without a

significant risk of explosion. And the risk of explosion only increases as winter

weather arrives. To address this imminent and substantial endangerment, EPA

issued an emergency permit authorizing the Army to conduct a controlled detonation

(“the Permit”).    Army munitions safety experts determined that controlled

detonation is the only safe way to treat the waste, because every alternative requires

disturbing material that may explode if disturbed.

      The choice therefore is not between a detonation and no detonation. It is

between a planned detonation (timed for favorable weather, preceded by public

notice, and followed by monitoring and cleanup) and an unplanned explosion that

could come at any moment, with no warning and no safeguards. The United States

recognizes that the controlled detonation will release lead dust. But a controlled

detonation is the only way to minimize the release. The Permit imposes numerous

conditions to mitigate harm, including that the Army detonate only in favorable

weather conditions to reduce dispersion of lead dust, run conservative modeling of



                                          1
   Appellate Case: 26-3374    Page: 3    Date Filed: 11/22/2026 Entry ID: 5708599
the projected dispersion, provide public notice, monitor, maintain records, and

complete necessary cleanup. An uncontrolled explosion comes with none of these

protections.

      Moreover, a permit is not even necessary for the Army to take emergency

action against an immediate threat from explosive material. See 40 C.F.R. §

270.1(c)(3)(i)(D).   The Army’s qualified explosives emergency specialist has

determined that such a threat exists here. Col. Aaron C. Teller Decl. ¶¶ 8, 30. Even

so, the Permit adds protections the public deserves.

      Petitioners do not challenge EPA’s finding that the lead azide waste poses an

imminent and substantial endangerment. Yet they ask this Court to stay the Permit

so that EPA can revisit alternative options. But the Army already has conducted that

alternatives analysis, and its munitions experts concluded that there is no other safer

option. Petitioners’ proposed delay would leave an explosion hazard in place, with

no end date as Iowa winter arrives. And the additional permit conditions Petitioners

propose ignore the reality that some lead dust is unavoidable. The prevailing

question is whether its release is controlled.

      Petitioners have failed to meet their burden to prove a stay is warranted. They

are not likely to prevail on the merits, and the equities weigh heavily against a stay.

An uncontrolled explosion would inflict greater irreparable harm on the public than




                                           2
   Appellate Case: 26-3374     Page: 4    Date Filed: 11/22/2026 Entry ID: 5708599
a controlled detonation. Thus, the Court should lift the administrative stay, deny the

stay, and permit the detonation to proceed under the Permit.

                                 BACKGROUND

I.      Legal Background

        RCRA provides for regulation of solid and hazardous waste, from generation

to disposal. See 42 U.S.C. § 6901 et seq.; City of Chicago v. Env’t Def. Fund, 511

U.S. 328, 331-32 (1994). RCRA Subtitle C governs hazardous waste as defined

under RCRA and EPA’s regulations. See 42 U.S.C. §§ 6921-39g; 40 C.F.R. pts.

260-279.

        Hazardous treatment, storage, or disposal (“TSD”) facilities are subject to

regulation under RCRA Subtitle C. See 42 U.S.C. § 6924(a). Owners and operators

of TSD facilities generally must have a RCRA permit to operate. Id. § 6925(a). To

issue a permit, the permitting authority–in this case, EPA–typically follows a notice

and comment process under EPA’s regulations. See 40 C.F.R. pt. 124.

        But EPA’s regulations also authorize EPA to issue emergency permits to

address an imminent and substantial endangerment to human health or the

environment. Id. § 270.61(a). Emergency permits are time limited, specify the

covered waste and manner of treatment, require a public notice upon issuance, and

“incorporate, to the extent possible and not inconsistent with the emergency

situation, all applicable requirements” for RCRA permits and TSD regulations. Id.



                                          3
     Appellate Case: 26-3374   Page: 5   Date Filed: 11/22/2026 Entry ID: 5708599
§ 270.61(b). And for the most urgent situations, EPA’s regulations do not require a

permit for

      treatment or containment activities taken during immediate response to
      . . . [a]n immediate threat to human health, public safety, property, or
      the environment from the known or suspected presence of military
      munitions, other explosive material, or an explosive device, as
      determined by an explosive or munitions emergency response
      specialist[.]

Id. §270.1(c)(3)(i)(D).

      In issuing a standard RCRA TSD permit, the permitting authority determines

whether the TSD facility complies with RCRA’s TSD regulatory standards. 42

U.S.C. § 6925(c)(1).      RCRA regulations, through permits, impose generally

applicable requirements on all TSD units, like facility standards, preparedness and

prevention, and closure and post-closure care. See 40 C.F.R. pt. 264, subpts. A-H.

There are also unit-specific regulations for particular categories of units, like

containers, landfills, or incinerators. See id. pt 264, subpts. I-X. The unit in this

case is classified as a “miscellaneous unit,” a unit that does not fall into one of the

specified categories. See id. §§ 264.601-.603. Permits for miscellaneous units “are

to contain such terms and provisions as necessary to protect human health and the

environment.” Id. § 264.601.

      In sum, EPA’s regulations scale the process to the danger: standard permits

for routine operations, emergency permits for imminent and substantial

endangerments, and no permit at all for immediate responses to explosive threats.

II.      Factual Background

         The Facility is a military installation in Middletown, Iowa, that loads,

assembles, and packages medium- and large-caliber munitions. See Emergency

Hazardous Waste Management Facility Permit for Storage and Treatment of Lead

Azide, EPA RCRA ID No. IA7213820445 (the “Permit”) at 1, Ex. 1. The Facility

operates under a 2018 RCRA TSD permit. Id. In March 2026, the Army identified

a stockpile of 68 drums in two earth-covered magazines that contained significantly

degraded and unstable lead azide. Emergency Permit Approval Memorandum

(“Approval Memorandum”) at 1, Ex. 2. Lead azide poses an explosion hazard when

it deteriorates. Borman Letter, dated Aug. 12, 2026 (“First Army Letter”), Ex. 3.

Those risks increase in cold weather because the lead azide crystalizes, which

increases the sensitivity of the lead azide—leading to an increased risk of an

unintended explosion. Approval Memorandum at 2. In this condition, disruption

like friction or movement poses an unacceptable risk of an uncontrolled explosion.

Id.; First Army Letter. The drums cannot even be opened. Crystal growth inside the

barrels and around the lids makes the lids unsafe to remove, so the water-ethanol

solution that keeps the material stable has not been checked or replenished since

September 2025, and evaporation has left it more prone to freezing. Teller Decl. ¶¶

11–13.

                                    ARGUMENT

I.      Even if the Court stays the Permit, the Army is not precluded from
        taking requisite emergency action.

        There is an immediate risk that the lead azide at the Facility will

spontaneously explode. Teller Decl. The waste has deteriorated to a degree that it

is extremely sensitive to friction from crystal formation. Id. ¶ 9. The barrels holding

the waste cannot be removed due to the risk of explosion, preventing the Army from

taking measures to reduce the risk of explosion. Id. ¶¶ 12-13. As the temperatures

drop, the risk of an uncontrolled explosion increases because the formed crystals can

break more easily in freezing temperatures. Id. ¶ 13. If the crystals break, this could

cause a chain reaction detonation that could cause an uncontrolled dispersal of lead

azide into the environment. Id. ¶¶ 15, 17. Temperatures have already dropped below

30 degrees Fahrenheit, with temperature swings as low as 24 degrees and high as 70

degrees. Id. ¶¶ 28-29. These temperature swings have dramatically increased the

      Under these circumstances, EPA’s RCRA regulations provide an exemption

from both substantive and permitting requirements to allow immediate action. The

RCRA regulations provide that the substantive standards “do not apply to”:

      a person engaged in treatment or containment activities during
      immediate response to . . . [a]n immediate threat to human health,
      public safety, property, or the environment, from the known or
      suspected presence of military munitions, other explosive material, or
      an explosive device, as determined by an explosive or munitions

at 40 C.F.R. § 270.1(c)(3)(i)(D).

      Here, an explosive or munitions emergency specialist has made the requisite

determination. Teller Decl. ¶ 3. Thus, EPA’s RCRA regulations allow the Army to

conduct a controlled detonation of the lead azide at the Facility even if the Permit

that is at issue here is not in effect. Any other options require handling the lead azide

in a way that poses an unacceptable risk of an uncontrolled explosion. See id. ¶¶ 6-

17. A controlled detonation would minimize the risk associated with the resulting

explosion. See id. ¶¶ 16-25. Thus, a controlled detonation is necessary now to

respond to the immediate threat of an uncontrolled explosion at the Facility. EPA’s

regulations allow the Army’s planned action without the need for a permit, and the

Army intends to act on November 24, 2026.

II.      The Court should lift the administrative stay and deny Petitioners’
         stay motion.

         A stay, like a preliminary injunction, “is an extraordinary and drastic remedy,”

and Petitioners have not made a “a clear showing” that one should be granted.

Mazurek v. Armstrong, 520 U.S. 968, 972 (1997). They must demonstrate: (1) a

likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable injury if relief is withheld; (3)

lack of harm to other parties from a stay; and (4) that a stay would serve the public

interest. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The third and fourth criteria

merge here. Id. at 435. A stay is not a matter of right, even if irreparable injury

might otherwise result.” Id. at 433. Rather, courts “pay particular regard for the

public consequences in employing the extraordinary remedy.”              Weinberger v.

Romero-Barcelo, 456 U.S. 305, 312 (1982). Accordingly, courts “must balance the

competing claims of injury and must consider the effect on each party of the granting

or withholding of the requested relief.” Winter v. NRDC, 555 U.S. 7, 24, (2008).

         Petitioners have not made the requisite “clear showing,” Mazurek, 520 U.S.

at 972, that they are “likely” to prevail on the merits. Starbucks Corp. v. McKinney,

602 U.S. 339, 349 (2024) (citation modified). EPA issued the Permit because the

unstable lead azide at the Facility poses an imminent and substantial endangerment.

Petitioners do not challenge that determination. Instead, they ask this Court to

second-guess the agencies’ technical judgments about the specific permit terms

needed to protect human health and the environment during a controlled detonation.


                                            11
      Appellate Case: 26-3374   Page: 13     Date Filed: 11/22/2026 Entry ID: 5708599
The decision to authorize treatment by a controlled detonation was reasonable, and

so were the permit terms to minimize risk. And the equities strongly favor a

controlled, planned detonation over the imminent, uncontrolled one that the stay

invites. Petitioners cannot show that the public will not be harmed if the Permit is

stayed. The Court should deny the motion.

      A.     Petitioners are unlikely to prevail on the merits.

      RCRA imposes a high bar to reverse EPA permitting decisions under the

Administrative Procedure Act standard of review. See, e.g., Mueller v. EPA, 993

F.2d 1354, 1356 (8th Cir. 1993); Greenpeace, Inc. v. Waste Techs. Indus., 9 F.3d

1174, 1180 (6th Cir. 1993). And Petitioners’ burden is even higher when an

agency’s decisions rest on highly technical judgments. See Missouri ex rel. Bailey

v. U.S. Dep’t of Interior, 73 F.4th 570, 581 (8th Cir. 2023). Plaintiffs have not made

a clear showing they are likely to succeed.

             1.    EPA reasonably authorized a controlled detonation to
                   avert a more damaging uncontrolled explosion.

      EPA reasonably determined that controlled detonation of the lead azide waste

was necessary based on the imminent and substantial endangerment at the Facility.

The crystallized, deteriorated lead azide waste is unstable and can explode

spontaneously. Approval Memorandum at 2; First Army Letter. Army explosive

experts evaluated the waste and determined that a mitigated, controlled detonation

is the only safe treatment option. Approval Letter at 3; First Army Letter. In doing


                                         12
  Appellate Case: 26-3374     Page: 14    Date Filed: 11/22/2026 Entry ID: 5708599
so, the Army considered “the full range of potential alternative for the treatment of

the lead azide,” including in place treatment, thermal treatment, relocation of drums,

and controlled detonation. Second Army Letter. The Army’s technical consensus

was that controlled detonation is the only safe option. Id. Other options that would

disturb the containers, like in-place chemical neutralization, would create an

unacceptable risk of an unintentional detonation. First Army Letter. And as the

weather gets colder, the risk of unintentional detonation increases as crystal

formation increases. Approval Memorandum at 2; First Army Letter.

      EPA’s determination that the controlled detonation was necessary to protect

human health and the environment was reasonable because it is more protective than

allowing the waste to unintentionally explode. In a controlled explosion, the Army

accounts for weather conditions that limit the spread of lead dust.         Approval

Memorandum at 5-6. For example, the Army can detonate on a day when the wind

is blowing away from population centers and at lower speeds that will reduce the

geographic extent of lead dispersion. Id. Further, during a controlled detonation,

the Army can ensure complete treatment of the waste. As EPA explained, “[t]he

possibility of spontaneous detonation may result in greater harm to human health

because the conditions of detonation would not be controlled to mitigate its impact.”

Approval Memorandum at 3. It was reasonable for EPA to give weight to the

Army’s munitions experts. And that is especially true where qualifying Army

      Petitioners theorize that treatment alternatives other than controlled

      B.     The equities overwhelmingly favor a controlled detonation
             over an uncontrolled one.

      The United States acknowledges that a controlled detonation will likely cause

airborne lead. See Mot., Ex. C. But the Permit that Petitioners seek to stay includes

full-scale mitigation measures—from before, during, and after detonation—to

enhance protection of human health and the environment. And the Army intends to

act consistent with the permit conditions regardless of whether the Permit is in effect.

Notice at 5, Dkt. No. 5708596.

      Petitioners’ focus on the lead released by the planned, controlled detonation,

and their request to delay this action, ignores the gravity of the situation facing the

public absent action by the Army. See Mot. at 23-27. As Petitioners do not dispute,

the lead azide has recrystallized and become sensitive to shock and friction. See

Mot. at 3. Even small movements in or around the magazines could set it off.

  Appellate Case: 26-3374     Page: 20     Date Filed: 11/22/2026 Entry ID: 5708599
Particularly with colder temperatures, the water-ethanol mixture surrounding the

lead azide will cycle through freezing and thawing, which in turn will create friction

dipping below freezing. Teller Decl. ¶¶ 13, 27-29.

      Moreover, an unintentional explosion can also result in an incomplete

detonation and thus cause the explosive material to disperse onto neighboring

communities. Id. ¶ 17. In short, the public faces an environmental and health threat

that must be promptly addressed. See id. ¶¶ 8, 12-17, 29-30; see Jim Macy Decl.

¶¶ 14-16; Mot. at 25-26.

      So the question is whether the public would be at greater risk of irreparable

injury from a planned, controlled detonation or from an imminent, uncontrolled

explosion with no public notice or safety measures in place to mitigate the risks from

lead dispersion. See Teller Decl. ¶¶ 18-25 (describing mitigation efforts); Macy

Decl. ¶ 25; cf. Mot. at 27 (stating that EPA’s permitting decision must balance the

risk of an unplanned explosion versus a planned detonation). The answer is not

close. The status quo is not safe; instead, it is the danger.

      Contrary to Petitioners’ assertion that an unplanned explosion is merely

“possibl[e],” Mot. 27, the unfortunate reality is that the risk of a lead azide detonation

is immediate, Teller Decl. ¶¶ 8, 30. Given recent temperature swings and the arrival

of freezing weather, the Army’s explosives emergency specialist has found “an

intolerable likelihood of spontaneous explosion that threatens public safety.” Teller

Decl. ¶¶ 8, 28-30; see Macy Decl. ¶ 15, 25. And because the stabilizing solution has

been evaporating for more than a year, it is more likely to freeze inside the barrels,

where even a small amount of friction can set off an explosive chain reaction. Teller

Decl. ¶¶ 13-14. All the harms Petitioners describe in their stay motion would come

to pass, and worse, if the lead azide detonates on its own. See Mot. at 23-24.

      Under the delay that Petitioners seek with their delay request, the public faces

greater risk of irreparable harm from lead inhalation and contamination, because no

one can know when an uncontrolled explosion will occur. See Macy Decl. ¶ 22. If

Petitioners were to have their way, the public, Army Personnel and EPA would have

no advanced warning that lead may be in the air. See Permit § IV.G.1-.2. And EPA

and the Army would be hindered in their ability to provide the public with close to

real-time air dispersion modeling to inform the public of the potentially impacted

area. See Macy Decl. ¶ 32. Further, an uncontrolled explosion may occur in adverse

meteorological conditions and may cause lead to disperse over a greater geographic

scope or densely populated areas. Approval Memorandum at 5-6; Macy Decl. ¶ 24.

The Army may be unable to implement its complete set of mitigation and control

measures beforehand to minimize lead dispersion. See Macy Decl. ¶ 31 (noting that

the Permit requires the Army to conduct extensive sampling to examine the extent

of contamination produced by the detonation soon after the Army acts).

      And an uncontrolled explosion may result in some lead azide catapulting onto

neighboring properties and detonating there.       Teller Decl. ¶ 17.    Incomplete

destruction would also create “an extremely unsafe condition” for cleanup crews and

leave “an ongoing explosives safety issue in the impacted area.” Id. In short, a stay

trades a managed risk for an unmanaged one.

      Conversely, the Permit and the Army’s planned, controlled detonation will

significantly reduce these risks. See Approval Memorandum at 4. The Permit

identifies weather conditions intended to reduce lead exposure, disperse in less

densely populated areas, and prevent harm to the individuals conducting the

detonation. See Permit §§ IV.D, IV.F, IV.O.3; Approval Memorandum at 5-6. And

before detonating, the Army will take numerous mitigation measures. Teller Decl.

¶¶ 18-25. The Army will provide frequent forecasts of the projected air dispersion

using a model that includes conservative estimates (i.e., the model assumes that

additional safety measures the Army will implement will not reduce lead dispersion)

to update the public on where the lead will likely disperse. See Permit at 3 and §§

IV.G.1.i-ii, at 10; app. B ¶¶ A–B. The Army will wet the area of the planned

detonation to limit the spread of lead in the air. Teller Decl. ¶ 22. Upon detonation,

the Army will sample those impacted areas within 24 hours and remediate properties

adversely affected. Permit at 3 and § IV.U.2. In sum, the Permit and the Army’s

controlled detonation significantly reduces the environmental and public health

harms that may imminently result from a spontaneous explosion.

      Petitioners’ other equitable argument—that there is no public interest in

perpetuating an unlawful agency action—fails because Petitioners are unlikely to

succeed on the merits. See above Arg. I.A. And as Petitioners acknowledge, the

equities often favor protecting the environment. See Mot. at 25 (citing Amoco Prod.

Co. v. Village of Gambell, 480 U.S. 531, 545 (1987)). Here, denying the stay is what

better protects public health and the environment.

      The Army has delayed detonation by three days now. But every day of delay

increases the risk of an uncontrolled explosion that will cause far greater public

health and environmental harm than the planned, controlled detonation initiated

under the protective terms of the Permit.

      Thus, the equities weigh decisively against a stay. And Petitioners are not

likely to prevail on the merits. EPA’s approval of the Permit was reasonable

considering the imminent and substantial endangerment the threat of an uncontrolled

explosion poses. Petitioners have not met their burden to show that the extraordinary

and drastic remedy of a preliminary stay is warranted.
