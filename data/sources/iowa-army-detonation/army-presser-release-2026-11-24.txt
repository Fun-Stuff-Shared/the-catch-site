MIDDLETOWN, Iowa — The U.S. Army, in close coordination with the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms & Explosives (ATF), and state and local emergency management authorities, announced plans today to conduct a controlled, in-place detonation of degraded, crystallized lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, at approximately 9:00 a.m. CST.
During a routine safety inspection, inspectors identified severe material degradation and crystallization affecting approximately 68 drums, totaling more than 9,700 pounds net explosive weight, of stored lead azide inside earth-covered magazines. In this crystallized state, lead azide becomes an ultra-sensitive primary explosive vulnerable to minimal friction, vibration, heat, or shock. This high sensitivity makes any physical manipulation, chemical neutralization, or off-site transport an unacceptably high risk to public safety.
Following an exhaustive multi-agency technical evaluation by the U.S. Army, the ATF, the EPA, the Department of War Explosives Safety Board, the Defense Threat Reduction Agency (DTRA), and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, leadership approved an in-place controlled detonation conducted by military explosive ordnance disposal experts from the 71st Ordnance Group as the only viable method to permanently eliminate the hazard.
"We're here today to express, from the entire federal interagency, that our number one priority here in Iowa today is the health and safety of the citizens of this region," said Adam Telle, Acting Secretary of the Army. "There is consensus across the experts, across the country, that a controlled, mitigated detonation of the lead azide here at Iowa Army Ammunition Plant is far preferable to an uncontrolled, spontaneous detonation of that substance."
"Inaction is no longer an option," said Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley, commanding general, U.S. Army Sustainment Command. "It would be irresponsible for us to not take the determined, consensus-based course of action moving forward that has been selected."
"Lead azide is a very sensitive primary explosive. It's very sensitive to heat, shock, and friction," said Robert Cekada, ATF director. "Everyone that we've spoken to, through their experience, has determined that this is the only safe path forward for not only the surrounding community of Middletown and the surrounding areas, but also the personnel that are tasked with mitigating this threat here on base. Lead azide is a very, very dangerous explosive, and what we're concerned with now, the fluctuation in temperatures is just as dangerous as the freezing temperatures. It's just a matter of time when we have an uncontrolled detonation."
"At the very beginning, we wanted to prevent having to do a high-order detonation, Col. Aaron Teller, group commander, 71st Explosive Ordnance Disposal. However, we were never able to find a viable alternative. I've seen the reporting on the chemical neutralization agent. That proved to be an unsafe method.”
“The barrels of lead azide have deteriorated rapidly over the past eight months. We've observed a significant accumulation of crystallized lead azide on the exterior of the barrels, including the barrel lids. It prevents those barrels from being able to be moved. It prevents us from removing the barrel lids. That's why they can't be treated, and that's why you can't open the lids to treat it with a chemical neutralization agent."
Recommended Community Relocation and Federal Support
Out of an abundance of caution, FEMA is supporting a recommended relocation for all residents of Middletown, Iowa, and surrounding impacted areas within a five-mile cone extending southwest of the plant, commencing today, Tuesday, November 24, 2026. This relocation zone aligns with atmospheric dispersion and ground deposition models developed by DTRA and the EPA, with a one-mile safety buffer.
"Our focus here in the FEMA mission space is to ensure that we coordinate with state and locals, and provide assistance to those who may be impacted," said Cameron Hamilton, administrator of FEMA. "We've made it very clear to those who are within a southwesterly location: if they choose to relocate, we will offer services. The President has been very clear to unlock direct federal assistance in support of this effort. So we are providing transportation services as well as relocation and food and nourishment for those who may choose to relocate out of an abundance of caution.
FEMA is providing comprehensive on-the-ground relocation resources, including FEMA-funded short-term hotel accommodations provided through Friday, November 27, 2026, with flexibility and extensions available upon request, as well as food, water, and emergency transportation for those unable to travel on their own. Affected residents can register online at DisasterAssistance.gov or call the 24/7 FEMA Helpline at 1-800-621-3362.
Residents remaining outside the relocation zone but within the broader advisory area are strongly encouraged to shelter in place. Before the detonation window opens, residents should close all exterior windows and doors, set home heating and air conditioning systems to indoor recirculation mode, turn off exhaust fans, and consider replacing furnace filters with those rated MERV 11 or higher.
Extra care should be taken to protect sensitive populations, including infants, children under six, pregnant women, and those with chronic illnesses, who are more vulnerable to air pollution and lead dust. Following the all-clear announcement, residents should wet-wipe high-contact exterior surfaces such as patios, decks, window sills, and outdoor play equipment to safely capture and remove any settled dust.
Engineering Safeguards and Environmental Compliance
Operating under an EPA-approved Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) emergency treatment permit, the Army is deploying unprecedented physical engineering safeguards. The concrete bunkers are barricaded with sand-filled HESCO barriers standing about two stories high with soil backfill to absorb explosive force and trap debris.
"This is an unprecedented amount of both protective works and mitigation measures," said Teller.
"The EPA has sampling teams and testing teams that are out in the field right now, taking samples before any detonation. They will be there during detonation with air monitoring systems, and then after detonation to all of the places within the affected area that need to be sampled," said Tom Crocci, associate administrator for national security and operations coordination at the EPA. "We're coordinating our sampling efforts with the Army so that we're covering more ground as quickly as possible. And those resources will be available to anybody who wants them.
Precise deployment of aqueous foam blocks, two walls of four 16,000-gallon water tanks, and gel blocks adjacent to the bunkers will absorb thermal energy and reduce airborne particulate emissions directly at the blast source. In addition, an environmentally friendly, PFAS-free suppressing foam is being applied over containment screens and tarps to submerge the structures. High-volume industrial misting units and water sprayers will thoroughly saturate the soil and airspace before and during detonation, ensuring escaping particles are immediately weighed down by water and deposited back onto the soil inside the secure installation footprint.
Extensive threat modeling shows that most of the rising cloud consists of harmless, non-toxic substances, including nitrogen gas, clean sand and soil, and water vapor. A small fraction of the cloud consists of fine lead oxide dust from the original compound. Stationary air sensors will run along post perimeters and in nearby communities during the event, and mobile environmental monitoring teams will immediately chase the plume path post-detonation to collect soil, surface-water, and surface-wipe samples to verify lead levels against baseline models and guide any localized cleanup.
Community Health and Federal Responsibility
"Day-to-day, Army Medicine's job is to build trust with soldiers and keep faith with families. And that was the direction Honorable Telle gave me when he brought us out here to do the same with your community," said Lt. Gen. Mary Izaguirre, the Army Surgeon General. "I'm also the mother of five children. Two of them are soldiers. So we understand the concerns and take them very seriously. We want to ensure that the information that we have, we share with you in your communities, and that we have that method for getting your questions and being here not only during the detonation, but post-detonation and following up throughout the entire mitigation."
"Listen, our message is very simple here. Your health matters. Your concerns deserve answers. And protecting your family is our responsibility," said Sean Kaufman, principal deputy assistant secretary for preparedness and response at the HHS. "We have boots on the ground. We're going to continue to be here. The work doesn't end after the detonation; our responsibility continues to move on. We will continue testing as we move forward."
If any residential property tests above the RCRA-approved standard of 200 parts per million (mg/kg), environmental remediation teams will remove affected soil, clean surfaces, and restore the property at complete government expense. Additionally, if any resident experiences physical property damage from blast vibrations, the U.S. Army Claims Service will process and pay claims immediately.
Combating Misinformation and Public Verification
"As much of a threat that lead is in public health, and it is a true threat, misinformation is as well. Be careful where you're getting your sources of information," said Kaufman. "I would strongly recommend that you're listening to FEMA and the Army’s IAAAP website. The CDC is also putting out information, and of course the State of Iowa is going to be continuously communicating to those living here."
