                STATEMENT OF JURISDICTION AND VENUE

      Petitioners seek review of a final permitting decision of the U.S.

Environmental Protection Agency Region 7 (“EPA”) authorizing the treatment of

hazardous waste through open detonation at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in

Middletown, Iowa. This Court has exclusive jurisdiction under Resource

Conservation and Recovery Act (“RCRA”) § 7006(b), 42 U.S.C. § 6976(b), which

provides for direct review in the United States Courts of Appeals of the

Administrator's action “issuing, denying, modifying, or revoking any permit”

under RCRA.

      Venue is proper here under 42 U.S.C. 6976(b) because Petitioners Iowa

Environment Council and Sierra Club are interested persons that reside and/or

transact business upon application of such persons in the state of Iowa, which is

within this Circuit.

   C. There Is No Safe Level of Lead Exposure, Especially for Children

      The danger of lead to children has been known for over a century. Lead is a

neurotoxin: it permanently damages children’s brains and development. Id. High

doses can cause the brain to swell and can be fatal, while lower doses can still

destroy nerve cells in a child’s brain, causing harm that cannot be undone. Id.

Children are most vulnerable because their brains develop rapidly before birth and

in early childhood, and the damage can last into adulthood. Id. Lead is also harmful

to adults, and has been linked to heart disease, kidney and liver disease,

reproductive harm, cognitive decline, and cancer. Id. A 2018 study estimated that

low-level lead exposure is responsible for about 412,000 deaths each year in the

United States, including roughly 256,000 from cardiovascular disease. Id.

      There is no known safe level of lead: researchers have found no point below

which it stops causing harm. Id. Research shows that even very low blood lead

levels, below 10 µg/dL, hurt children. Id. Lead exposure in childhood also harms

learning, attention, and behavior, making children more likely to struggle in

school. Id.

                            STANDARD OF REVIEW

      Pursuant to 42 U.S.C. §6976(b), this Court reviews EPA's action under the

standards set forth in the Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”), 5 U.S.C. § 706.

Petitioners contend that EPA's issuance of the emergency permit was arbitrary,

capricious, an abuse of discretion, otherwise not in accordance with law, and taken

without observance of procedures required by law.

                                    ARGUMENT

      Petitioners seek an emergency stay of the challenged permit pending this

Court’s review pursuant to the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 705 and

Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 18. Because this permit was issued without

notice and public comment, Petitioners did not have the opportunity to first appeal

and move for a stay of the permit before the agency. See 40 C.F.R. §§ 124.19(a)(1)

(providing for appeals of RCRA permits “issued under § 124.15”), 124.15 (final

decision on draft permits subject to public comment); see also 40 C.F.R. §

124.19(a)(2) (“Any person who filed comments on the draft permit…may file a

petition for review as provided in this section.”).

      A request for a stay under APA § 705 is analyzed under the same standard

that federal courts ordinarily use to analyze preliminary injunction requests.

Minnesota v. U.S. Dep't Agric., 826 F. Supp. 3d 975, 993 (D. Minn. 2026), citing

Immigrant Defs. L. Ctr. v. Noem, 145 F.4th 972, 995 (9th Cir. 2025) (explaining



                                          10

that “the factors used to determine whether to issue a § 705 stay under the APA are

the same equitable factors used to consider whether to issue a preliminary

injunction”). A party seeking a preliminary injunction “must establish that he is

likely to succeed on the merits, that he is likely to suffer irreparable harm in the

absence of preliminary relief, that the balance of equities tips in his favor, and that

an injunction is in the public interest.” Winter v. Nat. Res. Def. Council, Inc., 555

U.S. 7, 20, 129 S. Ct. 365, 374, 172 L. Ed. 2d 249 (2008). See also Dataphase Sys.,

Inc. v. C L Sys., Inc., 640 F.2d 109, 114 (8th Cir. 1981) (en banc) (same factors).

The third and fourth stay factors, “assessing the harm to the opposing party and

weighing the public interest,” “merge when the Government is the opposing

party.” Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 435 (2009).

   A. Petitioners are Likely to Succeed on the Merits

          1. EPA’s Permit Fails to Ensure Protection of Human Health and the
             Environment.

      Under RCRA, all hazardous waste permits, including the one at issue here,

must include conditions necessary to protect human health and the environment

and achieve compliance with the Act and regulations, including applicable

requirements in 40 C.F.R. subparts 264 and 266 through 268. See 42 U.S.C. 6925;

40 C.F.R. 270.32. The permit is unlawful and arbitrary and capricious because it

fails in many respects to include such requirements.



                                          11

                a. Failure to comply with environmental performance standards.

      First, the permit fails to comply with fundamental requirements applicable to

permits for open detonation of hazardous waste and necessary to protect human

health and the environment. Open detonation of hazardous waste is regulated under

40 C.F.R. part 264, subpart X. 52 Fed. Reg. 46952 (Dec. 10, 1987); see also EPA

OB/OD Memo at 6 (June 7, 2022),

https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/202206/OBOD_Policy_Memo_signed_6.7.22_508.pdf (“EPA 2022 Memo”) (“OB/OD

units treating explosive waste are permitted under 40 CFR Part 264, Subpart X

Miscellaneous Units”). The regulations governing emergency permits provide that

such permits “[s]hall incorporate, to the extent possible and not inconsistent with

the emergency situation, all applicable requirements of [part 270] and 40 CFR

parts 264 and 266.” 40 C.F.R. § 270.61(b)(6). In issuing this permit, EPA

acknowledged that the requirements of part 264 must be incorporated. See Permit

at 1 (“This Emergency Permit is issued…pursuant to…Sections 264 – Subpart X,

270.61”). The environmental performance standards under Subpart X provide that

“[p]ermits for miscellaneous units are to contain such terms and provisions as

necessary to protect human health and the environment." 40 C.F.R. § 264.601.

“Protection of human health and the environment includes, but is not limited

to…[p]revention of any releases that may have adverse effects on human health or



                                         12

the environment due to migration of waste constituents in the ground water or

subsurface environment… surface water, or wetlands or on the soil surface…[and]

in the air.” Id. § 264.601(a)-(c) (emphasis added).

      Far from preventing releases of hazardous constituents, the emergency

permit authorizes releases of toxic lead dust into the environment and surrounding

communities. The permit contemplates that open detonation of lead azide will

cause “atmospheric dispersion and ground deposition” of lead dust. See e.g.,

Permit Condition Q.2 (requiring a “Final Impact Area Report for the atmospheric

dispersion and ground deposition plume to the EPA for review”), App. B (requiring

modeling of air dispersion and dust-lead deposition), App. C (requiring public

notification of “Exposure Risks” from “Deposition of lead-containing dust [that]

may occur after the detonation on surfaces within certain areas affected by the

detonation plume” and advisory area notifications for “areas that are expected to be

affected by dust-lead deposition”). By the Army’s most recent modeling done to

comply with the permit, open detonation of lead azide threatens to deposit toxic

lead dust over an area approximately 25 miles long and 5 miles wide that

encompasses residences and surface waters, including tributaries to the Mississippi

River. Nov. 18 Army Notice (ex.C). Previous modeling showed the dispersal over

an area more than 40 miles long. Nov. 15 Army Notice (ex.B). Both models show

dispersion of lead in the air along an area stretching over more than 100 miles from



                                         13

the detonation site, including near population centers, and that the plumes could

shift at any moment.

      This expected widespread release of lead dust certainly “may have adverse

effects on human health or the environment.” 40 C.F.R. § 264.601. As noted, the

dangers of lead exposure have long been known. Supra p. 9. Despite these known

impacts, the permit utterly fails to prevent releases to air, water, and soil, in direct

contravention of the applicable requirement and thereby endangers human health

and the environment. EPA cannot rely on conditions for sampling and clean-up as a

substitute for preventing the harmful release in the first place.

      In addition, the emergency permit fails to incorporate several considerations

relevant to protection of human health and the environment that the environmental

performance standards expressly list. See 40 C.F.R. § 264.601(a)-(c). As an initial

matter, while EPA has denied the public access to the Army’s permit application,

EPA’s summary of the permit application in Appendix A to the emergency permit

demonstrates that EPA issued the permit without requiring the Army to submit all

necessary information for miscellaneous units. Namely, Appendix A does not show

that the Army submitted any “[d]etailed hydrologic, geologic, and meteorologic

assessments and land-use maps for the region surrounding the site that address and

ensure compliance of the unit with each factor in the environmental performance

standards of § 264.601.” 40 C.F.R. § 270.23(b). The failure to provide this



                                           14

necessary information shows that EPA could not have incorporated conditions

addressing and ensuring compliance with the Subpart X environmental

performance standards.

      Further, the permit itself shows that EPA largely ignored the potential for

releases to groundwater, the subsurface environment, surface water, wetlands, the

soil surface, and the atmosphere. For example, there are no conditions addressing

the “potential for damage to domestic animals, wildlife, crops, vegetation, and

physical structures caused by exposure to waste constituents.” 40 C.F.R. §

264.601(a)(9), (b)(11), (c)(7). This is particularly concerning given the large

amount of farmland that the Army’s modeling shows could be covered by lead

dust. Nor are there conditions to address potential impacts to current and potential

users of groundwater and surface waters. Id. § 264.601(a)(5), (b)(7). Despite

modeling showing potential dispersal of lead over a large area of land and nearby

waters, the permit fails to specifically account for and address the numerous human

and environmental receptors in that area. The permit’s cursory mentions of releases

to air, soil, and water do not actually address the express considerations required by

Subpart X. For example, Permit Condition IV.E requires the Army to “minimize

the possibility” of “any unplanned, sudden or non-sudden release of hazardous

waste constituents to air, soil, groundwater or surface water.” But the expected

atmospheric dispersal and ground deposition of dust-lead is not unplanned but



                                          15

rather explicitly authorized by the permit. Permit Condition L.1 requires a

contingency plan for activities associated with the open detonation of lead azide

and resulting cleanup to minimize hazards to human health or the environment

from any unplanned or non-sudden releases of hazardous waste or hazardous

constituents to the air, soil, groundwater or surface water. But this bare requirement

to follow a contingency plan that the Army developed (and has not been shared

publicly) itself falls short of actually incorporating permit conditions that expressly

address the considerations outlined in the Subpart X environmental performance

standards. Given these plain deficiencies, the permit is unlawful and arbitrary.

       EPA’s permit lacks other minimum protections that are necessary to protect

human health and the environment from the known risks associated with the Iowa

Army Ammunition Plant’s (IAAP’s) planned open detonation. EPA’s failure to

include additional permit provisions tailored to the significant toxic risk presented

is unlawful and arbitrary.

      As discussed, IAAP has acknowledged that the detonation could result in

exposure to toxic lead dust up to 100 miles away. This is a massive area with an

unknown number of people, daycares, hospitals, animals, livestock, parks,

properties, agricultural areas, gardens, wells, and waterways at risk. At a minimum,

for protection of health and the environment, it was critical for the permit to



                                          16

include specific conditions ensuring that members of the affected public were

given timely notice of the open detonations and the associated threats before they

occur. Yet the permit lacks even a clear deadline by which such notice had to be

provided. As a result, IAAP did not broadly notice its plan or information about the

potential scope of the exposure area until November 15, just five days before the

scheduled open detonations. According to local reports, many areas are only now

finding out about the planned detonations and it is unclear how many potentially

affected residents still do not know about the looming risks to their health and

homes.

                c. Failure to fully consider alternative treatment methods.

      EPA unlawfully authorized the Army to open detonate lead azide without

fully considering alternatives and demonstrating that open detonation is the only

safe option. As noted, the requirements of Subpart X applicable to open detonation

units apply to this emergency permit. Supra, Part A.1.a. EPA recently reaffirmed



                                          18

that, pursuant to Subpart X, permit applicants must “demonstrate that their waste

explosives ‘cannot safely be disposed of through other modes of treatment.’” EPA

June 2022 Memo at 7. To be permitted to open detonate, the Army therefore “must

successfully demonstrate, through accurate waste characterization and an

evaluation of alternative technologies, that the waste has the potential to detonate

and that there are no other technologies that can safely treat that waste stream.” Id.

at 7-8. “Where safe alternatives are available, facilities must use those alternatives

in lieu of OB/OD.” Id. at 1 (emphasis in original).

      The permit notes that the Army did evaluate alternatives, however that

evaluation was not attached to the permit and EPA has not provided the public with

access to the permit application which contains the evaluation. EPA Nov. 16

Notice, https://perma.cc/HXF8-38CR. The Army’s Environmental Assessment

(posted publicly yesterday), prepared to purportedly comply with the National

Environmental Policy Act, shows that it did not fully consider non-detonation

alternatives and mobile treatment units. Environmental Assessment (Nov. 18,

2026), https://d34w7g4gy10iej.cloudfront.net/pubs/pdf_78364.pdf

[https://perma.cc/2WUQ-97YK] (“EA”). It states that, while “Chemical

Neutralization (Non-Detonation)” “offered environmental protections by avoiding

a blast plume” containing toxic lead, this alternative was eliminated from further

consideration “due to unacceptable risk to human safety.” EA at 8. The Army’s



                                          19

reasoning, however, is unsupported by any publicly-available documents.

Specifically, the Army claims that the required additional “‘hands-on’” time and

physical manipulation of the lead azide drums for chemical neutralization

drastically increases the probability of accidental, fatal detonation during handling.

Id. But it provides no supporting data, let alone an “accurate waste

characterization,” to demonstrate the potential for this cache of lead azide to

detonate during chemical neutralization, nor does it explain why no mitigative

measures can be taken to reduce or prevent the risk of a detonation during

chemical neutralization operations. As noted, EPA has not provided the public with

the alternatives evaluation submitted as part of the Army’s application or the

correspondence between EPA and the Army concerning alternatives. Upon

information and belief—including the fact that Appendix A of the emergency

permit does not show that EPA provided any comments or required any revisions

to an alternative technology evaluation—EPA arbitrarily accepted the Army’s

conclusory assertions dismissing chemical neutralization without scrutiny.

      The arbitrariness of EPA’s unquestioning acceptance of the Army’s

conclusion is bolstered by the fact that EPA itself has acknowledged, and

disseminated for public information and use by facilities treating waste explosives,

that chemical neutralization is a viable alternative technology for treating lead

azide specifically. See EPA Compendium of Potential Alternative Technologies to



                                          20

OB/OD at 83 (Jan. 3, 2025), https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/202501/updated_potential_alternative_technologies_compendium.pdf (listing MuniRem

as a treatment technology that has been implemented for lead azide); see also Nat’l

Acad. Sci., Alternatives for the Demilitarization of Conventional Munitions at 6263 (2019), https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25140/chapter/1#ii (describing

MuniRem as a technology using “sulfur reduction chemistry” that “has been

demonstrated to…dissolve and neutralize explosive residues and propellants.”).

      The arbitrariness of EPA’s decision is further illustrated by the stark contrast

between the alternatives the Army considered. EPA accepted the Army’s dismissal

of chemical neutralization, which would avoid a blast plume and dispersal of toxic

lead, based on unsupported reasoning. And in doing so EPA authorized open

detonation instead, which poses threats to human health and the environment that

are supported by data, including the Army’s own modeling of widespread lead

dispersal and deposition and general scientific understanding of the serious harms

from even low levels of lead exposure.

      EPA also acted unlawfully by authorizing the Army to open detonate lead

azide even though the Army seemingly failed to consider using mobile treatment

units as an alternative to open detonation. EPA has identified mobile treatment

units as a class of technology that could “provid[e] alternative technology

treatment services sooner than permitting and constructing a permanent on-site



                                         21

unit,” provide “particularly keen” benefits for facilities “that do not treat waste

explosives routinely,” and “could offer an additional compliance option beyond

off-site shipment and building an alternative technology unit.” 89 Fed. Reg. 19952,

19992 (Mar. 20, 2024). These potential benefits are highly relevant to the Army’s

need to treat lead azide at the IAAP given the Army’s claimed need for expedient

action, the fact that the lead azide is a discreet volume of hazardous waste the plant

is not routinely treated at this site, and the Army has claimed off-site shipment is

not possible. Despite this alignment of needs and benefits, the EA shows that the

Army did not consider this entire class of treatment technology at all. The EA

looked only at the options of mitigated open detonation (what the EPA permitted),

no action, chemical neutralization, transport to the plant’s on-site open detonation

unit, and unmitigated controlled detonation. EA at 7-9. Thus, EPA’s grant of the

permit to authorize open detonation of lead azide, where the Army failed to

evaluate a whole class of technologies that EPA has identified as beneficial to

reducing the need for open detonation, is arbitrary and contrary to EPA’s own

regulations and guidance for open detonation permits.

          2. EPA Failed to Provide Notice and Comment Opportunity.

      EPA’s own regulations require all RCRA emergency permits to be

accompanied by public notice under 40 C.F.R. 124.10(b) that provides at least 45

days for public comment. 40 C.F.R. 270.61(b)(5). Nonetheless, EPA did not notice



                                          22

a draft permit nor provide any opportunity for public comment at all. Indeed, on its

website noticing the final permit, posted just four days ago, EPA announced it is

“neither requesting nor accepting comments on this public notice.” EPA Nov. 16

Notice, https://perma.cc/HXF8-38CR. The regulations do not allow EPA to skirt

this notice-and-comment obligation and EPA cited no such exception to the rule.

Any excuse it may offer now in the name of an “emergency” cannot be justified

under the terms of the rule or by the facts that show the plan to open detonate was

in the works for months and EPA had time to comply. EPA’s failure has left the

Petitioners’ members in the dark, lacking critical information relevant to their

health and safety and deprived of the opportunity to participate in and raise

concerns about EPA’s decisionmaking.

   B. Petitioners will suffer irreparable harm in the absence of a stay.

      “Environmental injury, by its nature, can seldom be adequately remedied by

money damages and is often permanent or at least of long duration, i.e.,

irreparable.” Amoco Prod. Co. v. Vill. of Gambell, AK, 480 U.S. 531, 545 (1987).

Petitioners and their members will be irreparably harmed if the open detonation of

lead azide proceeds as planned on Friday.

      Once lead dust is dispersed, there is no undoing the harm. Lead persists in

the environment and spreads easily between media. Lanphear Decl. ¶ 25. Lead dust

in the air will not just disappear once it settles to the ground—people, cattle,



                                          23

chickens and wildlife will continue to face exposures to lead from soil, water

runoff, and deposition into waterbodies. Id. Lead also builds up and stays in the

human body. Id. at ¶ 29. Once lead enters the body, it travels through the blood to

soft tissues and mineralizing tissues (bones and teeth). Id. It can then be stored for

years in mineralizing tissues where it can accumulate over time and, under some

circumstances—like during pregnancy—re-enter the bloodstream. Id. Lead also

accumulates in the bodies of cattle, chicken, and other animals that are exposed to

contaminated soil or drinking water. Id. Elevated lead levels have been detected in

chicken eggs, cow milk, and the in the tissues and bones of cattle, creating

additional pathways for human exposures. Id. The concentration of agricultural

operations around the detonation site creates a risk that lead will contaminate local

livestock, rendering them unsafe for sale or contaminating the food supply. Id.

      Again, there is no evidence of a threshold for the adverse consequences of

lead exposure. Id. at ¶ 12. In other words, lead is considered hazardous at any

level. Thus, the Army’s planned detonation of 9700 pounds of explosive lead

material poses a significant risk of irreparable harm that cannot later be remedied.

   C. A stay will serve the public interest.

      When the government is opposing a stay pending review, the balance of

equities and public interest factors merge. See Nken, 556 U.S. at 435; Minnesota v.

U.S. Dep't Agric., 826 F. Supp. 3d at 993 (citing Immigrant Defs. L. Ctr., 145 F.4th



                                          24

at 995). The Supreme Court has recognized that “[i]f [environmental] injury is

sufficiently likely, . . . the balance of harms will usually favor the issuance of an

injunction to protect the environment.” Amoco Prod. Co, 480 U.S. at 545.

Moreover, “[t]here is generally no public interest in the perpetuation of unlawful

agency action.” League of Women Voters of United States v. Newby, 838 F.3d 1, 12

(D.C. Cir. 2016) (internal citations omitted). “To the contrary, there is a substantial

public interest ‘in having governmental agencies abide by the federal laws that

govern their existence and operations.’” Id. (citations omitted). There can be no

protectable government interest in bypassing environmental review that is plainly

required by the law and are fundamental to protecting human health and the

environment.

      Petitioners do not dispute the EPA and Army’s assertion that there is risk

involved in leaving the lead azide at the IAAP untreated. Indeed, as the EPA’s

permit recognizes, recrystallization during the Army’s “long-term storage” of

reactive lead azide in an area that was “not covered for treatment, storage, or

disposal of hazardous waste” under the Army’s hazardous waste permit has created

circumstances posing a threat to public health and the environment, such that it

necessitated EPA issuing several emergency permits to provide needed oversight

of the Army’s continued storage of lead azide while the agencies decided how to

address the problem. Permit at 1-2; supra pp. 3-4. In more direct terms, the Army



                                           25

left 68 drums of a primary explosive to degrade for roughly two decades until it

decided that the only safe option became detonating them near populated areas.

That turned the Army’s storage management failure into a public health event for

people in at least two states. This rushed final week is a symptom of that; the Army

has been aware of the risk since at least March, yet it gave public notice of its plans

a mere five days before intended detonation.

      Petitioners do believe there is a public interest in adequately and safely

addressing the risk posed by the improper management of lead azide at the IAAP.

But that interest is not served by the permit’s authorization of open detonations that

would blast lead dust across an area of Iowa (and possibly Illinois) that contains

thousands of families (including young children highly sensitive to lead exposure),

significant quantities of farmland, water bodies including the iconic Mississippi

River, and countless wildlife and other natural resources. And particularly not

where, as explained, the open detonations are a result of unlawful government

activity—including the EPA’s arbitrary acceptance of the Army’s dismissal of

treatment alternatives that would avoid a blast plume—where much of the

impacted public received less than a week’s notice, where inaccurate and

potentially harmful information was disseminated by the Army on actions residents

should take to protect themselves from lead exposure, and where several crucial

conditions to protect human health and the environment are glaringly absent from



                                          26

the permit. Thus, the Army’s proposed solution creates entirely new risks, and does

so with too little time and transparency for members of the public to adequately

protect themselves.

      To be clear, the Court is not balancing the risk of treating lead azide with the

risk of not taking any action. What the Court must balance is the risks associated

with the possibility of unplanned detonation while crucial additional time is given

to take actions necessary to protect human health and the environment, against the

very significant known harm of a massive lead dust plume. Given the facts at hand,

the balance of equities and the public interest strongly favor a stay pending review.
