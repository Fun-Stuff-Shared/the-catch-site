Chapter 1: Purpose of and Need for the Proposed Action
1.1 Introduction
      The Department of the Army, through the Army Sustainment Command (ASC), is
evaluating the environmental impacts associated with the emergency disposal of
unserviceable explosive material at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP), located in
Middletown, Iowa. IAAAP is a vital military industrial facility responsible for the load,
assemble, and pack (LAP) of medium and large-caliber munitions in support of the
Department of War (DoW) warfighting missions.
         In March 2026, during a proactive facility safety review, IAAAP identified a
stockpile of lead azide, a highly sensitive primary explosive used in munitions detonators,
that had degraded and crystallized over time. Due to its chemical degradation, the
material’s legal status transitioned from a viable production component to an
unserviceable hazardous waste. Lead azide in a crystallized state becomes extremely
shock-and-friction sensitive presenting a serious safety hazard to plant personnel and
preventing safe physical transport of the material to standard off-site demilitarization
facilities.
       The material is currently secured within onsite, explosive-proof earth-covered
magazines (ECMs). The facility has coordinated with regulatory agencies to manage the
material under a Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) emergency
framework. However, to permanently abate the explosive safety hazard, the Army must
execute a final disposition of the material. This Environmental Assessment (EA) analyzes
the proposed operational plan to conduct a controlled, mitigated detonation in place to
safely destroy the unserviceable lead azide. The plan is designed to minimize blast
overpressure, noise, and the dispersion of environmental contaminants through the use
of engineered blast containment barriers and other controls.

1.2 Purpose and Need
        The purpose of the proposed action is to safely and permanently dispose of a
degraded unserviceable stockpile of lead azide at the IAAAP. Specifically, the objective
is to execute a controlled, mitigated detonation in place (Blow-in-Place / BIP) that
definitively destroys the shock-sensitive material, thereby eliminating the immediate
explosive safety threat to facility personnel and infrastructure, while utilizing engineered
mitigation measures to strictly monitor and control blast overpressure, noise, and the
dispersion of environmental contaminants.
       The proposed action is needed because the current stockpile of unserviceable lead
azide presents an unacceptable explosive safety risk to IAAAP personnel, infrastructure,
and surrounding operations. Temperature fluctuations can also cause small amounts of

lead azide to dissolve in the alcohol/water solution and then recrystallize into its pure state
as needles and platelets, which are significantly more sensitive to shock and friction. Due
to its current degraded, crystallized state, the lead azide has become highly sensitive to

prevent an accidental, uncontrolled detonation that could result in catastrophic loss of life,
severe and more unmitigated environmental contamination, and critical disruption to
IAAAP’s essential munitions production capabilities, which are vital to the military
readiness of U.S. ground forces.

1.3 Scope, Coordination, and Decision-making
       The U.S. Army has prepared this Environmental Assessment (EA) in order to
ensure informed decision making and compliance with the National Environmental Policy
Act (Title 42 United States Code ((USC) § 4321 et seq.) (NEPA); Department of War
(DoW) NEPA Implementing Procedures (-September 2026); Army Regulation (AR) 200-
1 (April 2026), Environmental Protection and Enhancement; and applicable Army NEPA
guidance. Per NEPA, this EA is a concise public document prepared to set forth the basis
of the Army’s Finding of No Significant Impact (FONSI) based on mitigations or
determination that an environmental impact statement is necessary. The resulting
decision and mitigation commitments that will be undertaken to avoid significant effects,
to be made by the Commander, Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP), will take into
consideration the analysis in this EA and, as applicable, associated comments received
from the public and from agency coordination activities.

Chapter 2: Description of the Proposed Action and Alternatives
2.1 Proposed Action
      The Proposed Action is to develop and execute an operational plan to safely and
responsibly dispose of unserviceable lead azide at the IAAAP by conducting a controlled
detonation. The mission objective is to destroy the lead azide while mitigating public
exposure to the resulting blast and plume.
       The project proponent is the U.S. Army. Execution of the Proposed Action is a
multi-organizational effort: the EOD team will conduct the detonation (Phase 2), and an
Environmental Services contractor will be utilized for initial cleanup (Phase 3) and long-
term remediation (Phase 4). Coordination of public information and evacuation plans will
be managed by the ASC Public Affairs Office (PAO) and installation leadership.
       The Proposed Action involves the controlled detonation of the lead azide stockpile
to eliminate the explosive hazard. The end state of the action is the total destruction of
unserviceable material, minimization of public and personnel exposure, develop an
empirical understanding of the extent of environmental contamination, and the restoration
of the site for standard operations or long-term remediation.
        All activities associated with the Proposed Action will occur within the boundaries
of the IAAAP in Middletown, Iowa. The detonation will be localized to the storage ECMs
currently containing the material. Figures 1-1 and 1-2 on page 4 show the installation
location and installation boundary, respectively.

       The intent is to execute the detonation during the fall to take advantage of periods
of reduced thermal cycling and to ensure the lowest impact to facility operations and local
agriculture (harvesting). The action will be conducted during daylight hours. The two
ECMs will be detonated simultaneously.
        The Proposed Action will be implemented through a phased strategy and a series
of strict operational controls:
Pre-implementation Activities (Phase I):

•   Operational Controls: The Army will implement a temporary access restriction
    and safety exclusion zone for all non-essential plant operations, with only
    emergency services remaining active.

•   Donor Sourcing: Required donor material will be sourced by the DoW EOD (primary)
    or Army Sustainment Command (ASC) (secondary) and staged on-site.

•   Engineering Control: Construction of blast containment barriers (lime-fill and sand-
    filled HESCO barriers) with a designated venting area.

•   Vegetation Clearance: Brush and vegetation removal will be conducted to reduce
    potential for fire hazard.

•   Planning & Modeling: Optimal weather conditions for execution will be determined
    based on atmospheric modeling and EOD recommendations (e.g., no precipitation,
    surface wind speeds not to exceed 15 mph, no thunderstorms, and no temperature
    inversions).

•   Go/No-Go Criteria: A comprehensive checklist must be satisfied prior to execution,
    including favorable weather, final EOD approval, confirmation of safety controls, and
    execution of public notification measures.

•   Communications: The PAO will execute an approved communication plan, including
    top-line messaging, news releases, and evacuation and public notification plans. A
    public open house was conducted on 15 October 2026.
Operational Activities (Phase 2):

•   The DoW EOD team will conduct the controlled detonation of the lead azide
    simultaneously.
Post-operational Requirements (Phases 3 & 4):

•   Initial Cleanup (Phase 3): An Environmental Services contractor will be deployed to
    assess the site and conduct initial cleanup activities to understand the extent of
    environmental impacts, if any.

2.2 Alternatives
2.2.1 Screening Criteria
      For an alternative to the proposed action to be considered viable and carried
forward for detailed analysis in this EA, it must meet the Purpose and Need identified in
Section 1.2. Furthermore, it must satisfy the following technical, safety, environmental,
and mission-oriented screening criteria. These criteria were developed by the Army and
the ASC to evaluate all potential Courses of Action (COAs) for the disposal of the
unserviceable lead azide stockpile:

•   Personnel Safety (Handling Minimization): The alternative must drastically limit or
    eliminate the need for personnel to physically handle, manipulate, or transport the
    crystallized lead azide. Because the degraded material is highly sensitive to shock,
    heat, and friction, prolonged or repetitive handling presents an unacceptable risk of a
    fatal, accidental detonation.

•   Environmental Protection and Regulatory Compliance: The alternative must
    incorporate mechanisms to monitor, contain, and mitigate the release of hazardous
    constituents—specifically airborne lead particulates, unreacted energetics, and toxic
    gases (e.g., hydrazoic acid). The process must comply with the Resource
    Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) and DoW environmental standards to
    prevent uncontrolled off-site contamination, including mechanisms to manage post-
    treatment hazardous waste.

•   Public Health and Off-Installation Impacts: The alternative must ensure that blast
    overpressure, noise (impulse decibels), and any residual atmospheric plumes do not
    exceed safety thresholds or negatively impact the civilian population and agricultural
    lands outside the IAAAP installation boundaries.

•   Mission Compatibility and Timeline: The alternative must be executable within a
    reasonable timeframe to abate the immediate hazard. It must .allow the Army to lift
    the facility-wide temporary access restriction and safely resume its essential
    medium and large-caliber munitions production mission as quickly as possible.

•   Technical Feasibility: The alternative must rely on proven, technologically mature
    disposal methods approved by the DDESB (e.g., standardized EOD detonation
    protocols or established chemical neutralization) that can be realistically resourced,
    staged, and executed at the current magazine locations without requiring the
    construction of new, permanent demilitarization facilities.

2.2.2 Alternative 1 – Mitigated Controlled Detonation (Proposed Action)
      Alternative 1 consists of the execution of the Proposed Action as described in
Section 2.1. Under this alternative, the Army would conduct a controlled, phased
detonation (Blow-in-Place) of drums of unserviceable lead azide at the IAAAP. This action
would be executed by the DoW EOD team utilizing donor explosives simultaneous
detonation of both ECMs.
       This alternative incorporates all operational controls and mitigation strategies
detailed in Section 2.1, including atmospheric plume modeling, a comprehensive Go/No-
Go checklist, facility-wide temporary access restrictions, and a phased environmental
cleanup and remediation plan. Alternative 1 is the proponent's preferred alternative
because it meets all screening criteria, specifically by minimizing personnel handling of
the crystallized material and providing a definitive, safe, and regulated path to hazard
abatement and mission restoration.
2.2.3 No Action Alternative
       Under the No Action Alternative, the Army would not proceed with the controlled
disposal of the drums of unserviceable lead azide. The material would remain in its
current storage configuration within the ECMs. This alternative is not viable because it
results in two significant, unacceptable outcomes:

•   Increased Safety Risk: The "No Action" alternative would allow the lead azide to
    continue its chemical degradation and crystallization process indefinitely. As the
    crystals grow and dehydrate, the material becomes increasingly sensitive to shock,
    friction, and thermal changes. The crystallization process is especially evident during
    colder months, when temperatures are below freezing. This significantly increases the
    probability of an unintended, accidental detonation, which poses a catastrophic threat
    to personnel life-safety, installation infrastructure, and the surrounding community.

•   Regulatory Non-Compliance: Because the lead azide has been declared an
    "unserviceable hazardous waste," the facility is legally bound by the RCRA permit to
    manage the material toward a final disposal or treatment pathway. Selecting "No
    Action" would place the Army in persistent non-compliance with RCRA hazardous
    waste storage and treatment standards, potentially resulting in regulatory enforcement
    actions, significant fines, and legal liability.
      Furthermore, the No Action Alternative would require the continuation of the
temporary restriction of installation access, indefinitely halting essential munitions
production missions and degrading the military readiness of U.S. ground forces. While
the No Action Alternative does not meet the Purpose and Need for the action, it is carried
forward in this EA as a baseline against which the impacts of the Proposed Action can be
compared.

2.2.4 Alternatives Eliminated from Further Consideration
       During the planning process, the Army developed and evaluated multiple Courses
of Action (COAs) to address the lead azide hazard. The following alternatives were
considered but ultimately eliminated from detailed analysis in this EA because they did
not meet critical screening criteria, specifically regarding human safety and regulatory
compliance.
Chemical Neutralization (Non-Detonation)
    This alternative proposed neutralizing and decomposing the drums of lead azide on-
site using a chemical reagent. The objective would be to eliminate the explosive hazard
in a controlled, non-detonation manner to prevent environmental contamination and
ensure a disposal pathway.

•   Methodology: The operation would be managed by a single prime contractor for all
    phases, utilizing U.S. Government (USG) or Army National Guard (ANG) support
    when feasible. Neutralization would occur during daylight hours, processing an
    estimated 3 to 10 drums per day (requiring 7 to 22 total operational days). Donor
    explosives would remain on-site as a contingency "branch plan" to transition back to
    a detonation COA if neutralization failed.

•   Operational Controls: Strict Go/No-Go criteria (e.g., high winds, precipitation, critical
    equipment failure) would dictate daily operations. The Army would issue a temporary
    access restriction notification during active neutralization, accompanied by public
    PAO messaging and evacuation plans.

•   Reason for Elimination: While this alternative offered environmental protections by
    avoiding a blast plume, it was eliminated from detailed study due to unacceptable risk
    to human safety. Because the lead azide has crystallized, it is highly sensitive to shock
    and friction. The chemical neutralization process requires significantly more "hands-
    on" time and physical manipulation of the drums by personnel over a prolonged 7-to-
    22-day period, drastically increasing the probability of accidental, fatal detonation
    during handling. Additionally, uncertainty remained for successful neutralization
    including the existing mixture of water/ethanol the lead azide is contained in.
Transport to On-Site Open Detonation (OD) Unit
  This alternative proposed treating the lead azide at IAAAP’s existing, RCRA permitted
Open Detonation (OD) unit, which is located in a different area of the installation.

•   Methodology: This option would have required personnel to physically handle and
    load all drums of crystallized lead azide from their current storage ECMs. The drums
    would then be transported across the installation's road network to the permitted OD


                                              8

                                                                          IAAAP-EA-2026-01


    area. At the OD unit, crews would bury one to three barrels at a time, cover the buried
    barrels with donor explosive charges, and then initiate detonation.

•   Reason for Elimination: This alternative was eliminated because it failed to meet the
    primary project screening criterion of minimizing or eliminating personnel handling of
    the shock-sensitive material. The Army determined that the risks associated with
    moving the drums, including potential sloshing and physical shock during transport,
    loading, and burial, presented an unacceptable safety risk and a high probability of
    accidental, uncontrolled detonation. Therefore, any alternative involving transport of
    the material was deemed not viable.
Unmitigated Controlled Detonation
       Under this alternative, EOD would conduct the controlled detonation of the lead
azide (similar to the Proposed Action) but without implementing engineered
environmental and protective controls (e.g., omitting atmospheric plume modeling,
omitting blast/noise mitigation, and foregoing immediate post-blast soil remediation).

•   Reason for Elimination: This alternative was eliminated because an entirely
    unmitigated detonation of drums of heavy-metal-based primary explosives would
    result in the uncontrolled airborne dispersion of toxic lead particulates and energetic
    residue. This would violate the RCRA and DoW environmental protection standards,
    presenting an unacceptable hazard to public health, surrounding agricultural lands,
    and the environment.

Chapter 3: Affected Environment and Consequences
3.1 Introduction
       This section describes the affected environment and analyzes the potential
environmental impacts associated with the No Action Alternative and the Proposed Action
(Alternative 1) described in Chapter 2.
       In accordance with 40 CFR 1501.3(d) and the DoW NEPA Implementing
Procedures (September 2026), the Army has determined the significance of potential
impacts by considering the degree of the effects. This includes evaluating both short-term
and long-term effects, beneficial and adverse effects, and the potential impact on public
health and safety. The severity of environmental impacts is characterized as follows:

•   None/Negligible: No measurable impacts are expected to occur.

•   Minor: Measurable adverse impacts are expected, but they are primarily short-term
    and do not result in a lasting change to the resource.

•   Moderate: Noticeable adverse impacts that have a measurable effect on a resource
    and may have long-term implications.

•   Significant: Adverse impacts would be obvious and serious. For the purposes of this
    EA, significant impacts are analyzed to determine if they can be mitigated to a less-
    than-significant level to support a Mitigated FONSI

•   Beneficial: Impacts would result in a positive change or improvement to the resource.
        The Army utilized both quantitative and qualitative analyses to determine these
levels. Where applicable, the analysis identifies whether effects are avoided or minimized
through the implementation of the specific mitigation measures defined in the Proposed
Action.
       The environmental analysis presented in the following sections is quantitatively
and qualitatively grounded in the detailed technical plans developed for the associated
RCRA emergency treatment permit application. The evaluation of potential impacts and
the effectiveness of mitigation measures are based on specific planning documents,
including, but not limited to, the Treatment Design and Implementation Plan, the
Environmental Sampling and Analysis Plan (SAP), the Waste Analysis Plan (WAP), the
Contingency Plan, and the Closure Plan for the treatment area. This approach ensures
that the NEPA analysis is fully aligned with the comprehensive engineering, safety, and
environmental planning conducted for the Proposed Action.

3.2 Land Use
3.2.1 Definition of the Resource
       Land use refers to the management and modification of the natural environment.
For this EA, land use is defined by the operational zoning and functional classifications
established by the IAAAP military mission and documented in the installation's Integrated
Natural Resources Management Plan (INRMP) for 2024-2029.
3.2.2 Existing Conditions
       IAAAP encompasses approximately 19,024 acres in Des Moines County, Iowa.
According to the INRMP, the installation's primary land use is Military Industrial /
Explosives Operations, and its landscape is managed in four distinct categories:
Agricultural Outleases, Improved Grounds, Semi-Improved Grounds, and
Unimproved/Other.
       The Proposed Action will occur entirely within a designated high-security
ammunition storage yard on the installation, which falls within the "Improved Grounds"
land use category. The area contains multiple ECMs, including the two that house the
unserviceable lead azide. The entire storage yard is enclosed by a security fence with
access restricted through a controlled gate. This area is strictly governed by DDESB
regulations and is fully contained within established Explosive Safety Quantity-Distance
(ESQD) arcs, which are pre-determined safety buffers that already restrict this land for
high-hazard activities.
       Land use immediately surrounding the storage yard's perimeter is a mix of
undeveloped and agricultural areas; it is forested to the northwest, with agricultural fields
located to the southwest, south, and east. Furthermore, the project area and the land
immediately surrounding it have been closed for recreational access, including hunting
and fishing.
3.2.3 Impacts of the No Action Alternative
        Under the No Action Alternative, the unserviceable lead azide would remain stored
in the two ECMs indefinitely. This would result in the immediate and permanent loss of
use of these storage facilities for their designated purpose, creating a long-term adverse
land-use restriction that conflicts with the military mission.
       More critically, this alternative allows the risk of accidental, uncontrolled detonation
to persist and increase over time. Such an event would cause catastrophic damage to the
ECMs and the surrounding land, creating a large-scale hazardous debris field and
potentially rendering adjacent storage facilities, access roads, and natural buffer zones
unusable. This would represent a significant, long-term adverse impact on land use at the

returned to any productive use.

      The Area of Potential Effect (APE) for archaeological resources is limited to the
ground that will be disturbed by the detonation and subsequent cleanup. This land has
been previously and extensively disturbed by the original ECM construction and decades
of associated industrial operations, making the presence of intact, undisturbed
archaeological resources highly unlikely.
3.8.3 Impacts of the No Action Alternative
       Under the No Action Alternative, the drums of crystallized lead azide would remain
stored in ECMs 1 and 2. While this alternative does not involve planned demolition, the
escalating instability of the explosive material presents a severe threat. An accidental,
unmitigated detonation would result in the sudden, uncontrolled destruction of the ECMs.

Because an accident is an unplanned event, it falls outside standard preservation

•   Severity Determination: Minor Adverse. The No Action Alternative presents a

3.8.4 Impacts of Alternative 1 (Proposed Action)
      Under Alternative 1 (Mitigated Controlled Detonation), the deliberate explosive

documentation and the physical preservation of period NHL properties in other locations.
Consequently, the demolition of ECMs 1 and 2 is legally compliant, and no further NHPA

anticipated.
•   Severity Determination: None/Negligible. Because the loss of the Tier 4 structures

3.9 Biological Resources
3.9.1 Definition of the Resource
      Biological resources encompass the native and introduced plants (flora) and
animals (fauna) that inhabit the installation, along with their associated habitats. This
resource area is heavily regulated under federal statutes, primarily:
•   The Endangered Species Act (ESA) of 1973 (16 U.S.C. § 1531 et seq.), which protects
    threatened and endangered (T&E) species and their critical habitats.
•   The Migratory Bird Treaty Act (MBTA), which prohibits the taking of migratory birds,
    nests, or eggs.
•   The Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act (BGEPA), which protects eagle populations
    and their nesting sites.
       The signed IAAAP 2024-2029 INRMP serves as the primary regulatory and
planning document coordinating the installation's military mission with the conservation
of these protected biological resources.
3.9.2 Existing Conditions
•   Vegetation and Habitat Classifications: According to the INRMP, IAAAP contains a
    diverse ecological landscape of forests, prairies, and agricultural lands. The specific
    project footprint is located entirely within "Improved Grounds," an area characterized
    by maintained turfgrass and gravel access roads. This area is directly bordered by
    "Unimproved Grounds," which consist of mature oak-hickory woodlands that provide
    habitat for a variety of wildlife.
•   Wildlife: The installation’s vast undeveloped acreage supports healthy populations of
    typical Midwestern fauna, including white-tailed deer, wild turkey, and various raptors,
    small mammals, and reptiles. The Red-headed Woodpecker, a species protected
    under the MBTA, is a common species on the installation. While not a threatened or
    endangered species, the Bald Eagle is also frequently observed on post and is
    protected under the BGEPA.
•   Threatened and Endangered (T&E) Species: The INRMP and project-specific
    environmental reviews confirm that several federally listed species are known to occur
    or have suitable habitat within the installation boundaries. The primary species of
    concern for this project are forest-dwelling bats. The dense woodlands adjacent to the
    project area provide suitable roosting and foraging habitat for the following species:
           -   Indiana Bat (Myotis sodalis): Federally Endangered.
           -   Northern Long-eared Bat (Myotis septentrionalis): Federally Endangered.

           -   Tricolored Bat (Perimyotis subflavus): Proposed for Federal listing as
               Endangered.
      In addition, the installation's open grasslands support milkweed, the host plant for
the Monarch Butterfly (Danaus plexippus), a candidate for Federal listing.
3.9.3 Impacts of the No Action Alternative
      Under the No Action Alternative, the explosive hazard would remain in storage,
presenting an ongoing risk of an accidental, unmitigated detonation.
•   Direct Habitat Destruction: An accidental detonation would occur without prior fire-
    suppression staging. Such an explosion could easily ignite a catastrophic forest fire in
    the adjacent Unimproved oak-hickory woodlands. During the bat active season (April
    1 to October 31), a wildfire would result in the direct "take" (mortality) of federally
    endangered Indiana and Northern Long-eared bats, including non-volant (unable to
    fly) pups in summer maternity roosts, and destroy critical nesting habitat for migratory
    birds.
•   Chemical Contamination: An unmitigated blast would scatter lead and explosive
    residues over a wide, unmanaged area. Subsequent rainfall would wash these heavy
    metals into the Brush Creek and Long Creek watersheds, leading to bioaccumulation
    of lead in the aquatic food web, poisoning fish, amphibians, and insectivorous
    birds/bats.
•   Severity Determination: Significant Adverse. The No Action Alternative presents an
    unacceptable risk of catastrophic forest fire, direct mortality of federally endangered
    species, and long-term heavy metal bioaccumulation in the local ecosystem.
3.9.4 Impacts of Alternative 1 (Proposed Action)
       Under Alternative 1, impacts to biological resources are minimized through the
project's design and operational controls.
•   Habitat Alteration: As part of a coordinated safety and maintenance action within the
    ammunition storage yard, some brush and trees have been removed to ensure
    compliance with explosive safety standards. Per the NEPA Record of Environmental
    Consideration (REC) for this action, all vegetation removal was coordinated with the
    USFWS to ensure compliance with the ESA, including adherence to time-of-year
    restrictions that prohibit tree removal when endangered bats may be roosting.
    Therefore, direct impacts to bat habitat have been avoided.
•   Acoustic Disturbance: The primary impact to wildlife will be the instantaneous
    impulse noise from the detonation, which will temporarily startle and displace birds
    and mammals in the immediate vicinity. This is a transient, short-term effect, and
    wildlife is expected to return to the area shortly after the event.

3.9.5 Mitigation Measures and Best Management Practices (BMPs)
      To ensure absolute protection of T&E species and adjacent forest habitats during
execution, the Army will implement the following measures:

•   Strict Prohibition Adherence to USFWS Conservation Measures (Mitigation): All
    vegetation management activities required for the project were conducted in full
    coordination with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. To avoid impacts to the Indiana
    Bat and Northern Long-eared Bat, a strict time-of-year restriction was enforced,
    prohibiting any tree removal during the bat active season (April 1 to October 31).

•   Pre-Staged Fire Suppression (Mitigation): Due to the proximity of the adjacent
    woodlands, IAAAP emergency services and fire crews will be pre-staged immediately

    extinguish any localized grass fires before they can spread to the forest canopy.

    disposal of the contaminated crater soil is a critical biological mitigation. This prevents
    lead from leaching into the soil-subsurface where it could be bioaccumulated by soil
    invertebrates and subsequently enter the diet of local insectivorous birds and
    endangered bats.

Chapter 4: Findings and Conclusions
4.1 Summary of Environmental Impacts
       This Environmental Assessment (EA) has evaluated the potential environmental,
socioeconomic, and safety impacts associated with the Proposed Action: the controlled,
mitigated detonation of drums of unserviceable, crystallized lead azide at the IAAAP. The
analysis compared the impacts of the Proposed Action (Alternative 1) against the No
Action Alternative across all relevant resource areas.
       The findings of this EA demonstrate that the No Action Alternative presents an
unacceptable, persistent, and escalating risk to human health, safety, and the
environment. Leaving the chemically unstable lead azide in storage guarantees
regulatory non-compliance with the RCRA and creates a constant threat of an accidental,
unmitigated detonation. Such an event would result in significant, uncontrolled adverse
impacts to air quality, water resources, historic properties, biological resources, and the
regional socioeconomic stability of Des Moines County.
       Conversely, the analysis confirms that Alternative 1 (Proposed Action) resolves
the serious explosive hazard and restores the installation to regulatory compliance. While
the controlled detonation will produce severe, instantaneous physical effects (blast
overpressure, noise) and generate a localized, short-term release of hazardous
constituents (lead particulates and combustion gases), these impacts are heavily
mitigated.
       By implementing strict operational controls, including atmospheric plume modeling,
meteorological Go/No-Go criteria, phased RCRA soil excavation, and execution within
established ESQD arcs, the severity of all adverse environmental impacts is reduced to
Minor (Short-Term) or None/Negligible. Furthermore, the action yields a Beneficial (Long-
Term) impact on Land Use, Human Health and Safety, and Socioeconomics by
permanently eliminating a catastrophic threat and restoring the military industrial capacity
of IAAAP.
4.2 Conclusion and Recommendation
       Based on the comprehensive analysis contained within this EA, and in accordance
with the Council on Environmental Quality (CEQ) regulations (40 CFR 1501-1508) and
DoW NEPA Implementing Procedures (September 2026), the Army concludes that the
Proposed Action will not result in significant impacts to the human or natural environment,
provided that all identified mitigation measures are strictly implemented and monitored.
       The implementation of the Proposed Action does not constitute a major federal
action significantly affecting the quality of the human environment. Therefore, the
preparation of an Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) is not required.
