The Lead Azide Is Stored in Earth-Covered Magazines That Buffer Outdoor Temperature
Swings

6. According to EPA’s emergency permit, the 68 drums of lead azide are stored within
Earth Covered Magazines at the plant.t The Army has stated that because of the material’s hazard,
it will be destroyed in place, so the drums will not be moved into the open. [Cite source.] Google
Earth imagery of the storage area, which I reviewed on November 20, 2026, is consistent with

earth-covered structures.

7. Soil is an effective thermal insulator. An earth-covered concrete magazine has
substantial thermal mass, and its interior temperature responds slowly to changes in outdoor air
temperature. In my professional opinion, a change in outdoor temperature of up to approximately
20 degrees Fahrenheit over a period of days would not produce an immediate or comparable

change in temperature inside the magazines.

8. Reports indicate that, in preparation for the detonation, wetted sand has been placed
against the sides and top of the magazines.> That material adds further thermal mass and would

further slow any change in interior temperature.

The Forecast Does Not Present a Departure from Conditions the Material Has Already
Experienced

9. The forecast for the Burlington, Iowa area through November 30, 2026, shows lows
in the 20s and highs in the upper 30s degrees Fahrenheit, with temperatures forecast to return to

the mid-50s after that date.°

10. Throughout November 2026, the Burlington area has already recorded lows in the

forecast warming.

Temperature and the Army’s Destabilization Concern

11. I understand the Army’s stated concern about “further destabilization” to mean that
lead azide may become more sensitive to friction or shock in colder conditions. The Army has not,
to my knowledge, published data or analysis showing that the sensitivity of this material changes
over the temperature range forecast or quantifying any such change. Regardless of the chemistry,
for the reasons stated in paragraphs 6 through 8, the forecast change in outdoor temperature would
not translate into an immediate or comparable change in temperature inside the earth-covered

magazines where the material is stored.

The EA (pdf page 7) claims that the Blow-in-Place mitigated detonation will “definitively” destroy
the shock sensitive material. Later, at pdf page 9 the Army states that the objective is to “destroy
the lead azide” and that the “end state of the action is the total destruction of unserviceable
material...” Detonations of any energetic materials are never 100 percent effective in destroying
energetic materials. In fact, so called “yields” of detonation (in simple terms, the percent of
energetic material that is destroyed in a detonation or the percent of energy that is released in a
detonation compared to the total energy in all of the energetic material to be destroyed) are often
well below 1| or 100 percent, indicating that not all of the energetic material is destroyed, given the
violent nature of the detonation and the resulting dispersal of the material itself as well as
detonation/combustion by products. In fact, the EA, at pdf page 12 confirms that among the
hazardous constituents that may be released are “*...unreacted energetics...”

In addition to unreacted energetics, the EA at pdf page 12 notes that the airborne releases will
include lead particulates and toxic gases (e.g., hydrazoic acid). Elsewhere the Army notes that the
releases will include unspecified “combustion gases” (see EA pdf page 20).

I note that hydrazoic acid, which the EA admits will be produced, is a colorless, volatile, and
explosive liquid at room temperature and pressure. It is also highly toxic.°

Yet, the only assessment I have seen to date has been for lead only and not for the unreacted
energetics or hydrazoic acid. Based on my knowledge and experience, I cannot rule out the
potential formation and release of a wider range of combustion toxics including many volatile
organic compounds (because the lead azide is stored in an ethanol/water mixture®), hydrogen
cyanide, and others.

When lead azide, stored in the 68 drums in an ethanol/water mixture, while partially crystallized,
is detonated using unspecified donor charges, as noted above the detonation will: (i) not destroy
all of the lead azide and some of it will be released via the opening in the mitigation barrier; (ii)
produce a range of lead compounds; and (iii) produce additional toxics such as hydrazoic acid and
a range of toxic VOCs. These will be released to the ambient atmosphere and exposure to these
chemicals can present serious risk to humans, animals, and the environment via inhalation, dermal
contact, incidental ingestion, impacts on land, and impacts on streams and waterways.

Given the lack of detail provided to the public, it is clear that the potential for release of such
dangerous chemicals and their impacts have not been fully assessed in planning for this detonation.
It only appears that the Army and EPA have modeled the release of lead alone and assessed its
impact using the National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS) for lead. It is important for the
public to know about impacts of the non-lead toxics I have noted. This will require additional
modeling and risk assessment.

I understand that there are mitigation measures in place for the detonation, however without
additional information, it is not possible to assure that those measures will be effective in protecting
human health. Little is publicly disclosed about the design basis for the mitigation such as the
Integrated Engineering Blast Controls (see FONSI pdf page 2) which include “19,000 cubic yards
...of lime-fill foundation and 300 sand-filled HESCO barriers...” How these quantities of
foundation and barrier materials were determined in not clear. Therefore, their ability to contain
the simultaneous blast energy from the detonation of the 68 drums in two separate earth-covered
magazines (ECMs) is not at all clear.

The public record also contains no information about what is contained in the other ECMs near
the two in question that will be destroyed and what impacts the lead azide detonation might cause
to the other nearby ECMs (see EA pdf page 17) and their unknown contents.

Without a more thorough analysis of the expected blast energy release, the type, numbers, and
placement of the donor charges, the expected yield(s), and the combustion byproducts that will
result from this proposed detonation action, a proper mitigation design cannot be assessed. The
presence of water cannons and wetting mitigation is simply impossible to evaluate without
additional details of how the detonation and its aftermath will unfold.

It is my opinion that the EA has hastily rejected the option of chemical neutralization. The Army
and EPA should engage with independent experts to comprehensively and transparently consider
all alternatives to detonating the crystallized lead azide (thus creating environmental and public
health risks), including chemical neutralization and those identified by the National Academies of
Sciences, and explain to the public why they were rejected (see Attachment B).
