Groups Sue to Halt Planned Detonation at Ammunition Plant in Iowa - Earthjustice

Detonation near the Iowa-Illinois border could spread a lead plume for at least 100 miles
Contacts
Tylar Greene, tgreene@earthjustice.org
Earthjustice, on behalf of the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club Iowa Chapter, sued today over an emergency permit the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) issued to the U.S. Army to detonate lead azide, a toxic lead-based explosive, in Middletown, Iowa, on Nov. 20. The detonation at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant would release a toxic plume of lead dust into the air that could travel 100 miles north of the site, depending on weather conditions.
The organizations are challenging the validity of the emergency permit the EPA granted under the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) to the U.S. Army authorizing detonation of the lead-based hazardous waste at the plant.
“The EPA itself admits that there is no safe level of lead exposure for humans,” said Michael Schmidt, General Counsel at the Iowa Environmental Council. “When our federal government makes decisions that result in a potentially toxic plume of lead dust that could cover much of southeastern Iowa, Iowans deserve to know that every available alternative was considered before choosing this method of disposal.”
There are 68 barrels, amounting to about 9,500 pounds, of lead azide on-site, according to information from the U.S. Army. The material is volatile, meaning it may not be safe to move or to store it long-term. By the Army’s own admission, its modeling predicts elevated lead in the air and that lead dust will land on the ground. But lead does not break down and can linger on yards, gardens, and play areas for years.
“The Army left 68 drums of a primary explosive to degrade for 20 years until it decided that the only safe option was to blow them up near populated areas. That turned the Army’s storage management failure into a public health catastrophe for people in at least two states,” said Earthjustice Attorney Thien Chau. “Instead of keeping people safe, the Army is knowingly blanketing communities with lead and putting the burden on families to understand and mitigate the risks of the massive plume of lead dust.”
Many Iowans, including those in the path of the plume, say they did not receive information about the potential lead exposure and what they could do to protect their homes and families, until days before the planned detonation.
“It is obvious that the Army is proceeding recklessly with this action. Iowans deserve to see an Environmental and Health Impact Statement and investigation into better alternatives than this extreme planned explosion. We’re hoping the Judge halts this action before it takes place tomorrow,” said Wally Taylor, attorney and legal chair for the Sierra Club Iowa Chapter.
Public health experts say that even tiny amounts of lead can permanently harm a child’s developing brain, lowering IQ, and causing learning and behavior problems that can last a lifetime. More than 400,000 deaths are attributable to lead exposure each year in the United States, with more than 250,000 deaths from lead-linked cardiovascular disease. Lead exposure during pregnancy is particularly dangerous as it can cause premature births.
“It very well could be the case that the U.S. Army has no other safe alternative to disposing of the toxic lead azide,” said Sarah Green, executive director at the Iowa Environmental Council. “But our federal government needs to do better at communicating the potential health risks of lead contamination in our communities and providing more time to keep our communities and vulnerable groups safe, like children and pregnant women.”
The complaint filed in the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals highlights EPA’s failure to require the Army to consider safer treatment alternatives as well as the lack of permit conditions that are adequately protective of human health. It also highlights the Army’s total failure to give adequate public notice of its plans, which it published a mere five days before the scheduled detonation.
For decades, military bases have relied on a practice known as open burning/open detonation, or OB/OD, to destroy excess, unserviceable, or obsolete military munitions, including small arms cartridges, rockets, mortars, and missiles. The practice is outdated, and EPA and the National Academy of Sciences have identified safer alternative technologies.
