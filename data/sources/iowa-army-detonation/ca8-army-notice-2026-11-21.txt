RESPONDENTS’ NOTICE OF THE ARMY’S INTENT TO RESPOND TO
 IMMEDIATE THREAT UNDER RCRA’S EMERGENCY EXCLUSION

      The Government notifies the Court that the Department of the Army (“Army”)

intends to invoke the emergency munitions and explosives exclusion to EPA’s

regulatory and permitting Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (“RCRA”)

requirements, under which the Army may perform treatment by controlled

detonation of the lead azide waste without the Emergency Permit that is challenged

in this petition. See 40 C.F.R. §§ 264.1(g)(8)(i)(D), 270.1(c)(3)(i)(D). There is an

immediate risk that the lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (the

“Facility”) will explode as temperatures are expected to drop below 30 degrees

Fahrenheit in the next several days. The Army must perform a controlled detonation

                                         1
to protect the public from the more significant threat from an uncontrolled explosion,

and the Army intends to act by November 24, 2026. A controlled detonation allows

the Army to implement measures to minimize the risk to the public, including the

risk from the ejection of partial unexploded ordnances that can explode later from

the Facility. The Army intends to act consistent with the terms of the Emergency

Permit and its mitigation measures even if not in effect. That includes mitigation

measures to minimize the risk of the dispersal of lead dust. In short, a controlled

detonation is by far safer than an uncontrolled explosion.

      The emergency exception does not depend on the Emergency Permit and is

therefore not affected by, nor contradictory to, the terms of this Court’s

administrative stay. But out of an abundance of caution, the Army provides this

notice to explain, for the Court’s benefit, the immediate threat of an imminent

explosion of lead azide at the Facility.

I.    The Army does not need a permit under EPA’s RCRA regulations to
      conduct a controlled detonation of the lead azide at the Facility in this
      emergency situation.

      There is an immediate risk that the lead azide at the Facility will

spontaneously explode. See Declaration of Col Aaron Teller. The waste has

deteriorated to a degree that it is extremely sensitive to friction from crystal

formation. Id. ¶ 9. The barrels holding the waste cannot be removed due to the risk

of explosion, which prevents the Army from taking other measures to reduce that


                                           2
risk. Id. ¶¶ 12-13. As the outside temperatures drop the risk of an uncontrolled

explosion increases because the formed crystals can break more easily in freezing

temperatures. Id. ¶ 13. If the crystals break, this could cause a chain reaction

detonation that could cause an uncontrolled dispersal of lead azide into the

environment. Id. ¶ 15. Temperatures have already dropped below 30 degrees

Fahrenheit, with temperature swings as low as 24 degrees and high as 70 degrees.

Id. ¶¶ 28-29. These recent temperature swings have dramatically increased the risk

of an uncontrolled explosion and threat to public safety. Id. ¶ 29. Further, the

bunkers, where the barrels are located, are now more vulnerable to temperature

changes because the blast doors were removed in preparation of the controlled

detonation and cannot be replaced. Id. ¶ 27.

      Under these circumstances, EPA’s RCRA regulations provide an exemption

from both substantive and permitting requirements to allow immediate action. The

RCRA regulations provide that the substantive standards “do not apply to”:

      a person engaged in treatment or containment activities during
      immediate response to . . . [a]n immediate threat to human health,
      public safety, property, or the environment, from the known or
      suspected presence of military munitions, other explosive material, or
      an explosive device, as determined by an explosive or munitions
      emergency response specialist as defined in 40 C.F.R. § 260.10.

40 C.F.R. § 264.1(g)(8)(i)(D). The same exclusion from permitting requirements is

at 40 C.F.R. § 270.1(c)(3)(i)(D).



                                        3
      Here, an explosive or munitions emergency specialist has made the requisite

determination. Teller Decl. ¶ 3. Thus, EPA’s RCRA regulations allow the Army to

conduct a controlled detonation of the lead azide at the Facility even if the

Emergency Permit that is at issue in this case is not in effect. As will be addressed

further in the Government’s response to Petitioners’ stay motion that the

Government will file tomorrow, a controlled detonation is the safest way to prevent

an uncontrolled explosion of the lead azide. See Teller Declaration ¶¶ 18-25. Any

other options require handling the lead azide in a way that poses an unacceptable

risk of an uncontrolled explosion. Id. ¶¶ 6-17.

      A controlled detonation minimizes the risk associated with the resulting

explosion. Id. ¶¶ 16-25. During a controlled detonation, the Army can implement

a full suite of mitigation efforts including, providing advance public notice;

performing the detonation under favorable weather conditions (e.g., less wind, light

rain) to mitigate the spread of lead dust; wetting to reduce lead dust dispersal;

treating the lead to prevent only a partial detonation; facilitating any necessary

community evacuation; and monitoring to identify where any lead dust may spread

and where any environmental response actions may be needed. Id. ¶¶ 18-25. Indeed,

in preparation for the controlled detonation, the Army has already constructed

protective barriers around the munitions bunkers to minimize the escape of blast

material. Id. ¶ 21.


                                         4
      A spontaneous explosion, on the other hand, is much riskier. In an

uncontrolled explosion, the Army cannot implement its robust mitigation efforts.

Failing to treat all the lead azide, for example, could result in a random dispersal of

explosive material. Id. ¶ 17. This partially exploded lead azide could explode later

—a second spontaneous explosion—creating extremely unsafe conditions for the

public and for personnel conducting post-detonation cleanup. Id. Unfavorable winds

could increase the dispersion of lead dust in the community. Id. ¶ 25. And without

public notice, the public may panic upon the spontaneous explosion. A controlled

detonation is unquestionably much more protective to the public than an

uncontrolled explosion.

      A controlled detonation is necessary now to respond to the immediate threat

of an uncontrolled explosion of military munitions at the Facility. EPA’s regulations

allow the Army’s planned action without the need for a permit and the Army intends

to act on November 24, 2026.

II.   The administrative stay does not bar the Army’s emergency response.

      A stay and an injunction do different things. A stay “operates upon the

judicial proceeding itself” by “temporarily divesting an order of enforceability,”

while an injunction “is directed at someone, and governs that party’s conduct.” Nken

v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 428–29 (2009). The Court’s order stays EPA’s Emergency

Permit. It suspends the authority the Permit conferred, but it does not enjoin or


                                          5
otherwise prohibit the Army from acting under a separate legal authority that does

not depend on the Emergency Permit. Because 40 C.F.R. § 264.1(g)(8)(i) and

§ 270.1(c)(3) of EPA’s regulations allow the Army’s planned action without a

permit, the stay does not reach it.

      RCRA’s emergency exclusion controls here. An uncontrolled detonation of

nearly five tons of primary explosive would endanger soldiers, contractors, and the

surrounding communities, all without many of the protective measures meant to

minimize the risks from the blast—harm that cannot be undone. Teller Decl. ¶¶ 16-

17. The environmental and health interests Petitioners seek to protect plainly are

better served by a controlled detonation than by an uncontrolled explosion, which

would not allow for wetting the blast material, control the timing to align with

favorable weather conditions, and prior public notification, and could scatter

partially destroyed lead azide. Id. ¶¶ 14–15, 17–21. The real choice, in other words,

is not between a detonation and no detonation. It is between a planned and mitigated

detonation and an unplanned and unmitigated explosion. To protect its personnel

and the surrounding communities, the Army needs to take swift action. Protection

of human health and the environment demand action now, not delay.
