Public Safety Advisory: Controlled, Mitigated Detonation of Unstable
By Lori McFate, Public and Congressional Affairs Specialist, Army Sustainment Command
November 24, 2026
Share on Twitter

To protect the public from an uncontrolled, spontaneous explosion, on Wednesday, November 25, 2026 at approximately 9:00 a.m. CST, the U.S. Army Sustainment Command plans to conduct a controlled detonation of unstable, degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP) located in Middletown, Iowa. A more precise time will be communicated after evaluating changing weather patterns and waiting for the safest possible conditions. This controlled, mitigated detonation has been meticulously planned alongside interagency partners across the U.S. Government to significantly mitigate risk to the public, provide critical resources and support, including temporary housing, transportation, meals; and inform the community of resources and actions. The U.S. Army, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), Centers for Disease Control (CDC), Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and other federal partners are taking proactive steps to keep people safe before, during and after the planned detonation.
Evacuation Information and Resources:
Out of an abundance of caution, FEMA is supporting the recommended relocation for all residents of Middletown, Iowa, and immediate surrounding impacted areas commencing on Tuesday, 24 November 2026. This defines the evacuation zone as a 5-mile cone extending southwest of IAAAP consistent with consequence management modeling, developed constantly by the Department of War’s Defense Threat Reduction Agency (DTRA) dispersion and deposition path (referenced below). Out of an abundance of caution, the evacuation extends one mile beyond the dispersion path (referenced below). Individuals in the surrounding area with questions or concerns about the evacuation area and eligibility can contact FEMA at
1-800-621-3362
.
The evacuation support includes transportation to a safe refuge for those who cannot travel on their own; safe, sanitary, and secure shelter, including short-term hotel lodging; and food, water, and other supplies. At this time, lodging will be provided through Friday, November 27, but individuals are encouraged to contact FEMA if greater flexibility is needed. Evacuated individuals will be notified by phone once the all-clear is given and at that point may return home at their convenience. Residents may also check the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant website for dispersion and deposition model status at
https://www.army.mil/asc#org-iowa-army-ammunition-plant
.
Individuals may information related to register for evacuation assistance at
DisasterAssistance.gov
. The FEMA Helpline is available for all individuals who have questions 24/7 at 1-800-621-3362.
For Residents Outside the Evacuation Zone:
Residents who live outside the recommended evacuation zone are encouraged to shelter in place. Best practices include closing all windows and doors, setting heating to recirculate (heat can stay on), turning off fans that pull in outside air (e.g., bathroom vents and kitchen exhausts), keeping small children and pets inside, and covering feed and water for livestock and crops. Infants and children under 6 years old, pregnant women, and individuals with chronic diseases, such as heart disease, dementia, and chronic kidney disease, may be more sensitive to lead exposure and air pollution. For more information about staying safe from lead, visit:
Lead Exposure & Surveillance | Health & Human Services
and
About Childhood Lead Poisoning Prevention | Childhood Lead Poisoning Prevention | CDC
Steps to Take Ahead of the Detonation Time for Persons in the Affected Area:
If it is safe to do so, set heating and cooling systems to recirculate to minimize outdoor air exposure and maximize indoor air filtration until the dispersion passes by your community. If you have an air filter on your furnace, consider replacing it before the detonation with a new one, especially if there are pets in your home or people who smoke. Use a filter rated MERV 11 or higher if possible.
Attention should be given to protecting infants and children under 6 years old, pregnant women, and individuals with chronic diseases, such as heart disease, dementia, and chronic kidney disease, who may be most sensitive to lead exposure and air pollution.
Local and federal officials strongly urge community members to vacate the evacuation area and avoid the affected area— utilizing the resources and support provided through FEMA.
Steps to Take After the Detonation Time for Persons in the Affected Area:
Remain indoors during the potential exposure period if you cannot evacuate beforehand. Keep windows and doors closed.
If it is safe to do so, set heating and cooling systems to “recirculate” to minimize outdoor air exposure and maximize indoor air filtration until the plume passes by your community.
Avoid unnecessary travel through affected areas and evacuation zones.
After officials determine the air exposure period has ended, clean indoor and outdoor surfaces that may be contacted by children. Follow guidance from the Iowa Department of Health and Human Services to protect yourself from lead exposure during clean up. Learn more about actions you can take to protect you and your family from potential exposures to lead at
https://www.epa.gov/lead/actions-reduce-potential-lead-exposure
and
https://www.epa.gov/lead/learn-about-lead
.
In the deposition area, you must do all of the above and wet-clean surfaces that may be contacted by infants or children, including outdoor play equipment, toys, tables and chairs, handrails, patios, decks, exterior windowsills, and other high-contact surfaces.
U.S. Government personnel will return immediately] to conduct post-detonation sampling with results published when available. Until results are available, take precautions as detailed above.
Potential for Exposure:
The U.S. Army and EPA, CDC, FEMA, and other interagency partners are working together to provide the public with actionable information out of an abundance of caution to minimize lead exposure. Deposition of lead-containing dust may occur after the detonation on surfaces within certain areas affected by the detonation’s dispersion. All people, but especially infants, children, pregnant women, adults and children with chronic diseases such as heart disease, kidney disease, or dementia, should avoid outdoor activities within affected areas until further guidance is provided.
Individuals may be exposed to lead from the detonation either from breathing it in or from lead dust that lands on surfaces.
The Army Sustainment Command has published maps
https://www.army.mil/asc#org-iowa-army-ammunition-plant
. that outline the potential for where lead may be present in the air and where lead may be deposited on the ground. These maps reflect the best estimates of where lead may be present in the air and deposit on surfaces. As weather conditions may change, where lead may be present may also change. These maps will be revised as often as possible prior to the detonation based upon weather forecast changes.
Over the next 24 hours, the Army Sustainment Command will release the latest version of these maps:
https://d34w7g4gy10iej.cloudfront.net/pubs/pdf_78382.pdf
Six hours in advance of the detonation and every hour thereafter until the detonation.
After the detonation, the U.S. Army will issue a final estimate of the extent of the affected area.
Soil, air, and water near the site are being extensively tested before and after the detonation to enable Army Sustainment Command to take appropriate action in areas where lead from the detonation is deposited.
A Controlled Mitigated Detonation Minimizes Risk and Protects the Public:
A routine monitoring inspection on March 11, 2026, found that decades-old, stored lead azide had partially crystallized, making it increasingly sensitive to friction, shock, and cold winter temperatures.
This deterioration created a new and growing risk of spontaneous, uncontrolled detonation. Lead azide is a highly sensitive explosive that becomes increasingly unstable as it deteriorates, making it vulnerable to accidental detonation from even slight friction or movement.
An operational planning team, which consisted of experts from the Army, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF), the Department of War Explosives Safety Board, state and local authorities, and emergency-response partners, evaluated several mitigation options, including chemical neutralization, thermal treatment, relocation, and continued storage. After extensive laboratory testing and explosives-safety reviews, each was deemed too dangerous: chemical treatment would require opening and mixing the drums, while thermal treatment and relocation would expose the unstable material to movement, vibration, and impact, generating an unacceptable risk of accidental detonation. Continued storage subjects the community to increased risk as temperatures decrease, leading to an uncontrolled explosion.
Our federal, state, and local partners, elected leaders, and military are united in their commitment to protecting service members, civilian employees, nearby residents, families, businesses, critical infrastructure, and the surrounding environment from the dangers of uncontrolled detonation.
Conducting a controlled mitigated detonation of this unstable lead azide allows us to deliberately manage and minimize the risks, rather than leaving the material vulnerable to an unpredictable detonation—a controlled, mitigated, in-place detonation is the only viable option to minimize the safety, of the public.
This conclusion was a consensus among the most experienced and knowledgeable scientists across the federal government. Conducting this operation is about preventing a potentially catastrophic situation and protecting the American people.
The team overseeing this operation conducted extensive safety and environmental reviews, analyzed potential blast impacts, practiced the planned detonation procedures, and put safety zones, monitoring systems, and emergency-response plans in place. They also worked directly with community members and congressional leaders to keep them informed throughout the process.
The lead azide will be detonated in earth-covered magazines – a reinforced structure made of concrete purpose-built to hold ammunition or explosives. The U.S. Army and its interagency partners have constructed, assembled, and stacked unprecedented innovative mitigation measures, including extensive drone surveillance of lead levels, placement of physical barriers full of sand that are approximately two stories high, a “water wall” and gel blocks to contain particle dispersion, precise deployment of aqueous foam barriers to absorb thermal energy and reduce airborne particulate dispersal, state-of-the-art atmospheric dispersion modeling to determine the design of the controlled detonation. Before and during the controlled detonation, the soil above and surrounding the earth-covered magazines will be thoroughly saturated with water by misters. These measures will reduce the amount of lead that travels from the detonation site through the air and off the installation to minimize deposition outside IAAAP.
The entirety of the federal government remains committed to protecting public safety and health, which are our top priorities.
