How Arab Americans who voted for Trump feel about him and Abdul El-Sayed

Republicans, after making a concerted effort at outreach to Arab Americans in 2024, have used alienating rhetoric about the group throughout this election cycle.

In Michigan, where a toss-up Senate race is underway, President Donald Trump called the Democratic nominee, Abdul El-Sayed, a “jihadist” who was “on the side of the terrorists” and would “have to go through a metal detector before he’s allowed to go into the Senate.”

In a Monday campaign stop for Mike Rogers, the race’s Republican nominee, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. insulted Arabs, falsely claiming they fail to produce science and literature.

Arab Americans, who were evenly split in the 2024 presidential race, already were gravitating to Democrats.

In Dearborn, the first Arab American-majority city, many Trump 2024 voters describe El-Sayed as an “America First” candidate due to his opposition to unconditional military aid to Israel and focus on the domestic economy.

El-Sayed himself recently embraced the America First label in a campaign ad on the conservative Fox News and Newsmax networks.

“Mike Rogers will put Israel first,” said Michigan voter Mustapha El-Jaroudi, 27.

Having voted for Trump twice, El-Jaroudi now calls the president “a con artist” who betrayed his campaign refrain of “no new wars.”

Read the stories of El-Jaroudi and other Arab American Trump 2024 voters to learn why they turned away from the president and embraced El-Sayed, a son of Egyptian immigrants.

Some veterans groups sit out fight over Trump’s arch

Several veterans groups have declared neutrality on President Donald Trump’s triumphal arch, breaking from other veterans and advocacy groups that have said the towering structure will harm the experience of visiting nearby Arlington National Cemetery.

“Our organizations … do not write to endorse or oppose any specific architectural design or proposal," the Vietnam Veterans of America and three other associations said in a statement shared with The Washington Post, distancing themselves from efforts by other veterans to halt construction. The groups called for Americans to unify around the prospect of a new monument to honor veterans built in Memorial Circle.

The move comes as U.S. District Judge Tanya S. Chutkan weighs whether to let the arch move forward. A September YouGov poll found that 48 percent of Americans oppose the arch, and 30 percent support the project, with the remaining 22 percent unsure.

The Post on Tuesday published an analysis of how the towering arch’s shadows would move across the graves of veterans and their next of kin from the Vietnam War, Korean War, World War II, World War I, Spanish-American War and Civil War, according to a 3D model.

The symbolism of Trump’s arch overshadowing graves has drawn rebukes from VoteVets, a political organization aligned with Democratic veterans, which last month launched a campaign to mobilize more veterans against the planned 250-foot-tall arch.

Jay Kalner, the president of the Vietnam Veterans of America’s Northern Virginia chapter, and other local VVA leaders oppose the arch, Kalner told The Post on Tuesday.

A federal review panel, which Trump has stocked with allies, is expected to approve the arch as early as Nov. 5. Administration officials hope to begin work immediately afterward.

Chutkan, an appointee of President Barack Obama, is reviewing a lawsuit brought by three Vietnam War veterans who say that the arch would disrupt the experience of visiting the cemetery and interfere with the intent of nearby monuments.

Maryland county approves plan to bring a Sphere to National Harbor

A Sphere entertainment venue is officially coming to Prince George’s County following a vote by the county council Tuesday.

The 8-0 vote authorized the county to issue up to $215 million in special obligation bonds to help build the 6,000-seat entertainment venue, operated by Sphere Entertainment Co. The venue, a smaller version of the famed Sphere in Las Vegas, will cost approximately $1.4 billion to build.

Sphere National Harbor is estimated to generate $63 million for Prince George’s in annual taxes once operational, according to an economic impact report released by the county.

Throughout negotiations between county leaders and Sphere Entertainment, county workers and labor unions pushed for more protections for workers during construction.

There is currently a labor peace agreement that protects individuals who will work at the Sphere once it’s operational. Prince George’s County Executive Aisha Braveboy (D) said that is not outside the norm for Sphere Entertainment.

“They have never entered into what’s called a project labor agreement, and they did not do so here on this project,” she said at a news conference after the council’s vote.

Braveboy added that “everyone, including the unions we talked to, expect to work on this project, and they will.”

Egypt’s crackdown on press draws criticism on Capitol Hill

A Cairo court Tuesday moved to shut down Mada Masr, one of the last independent publications in Egypt, on the grounds that its editor, Lina Attalah, was running the news site without a license.

The court fined Attalah the equivalent of $53,400 and ordered her detained until the fine was paid later that day, a Mada Masr representative told The Washington Post, speaking on the condition of anonymity for fear of reprisal.

Attalah is set to appeal the decision, arguing that the site had applied for a license twice but received no response to its application, the representative said.

The incident comes after a crackdown on the press last month: Egyptian authorities arrested the entire newsroom of the fact-checking site Matsda2sh on charges that it was affiliated with the Muslim Brotherhood.

The Islamist organization was banned in Egypt after the country’s current president, Abdel Fatah El-Sisi, took power in a 2013 coup. Sisi, whom President Donald Trump has called “my favorite dictator,” has since crushed dissent, banned protests and imprisoned thousands including journalists and human rights advocates.

There are 25 journalists behind bars in Egypt, according to the Committee to Protect Journalists, a New York-based rights group.

The arrests have drawn the attention of Congress, with Rep. Rashida Tlaib (D-Michigan) calling for the release of the fact-checkers.

“This assault on the free press must end NOW. Journalism is not a crime,” Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Maryland) said in a post on social media after Attalah was initially summed last week.

Trump to greet Venezuelan oil shipment, calling it a ‘gift.’ The U.S. paid for it.

President Donald Trump, campaigning for Republicans in hotly contested Texas, claimed Tuesday that he was poised to receive “a gift” of 1 million barrels of oil from Venezuela, following what he described as the U.S. takeover of the country.

The big shipment of Venezuelan crude due at a Texas port, however, is not free — but rather, deeply discounted.

The shipment is the first under the contract signed in August between the United States and North American Blue Energy Partners, a firm controlled by the Venezuelan billionaire Alejandro Betancourt. Under the deal, the U.S. has the right to purchase 20 percent of NABEP’s crude at $30 a barrel — or less than a third of the current market price, according to two people familiar with the shipment who spoke on the condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to speak to the press.

As part of the U.S. agreement, NABEP secured rights to vast reserves in Venezuela.

The crude arriving Tuesday is set to arrive on two different tankers. Oil experts have said the deal is not expected to have a significant impact on U.S. gas prices.

U.S. forces captured Venezuela’s former president, Nicolás Maduro, on Jan 3, and Trump has since backed Maduro’s No. 2, Delcy Rodriguez, as interim president. Trump’s words on Tuesday suggested the extent to which the White House is seeking to leverage its influence over oil-rich Venezuela for political benefit at a time of high gas prices amid the ongoing conflict with Iran.

“We took over Venezuela very successfully and they’ve sent me as a gift a boatload of a million barrels of oil and I’m going to give that to the United States of America,” Trump told reporters in Texas. He added, “we’re going to accept a million barrels of oil. And I’m going to give it to you people to use it the way you want and oil prices are going to be dropping very sharply very soon.”

The FEC’s filing system is down a week before the election. The agency won’t say why.

One week before the midterm elections, the Federal Election Commission’s electronic filing system — where campaigns, parties and PACs report their fundraising and spending — went offline Monday night and remained down Tuesday afternoon.

The outage comes during a period of accelerated disclosure. In the 20 days before an election, House and Senate candidates must report contributions or loans of $1,000 or more within 48 hours. Political committees and other groups must report certain spending totaling $1,000 or more within 24 hours after they are publicly disseminated.

FEC officials declined to comment on what caused the outage, including whether it was a hack.

In a statement Tuesday, the agency said its information technology staff was “actively working to restore service.” The FEC’s website, where the public can search filed reports, was not affected by the outage, the agency said.

The FEC began investigating an issue with its online systems around 9:30 p.m. Monday, according to the agency’s online status page, and confirmed that its filing system was down around 11 p.m.

The outage comes as the agency is badly diminished. The six-member commission has lacked the four members required for official commission action since May 2025. It has been unable to vote on enforcement matters, regulations, advisory opinions and audits, and it has had only two commissioners, both Democrats, since October 2025.

President Donald Trump nominated two Republicans to the commission in February, but the Senate has not confirmed them. The quorum loss does not shut down the agency: Career staff continue to process disclosure filings and carry out other day-to-day work. But an inspector general report found that the agency’s full-time workforce fell from 363 employees in 2002 to 293 in 2022 — a decline of about 19 percent.

The next major filing deadline for campaign finances will come after the general election on Dec. 3.

Trump declines to unconditionally accept midterm results

President Donald Trump declined to accept the midterm election results unconditionally Tuesday, echoing formulations he has used in past campaigns.

“I will — if they’re honest elections,” he told reporters at Joint Base Andrews in Maryland before taking off for two rallies in Texas.

In 2016, Trump said he would accept the election results “if I win.” In 2020, he repeatedly declined to commit to accept the results, saying “We’re going to have to see what happens.” In 2024, Trump said he would accept the results if the election was “fair and legal and good.”

As Americans across the country start voting early and by mail leading up to Nov. 3, the Trump administration has filed lawsuits laying the groundwork to challenge election results, and media allies are telling supporters to expect cheating. Unsubstantiated allegations directed at mail ballots and voting machines in past elections have given way to a focus on noncitizen voting, which is rare.
