Supreme Court allows New Hampshire student ID ban at the polls to stand

The Supreme Court has let New Hampshire enforce its student-ID voting restriction for the Nov. 3 election, blocking a lower-court injunction issued weeks before voting.

Kevin LandriganUnion Leader Staff

The nation’s highest court has allowed a law blocking the acceptance of student IDs to cast a ballot to stay in place during the Nov. 3 general election while litigation continues.

Reversing rulings from both a lower court judge in Concord and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, the U.S. Supreme Court, in an unsigned decision, said it wasn’t appropriate for those lower courts to set aside state laws weeks before an election.

The court cited the Purcell principle, which holds that orders too close to when people are going to vote can cause confusion.

"Here, the District Court did not point to anything that would justify deviating from that principle," the majority wrote in its opinion.

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, a member of the liberal minority, dissented.

She said U.S. District Court Judge Joseph Laplante with his lower court ruling was helping make sure the public received the most accurate facts about the issue.

"When a federal court determines that material misrepresentations are being made about a state’s election rules, an injunction to prohibit that conduct does not contribute to public confusion, it prevents that very problem," Jackson wrote. "I would not disturb a clearly justifiable federal-court order issued to ensure that the public receives accurate information about a State’s electoral processes."

Gov. Kelly Ayotte signed HB 323, which struck from the books an explicit requirement that election officials recognize college-issued ID cards as a valid form of identification.

In his preliminary ruling, Laplante allowed that law to stand but he said Secretary of State David Scanlan had gone too far with an advisory directive to all election officials that student IDs were not to be considered in any manner.

This directive ignored the fact the Legislature did not remove a separate law that allows student IDs to be considered with other evidence to support someone’s request to register to vote or cast a ballot, Laplante ruled.

Law still faces a trial on the merits

The high court’s decision means, for this election at least, Scanlan’s directive will stand.

“New Hampshire has a responsibility to protect the integrity of its elections and ensure that election laws are applied as written,” Attorney General John Formella said in a statement.

“The Supreme Court’s decision is an important victory for our efforts to defend those laws and prevent last-minute court orders from creating uncertainty for voters and election officials. We will continue to defend New Hampshire’s election laws and the safeguards they provide.”

The Coalition for Open Democracy, a nonpartisan voting rights group, had sued the state charging that the student ID ban was unconstitutional and discriminated against one group of voters.

State Rep. Ross Berry, R-Weare, said unelected judges had no right to overrule the New Hampshire Legislature and allow student ID cards to be accepted at polling places along with other documents to prove age, identity, domicile and U.S. citizenship. DAVID LANE/UNION LEADER

"The U.S. Supreme Court’s order today granting a stay of the injunction issued by the federal district court in Coalition for Open Democracy v. Scanlan is wrong,” Free Speech for People, the coalition’s lawyers, said in a statement.

“The Court’s decision grants a stay which both the federal district court and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit denied, blocking an injunction that has now been in place for almost three weeks, injecting confusion into the election — precisely what the Purcell principle is supposed to prevent — and authorizing an unconstitutional policy that will deprive young voters of their right to vote.”

The group vowed to continue trying to convince the federal court at trial to set the law aside.

House Election Law Committee Chairman Ross Berry, R-Weare and a bill co-sponsor, said it treats all voters the same, requiring anyone to have proof of identification to participate in New Hampshire elections.

"I said it then, and the Supreme Court confirmed it today: you don't change the rules with the election right around the corner," Berry said. "This decision brings clarity and stability at the moment it matters most. It also exposes the same modus operandi Democrats employ every time things don't go their way: whine and sue."

House and Senate Democrats as a group had opposed the bill, charging it was the latest step in a GOP strategy to suppress the student vote that in most elections strongly leans Democratic.
