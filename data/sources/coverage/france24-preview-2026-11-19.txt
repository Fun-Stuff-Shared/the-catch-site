Myanmar intended to destroy ‘substantial part’ of Rohingya, says top UN court - France 24

The United Nations International Court of Justice said on Thursday that Myanmar's forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians during its brutal 2017 crackdown on the Muslim minority. The UN's top court is weighing whether those actions constituted genocide, which would mark the second time it has found that genocide has taken place since the creation of the 1948 UN Genocide Convention.
Myanmar security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians during its brutal 2017 crackdown on the Muslim minority, the International Court of Justice said on Thursday, as it weighs whether such actions constituted genocide.
Myanmar's soldiers killed and raped Rohingya, committed violence against children, and destroyed Rohingya villages and settlements, the top UN court said.
They "killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, perpetrated widespread acts of sexual violence as well as acts of extreme violence against children, and attacked fleeing Rohingya as they sought to flee in large numbers to Bangladesh", said ICJ President Yuji Iwasawa.
'Targeted for destruction': Top UN court hears Rohingya genocide case against Myanmar
The ICJ has not yet ruled whether Myanmar has committed the crime of genocide, in a landmark ruling that is expected to take around three hours to read out.
However, Iwasawa said the "only reasonable inference" was that Myanmar intended to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya population – a key test when determining genocide.
"The court has also determined that the estimate of 10,000 Rohingya violently killed during (2017) clearance operations is supported by the evidence," said Iwasawa.
'Justice'
For the more than one million Rohingya now living in the world's largest refugee camp over the border in Bangladesh, the historic ruling offers a glimmer of hope for accountability.
"I want justice," a 23-year-old Rohingya rape survivor said in the cramped shelter where she lives with her family.
"There should be stringent punishment for those who raped women. The ICJ should say something separately about this," she said, requesting anonymity.
Survivors of the violence brought with them harrowing tales of gang rapes, mass executions, and cruelty towards families and children.
Far from the squalor of the refugee camps, the panel of 15 judges has been weighing for seven years whether Myanmar's actions constitute genocide.
The case has been brought by The Gambia under the 1948 UN Genocide Convention, which allows any state to bring a case against another it suspects of breaching the international agreement set up after World War II.
While the ICJ has no way of enforcing its decisions, a ruling that Myanmar committed genocide would heap more international pressure on its leader Min Aung Hlaing.
He commanded the military during the Rohingya crackdown before ousting the elected government of Aung San Suu Kyi in a 2021 coup.
This year he was installed as civilian president following deeply restricted elections and is waging a diplomatic campaign for legitimacy which may be complicated by the ruling.
Myanmar has always denied genocide, arguing that its actions targeted a military uprising from insurgents after a series of attacks left a dozen security personnel dead.
"Myanmar was not obliged to remain idle and allow terrorists to have free rein," its representative Ko Ko Hlaing told judges during hearings on the case in January.
'Targeted for destruction'
The Gambia's justice minister said the country filed the case "after reviewing credible reports of the most brutal and vicious violence imaginable inflicted upon a vulnerable group that had been dehumanised and persecuted for many years".
The Gambia has the "full backing and support" of the 57-member Organisation of Islamic Cooperation for its efforts at the ICJ, said the minister, Dawda Jallow.
"It is not about esoteric issues of international law, it is about real people, real stories and a real group of human beings," added Jallow.
"The Rohingya of Myanmar. They have been targeted for destruction."
The ICJ has only once before found that a genocide has taken place, the 1995 Srebrenica massacre in Bosnia.
Genocide is notoriously difficult to prove in international law because the Convention requires proof of a specific "intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group".
The Gambia argued during the hearings that genocidal intent was clear from the "organised nature and execution" of the crackdown, its "extreme brutality", the "pervasive sexual violence", and the targeting of children.
Legal experts are watching the case closely for clues as to how the ICJ will handle another seminal case before it – accusations by South Africa that Israel committed genocide against Palestinians in Gaza.
Survivor Mohammed Osman said he was hoping the verdict would allow him to go back home and perform last rites for his parents and younger brother killed by the Myanmar military.
"They shot my father dead, forced my mother into a house with a few other women, raped them and burnt down our homestead," Osman, 20, told AFP.
