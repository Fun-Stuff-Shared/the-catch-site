Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya minority, UN's top court rules

19 November 2026

Summary

  • We have ended our live coverage - you can read more in our news story

  • Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya minority population, the International Court of Justice (ICJ) finds

  • The verdict - decided by a panel of judges from around the world - is the first time a state has ever been found guilty by an international court of committing genocide

  • In his summarising remarks, Judge Yuji Iwasawa says Myanmar had the "intention to destroy" a substantial part of the Rohingya population

  • He adds that it engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence", and failed to investigate, prosecute and punish people who committed genocide offences

  • The judgment is hugely symbolic - but it does not, on its own, bring the Rohingya safely home, our correspondent at The Hague explains

  • In 2017, a military crackdown killed thousands of Rohingya and forced more than 700,000 to flee for neighbouring Bangladesh. Myanmar has denied the claims made in the case, saying it was responding to attacks by armed groups

  • Our South East Asia correspondent describes what he saw unfold

Live Reporting

Edited by Jenna Moon, with reporting from Anna Holligan and BBC Burmese's Wai Phyo Aye at The Hague and South East Asia correspondent Jonathan Head

  1. Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya Muslims, top UN court rulespublished at 16:27 GMT 19 November
    16:27 GMT 19 November

    Malu Cursino
    Live reporter

    The International Court of Justice (ICJ) has ruled that Myanmar committed genocide during the country’s military campaign against the Rohingya group.

    Judge Yuji Iwasawa delivered the top UN court’s judgment in a session spanning close to three hours. The court found Myanmar had the “intention to destroy” a substantial part of the Rohingya population during its military crackdown in 2017.

    The court found that around 10,000 Rohingya were "violently killed during clearance operations" in 2017. More than 700,000 people fled to Bangladesh.

    A panel of judges voted on 12 motions to reach the verdict. While the judges found Myanmar committed genocide and failed to prevent it, the ICJ found Myanmar had not "directly and publicly" incited genocide against the Rohingya group.

    The Gambia brought the case to the ICJ. Philippe Sands KC, a human rights lawyer part of the Gambian legal team called the judgment “historic”. It's "right up there with the great moments of international justice: Nuremberg, Pinochet, and others", he says.

    The BBC’s south east Asia correspondent, Jonathan Head, recalls his visit to Rakhine state back in 2017.

    While the judgment marks an important moment for the Rohingya community, it is largely symbolic, as the ICJ does not have the power to enforce it.

    Myanmar has consistently denied claims of genocide and has maintained it was responding to attacks by armed groups.

    We’re now wrapping up our live coverage but you can read more on this story in our news article.

  2. Ruling 'important step towards ending impunity', says UN Special Rapporteurpublished at 16:22 GMT 19 November
    16:22 GMT 19 November

    BBC Burmese Service

    I've been speaking to Kelley Anne Eckels-Currie, the UN's Special Rapporteur on human rights in Myanmar, following the ICJ’s landmark ruling.

    "This is a very powerful verdict," she says.

    "It is a very important step towards ending impunity, which has been a problem with the Myanmar forces for decades, and towards accountability for the perpetrators, and justice for the survivors and victims," Eckels-Currie adds.

    The Rohingya community will be disappointed that the court didn't find that citizenship was within the scope of remedies it could provide, but will welcome compensation and reparations, the she explains.

  3. Verdict being closely watched in Israelpublished at 16:11 GMT 19 November
    16:11 GMT 19 November

    Katy Watson
    Reporting from Jerusalem

    Today’s ruling could give clues as to whether Israel could face something similar to Myanmar - the case is certainly been watched closely here in Jerusalem.

    In 2023, South Africa brought a case against Israel arguing that since 7 October, it had violated its obligations under the Genocide Convention in relation to Palestinians in the Gaza Strip.

    Israel has strongly rejected accusations of genocide, arguing it seeks to minimise civilian casualties.

    But now we’ve had this ruling, this argument will likely be heavily scrutinised.

    "The Court’s clarification that genocidal intent can co-exist with other goals, including military aims, is especially significant for other cases pending at the ICJ," says head of Amnesty International Agnès Callamard.

    Still, it’s unlikely we will get another ruling any time soon - proceedings at the ICJ take years.

    16:02 GMT 19 November

    The Gambia's president, Adama Barrow, says the International Court of Justice's ruling marks "a historic day for international justice".

    In a statement from his office, the president praised the courage of the Rohingya survivors and witnesses, saying their "testimony made this outcome possible" and their perseverance "has been vindicated".

    The Gambia launched the case against Myanmar in November 2019, accusing Myanmar of breaching the 1948 Genocide Convention.

  5. Myanmar ruling is 'a demonstration of international justice', UN chief sayspublished at 15:55 GMT 19 November
    15:55 GMT 19 November

    UN Secretary General António Guterres says the Myanmar ruling is "a demonstration of international justice" and reaffirms that "impunity for such horrific acts must not stand".

    He says in a statement that the verdict is binding, final and without appeal, adding "Myanmar must act in accordance with its obligation to comply with the court’s decision".

    "I reiterate that breaking the cycle of impunity in Myanmar and ensuring accountability are essential to national reconciliation and durable peace," Guterres adds.

  6. Finding that genocide committed through sexual violence significant, lawyer sayspublished at 15:42 GMT 19 November
    15:42 GMT 19 November

    In its judgment the court said rape, gang rape and mass rape against Rohingya women and girls had caused physical harm and psychological trauma impacting the ability of both sexes to reproduce. It also noted marriage and birth restrictions on the Rohingya people.

    "It's difficult to overstate the importance of the court’s findings on sexual and gender-based violence,” says Marianne Vitug, legal adviser at the Global Justice Center.

    "By making it clear that genocide can be committed through rape, reproductive violence, and other gender-based acts, the court is acknowledging how gender and reproductive capacity influence the commission of genocide."

    15:31 GMT 19 November

    The court's ruling today largely focused on the Rakhine state, where most of the Rohingya population lived before 2017.

  8. South Africa's case against Israel 'elephant in the room' at ICJ - top lawyerpublished at 15:15 GMT 19 November
    15:15 GMT 19 November

    Earlier, we brought you comments from Phillipe Sands KC, a top human rights lawyer. He has called a separate ICJ case about Israel's military operations in Gaza the "elephant in the room" at the court.

    The ICJ is currently hearing a case brought by South Africa that accuses Israeli forces of genocide.

    Sands tells BBC Radio 4's World and One programme that Myanmar being found responsible for genocide against the Rohingya doesn't mean the ICJ will find there was a genocide in Gaza.

    But if the court had gone the other way and not found Myanmar guilty, South Africa's case against Israel "would, in effect, be over," he says.

    "The evidentiary standard has been defined very clearly and it will be now for the parties in that next case and in other cases to meet those standards."

    The proceedings could take years to reach a conclusion. Israel has called the case "wholly unfounded" and based on "biased and false claims".

    14:51 GMT 19 November

    Wai Phyo Aye
    BBC Burmese

    So far there has been no official response from Myanmar about today’s historic verdict.

  11. This is a historic judgement, top lawyer on Gambian team tells BBCpublished at 14:14 GMT 19 November
    14:14 GMT 19 November

    Philippe Sands KC, a top human rights lawyer who is part of the Gambian legal team that brought the case, tells BBC Radio 4's the World at One that this is a "historic judgment".

    It's "right up there with the great moments of international justice: Nuremberg, Pinochet, and others", he says.

    It is, however, "bittersweet" for Rohingya survivors, he says. "I've been with two or three dozen of them in court all morning, and you can imagine the emotion that they felt."

    Earlier we noted the court had imposed "measures of restitution" on Myanmar. Sands says this means Myanmar has been given two years to allow the Rohingya to return to their homes.

    That's difficult as most have been destroyed, he explains, so if it can't happen there will be a "full assessment" by the court of what reparations are due.

  12. Recap: The judgements voted on by the ICJpublished at 14:02 GMT 19 November
    14:02 GMT 19 November

    The International Court of Justice has voted on several judgements relating to military operations that killed thousands of Rohingya Muslims in Myanmar in 2017.

    Here's a reminder of how the panel of judges voted:

    • Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya group in northern Rakhine state - by nine votes to six
    • Myanmar violated its obligation to prevent genocide by individuals alongside its security forces - by nine votes to six
    • Myanmar has not "directly and publicly" incited genocide against the Rohingya group in the northern Rakhine state - by 14 votes to one
    • Myanmar violated its obligation to punish genocide and other acts listed under article three of the Genocide Convention - by 11 votes to four
    • Myanmar violated its obligation to enact the necessary legislation to give effect to the provisions of the Genocide Convention - by 14 votes to one
    • Myanmar must cease any ongoing violation of its obligations under Article 5 of the convention on the prevention and punishment of the crime of genocide by enacting legislation - by 14 votes to one
    • Myanmar must take "immediate and effective steps" to investigate "all persons reasonably suspected of having committed acts contrary to Article 3 of the convention" - by 13 votes to two
    • Myanmar must "provide assurances and guarantees of non-repetition of the violation of its obligations" under the Genocide Convention and prevent similar violations from happening in the future - by nine votes to six
    • Myanmar must adopt "measures of restitution" as laid out in the judgment - by 10 votes to five
    • Myanmar is under obligation to compensate members of the Rohingya group - by 10 votes to five
    • The amount and modalities of compensation or other reparation due to the Rohingya group should be settled by the ICJ - by 10 votes to five
    • Myanmar violated its obligation to prevent the destruction and ensure the preservation of evidence related to alleged genocidal acts - by 11 votes to four

  15. 'No indication' of lowered threshold for genocide, international lawyer tells BBCpublished at 13:35 GMT 19 November
    13:35 GMT 19 November

    Oliver Windridge, a former UN lawyer, disagrees with the US envoy's suggestion that this case has "lowered the legal threshold for proving genocide".

    He says it "demonstrates how the existing threshold can be satisfied when the evidence is sufficiently compelling".

    The judgment shows a state can be held responsible even when evidence of genocidal intent is largely circumstantial, rather than based on an explicit written order or declared policy, Windridge explains.

    While the implications are "wide and far-reaching", he continues, it doesn't automatically determine the outcome of future cases.

    Each case must be assessed "on its own facts, evidence and legal arguments".

  16. US diplomat earlier argued case could 'lower threshold for proving genocide'published at 13:27 GMT 19 November
    13:27 GMT 19 November

    Anna Holligan
    Reporting from The Hague

    The guilty verdict will be controversial in some quarters.

    The US ambassador to the UN, Mike Waltz, last month accused The Gambia's designated judge Navi Pillay of "tipping the scales" to "lower the legal threshold for proving genocide so that [a] weakened and false standard can later be weaponized against American and allied soldiers whenever they raise a finger against terrorists that hide behind civilians".

    A White House-aligned think tank predicts the US might impose sanctions on the ICJ. But even with the guilty verdict reached today, it would be wrong to say the ICJ is simply "lowering the threshold".

    To prove genocide you have to show attackers specifically intended to destroy a protected group, in whole or in part. Because leaders rarely admit this openly, judges have to figure out intent based on their actions - and that's where the legal dispute lies.

    Pillay, who won this year's Nobel Peace Prize, has not publicly responded to Waltz's remark.

  17. UK politicians call on government to resume sanctions against Myanmar militarypublished at 13:09 GMT 19 November
    13:09 GMT 19 November

    Responding to the International Court of Justice's guilty ruling, cross-party politicians in the UK have called for the British government to resume sanctions against the Myanmar military.

    Labour MP Rushanara Ali and Tory MP Jeremy Hunt co-chair the All-Party Parliamentary Group (APPG) on Burma. In a joint statement they say today's ruling "must serve to mobilise the international community to do everything it can to protect the Rohingya, and the entire population of Myanmar, from continued violations of international law by the Myanmar military".

    The pair call for the government to resume "regular rounds of sanctions" against the country's military, and to bring a resolution to the UN Security Council "compelling enforcement action".

    Pointing to the refugee crisis caused by the persecution of the Rohingya group out of Myanmar, Ali and Hunt say the government should "significantly increase aid to Rohingya refugees".

  18. What I saw in Myanmar with my own eyes back in 2017published at 12:58 GMT 19 November
    12:58 GMT 19 November

    Jonathan Head
    South East Asia correspondent

    Back in 2017, I visited the Rakhine state in Myanmar, where hundreds of thousands of people fled to Bangladesh weeks after violence erupted across the country.

    On arrival at Sittwe, the Rakhine state capital, we were given instructions. No-one was to leave the group and try to work independently. There was a curfew at 18:00 local time, so no wandering after dark.

    We could request to go to places that interested us; in practice we found such requests were rejected on grounds of security. To be fair, I believe they were genuinely concerned for our safety.

    The government's purpose in bringing us was to balance the overwhelmingly negative narrative coming from the Rohingya refugees arriving in Bangladesh, who had almost all spoken of a deliberate campaign of destruction by the Myanmar military and Rakhine mobs, and appalling human rights abuses.

    But right away these efforts faltered.

  19. Facebook has long been accused of amplifying hatepublished at 12:48 GMT 19 November
    12:48 GMT 19 November

    We heard earlier the court was not "fully convinced" that Myanmar's army had incited genocide on Facebook.

    This has been a contentious claim for many years.

    In 2021, dozens of refugees in the UK and US sued Facebook, accusing the platform of letting hate speech against them to spread and demanding more than $150bn in compensation.

    The following year an Amnesty report argued "dangerous algorithms and reckless pursuit of profit substantially contributed to the atrocities".

    The refugees' lawsuit was dismissed this spring, with a court finding US law gives protection to online platforms over third-party posts.

    In 2021 Meta said it had built a team of Burmese speakers, banned Myanmar's military and taken "action on harmful misinformation to help keep people safe".

  20. The ICJ can't enforce its guilty rulingpublished at 12:40 GMT 19 November
    12:40 GMT 19 November

    Anna Holligan
    Reporting from The Hague

    No state has previously been found responsible for genocide by this court. For Rohingya survivors and the hundreds of thousands living in refugee camps in Bangladesh, it is formal international recognition that what happened to them amounted to genocide.

    Legally, Myanmar has been ordered to stop any ongoing violations, pay reparations to victims, and give guarantees that it will not repeat them.

    But it is important to be clear: this isn't a criminal trial. The ICJ cannot send individual generals to prison. And there's no enforcement mechanism. The court relies on the UN Security Council and political pressure to make its rulings stick. So for many Rohingya, the judgment is hugely symbolic - but it does not, on its own, bring them safely home.

    It also does not directly imprison Myanmar’s commanders: the ICJ adjudicates state responsibility, while the ICC prosecutes individuals.

    But a genocide finding could add legal and diplomatic weight to separate criminal proceedings and to calls for enforcement through the UN Security Council.
