Trump administration sues to block Alaska’s US Senate election result if Peltola wins
The move is the latest attempt by the President to support incumbent Republican U.S. Sen. Dan S. Sullivan against a Democratic challenger
U.S. Sen. Dan Sullivan, R-Alaska, speaks at a rally featuring President Donald Trump on Tuesday, Oct. 13, 2026, in Wasilla. On Oct. 22, Trump ordered the U.S. Department of Justice to file a lawsuit that would prohibit anyone but Sullivan from being certified as the winner of the U.S. Senate election. (James Brooks photo/Alaska Beacon)
The U.S. Department of Justice filed suit against the Alaska Division of Elections late Thursday, seeking to block the division from certifying the result of the state’s U.S. Senate election if the Democratic challenger, Mary Peltola, wins.
President Donald Trump told reporters on Friday in Florida that he ordered the lawsuit be filed.
“I’m the one that said, ‘do it,’” he said.
Peltola is the leading opponent to incumbent Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan, and polls show her with a small lead over Sullivan, but the race also includes two other people — Republican Gerald Heikes of Palmer and undeclared candidate Dan J. Sullivan of Petersburg.
In the complaint, the Department of Justice claims that Dan J. Sullivan’s candidacy is “frivolous,” and was intended solely to confuse voters and benefit Peltola.
Until there is an investigation to determine the extent of that confusion, the Department of Justice argues, Alaska should not be permitted to certify the result of the ongoing Senate election.
Legal experts immediately dismissed the federal argument as bunk.
‘A sad and scary sight’
Under the U.S. Constitution, states have authority over their own elections. The federal claim is based on the preamble to the National Voter Registration Act, which states in part that the law is intended “to protect the integrity of the electoral process.”
In an immediate analysis posted online, Notre Dame Law School election law professor Derek Muller said, “the legal argument is one of the worst ones I’ve ever seen.”
In addition to being poorly argued, it was poorly written, he said, noting that one paragraph is missing a verb that would ask the court to take action.
Libby Bakalar is a former Alaska state elections attorney who frequently speaks against the Trump administration. On social media Friday morning, she said the federal argument “is laughably bad, almost to the point of sanctionably frivolous. Watching MAGA on the ropes like this in court up here is a sad and scary sight.”
The case has been referred to Judge Sharon Gleason, an Obama appointee who is the most senior of the two judges in the U.S. District Court for the District of Alaska.
Trump says he made the call to file the lawsuit
In numerous prior interviews, Dan J. Sullivan has said that his candidacy was motivated by dissatisfaction with Sen. Sullivan, and that he is not linked to the Democratic Party or Peltola’s campaign.
Democratic officials have said the same thing.
But that hasn’t stopped Trump and Sen. Sullivan — as well as other Republicans — from accusing both Peltola and Petersburg Sullivan of fraud.
Trump and other administration officials hinted throughout the summer and fall that they were working to bring a criminal case against Dan J. Sullivan, who was a registered Republican in July. He’s now listed as “undeclared,” an independent, in state voter files.
Thursday’s filing does not name him as a defendant.
The listed attorneys for the Department of Justice are Stanley E. Woodward, an associate attorney general in Washington, D.C. and E. Bryan Wilson, an assistant U.S. attorney in Anchorage.
Alaska U.S. Attorney Michael Heyman was at Trump’s rally in Wasilla this month.
“I told him — I just shook his hand, took a picture — I said I hope you’re going to make sure there’s no cheating, because any place you have rank(ed) choice, you have damn bad cheating. And we can’t let that happen. So, we’re going to defend the integrity of Alaska’s elections,” Trump said at the Oct. 13 event.
Legal action isn’t expected until after the election
By phone, Muller said he doesn’t expect the federal government’s case to advance until after Election Day, if at all.
That’s because the lawsuit is based on possible confusion by Dan J. Sullivan voters. If Dan S. Sullivan wins, any confusion didn’t matter. If Peltola wins by a margin bigger than the number of votes for Dan S. Sullivan, the confusion wasn’t decisive.
“The court’s going to sit and wait till after the election to adjudicate it. Presumably, anyway,” Muller said.
Because Alaska uses ranked choice voting, any hypothetical confused voter would simply have to rank both Sullivans to have their vote count the way they want.
“If there’s any confusion, a lot of that is mitigated by ranked choice voting … it’s really not going to affect the outcome of the election simply because of the way ranked choice voting is designed to work,” he said.
Even in a scenario where Judge Gleason declines to dismiss the case and Peltola wins by a smaller margin than the number of first-choice votes for Dan J. Sullivan, Muller said, the federal government would have to find those Dan J. Sullivan voters and prove they were confused.
“It’s an impossible evidentiary question at the end of the day,” he said.
Tom Lamb is a former Republican U.S. Senate candidate who argued in favor of Dan J. Sullivan in an Alaska Supreme Court case earlier this year. He’s preparing to do so in the newly filed federal case too, and said there’s no way to prove what the federal government wants to.
“If I say I voted for Sullivan Jr. and I made a mistake, there’s no way of verifying that,” he said.
Peltola or her campaign may intervene in suit
Reached by email Friday morning, Dan J. Sullivan declined immediate comment, saying the lawsuit came as a surprise and he was still analyzing it. He did not answer additional messages Friday seeking an interview.
The Beacon contacted the Division of Elections and Lt. Gov. Nancy Dahlstrom early Friday and repeatedly throughout the day. Sam Curtis, a spokesman for the Department of Law, said Friday afternoon that the division is “focused on running the ongoing election and giving voters confidence that their votes will count.”
The Department of Law represents the division and lieutenant governor in court.
“The complaint is being reviewed, and we do not have any comment on the litigation,” he said.
A person affiliated with Sen. Sullivan’s campaign said Friday morning that he did not ask the Department of Justice to file the lawsuit and that he was surprised by the act.
Nate Adams, campaign spokesman for Sullivan, said by email, “We’ll let the DOJ complaint run its course. Alaskans can tell the difference between a Democrat sideshow and Senator Sullivan’s strong record of delivering for Alaska, a record he’ll keep building on for the next six years.”
The chair of the Alaska Republican Party, Carmela Warfield, did not return a text message or phone call seeking comment Friday.
Harry Child, a spokesperson for Peltola’s campaign, replied to questions by text message and implied that the campaign may intervene in the lawsuit.
“This is a despicable political threat to overturn an election, designed by Senator Dan Sullivan’s allies to suppress the voices of Alaskans because Sullivan is terrified he’ll lose the seat that has allowed him to enrich himself at Alaskans’ expense for over a decade,” Child said. “We will fight to ensure every single eligible voter in Alaska can make their voice heard and that no bureaucrat or politician from the Lower 48 can interfere with our lawful, secure elections.”
Asked his opinion, Alaska Democratic Party chair Eric Croft said, “Dan Sullivan is scared he’ll lose and that’s why he’s looking to any corrupt, desperate method to win.”
Review of primary election results indicates few voters were confused
Alaska’s state courts have already argued — and decided — Dan J. Sullivan’s eligibility as a candidate. In June, the Alaska Division of Elections struck him from the primary ballot, saying his candidacy was “for the purpose of confusing or misleading voters.”
He appealed that decision, and in late June, an Anchorage Superior Court judge overturned the division’s decision.
“The director’s assertion that Mr. Sullivan seeks to confuse or misguide voters is not supported by a preponderance of evidence,” wrote Judge Thomas Matthews in a 32-page analysis.
The state appealed, but the Alaska Supreme Court upheld the trial court’s decision on June 29. It has not yet published a full explanation of its decision.
The state did not seek an appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court.
After that decision, Peltola, Dan J. Sullivan, Dan S. Sullivan and 13 other candidates appeared on the primary ballot in August. Peltola finished first, with 49.5% of the vote, followed by Dan S. Sullivan with 41.4% and Dan J. Sullivan with 2.5%.
The fourth-place finisher, Democratic candidate David Leslie, withdrew from the election, an act that promoted Heikes into the final four with 1.1% of the vote.
A subsequent review of the results by the Anchorage Press found a large proportion of votes for Dan J. Sullivan came from left-leaning districts, indicating that Democratic-inclined voters — rather than confused Republicans — put the Petersburg man into the general election.
“I feel pretty vindicated that it seems to prove that there was very little, if any, voter confusion between the senator and myself,” Dan J. Sullivan told local radio station KFSK-FM after the result.
