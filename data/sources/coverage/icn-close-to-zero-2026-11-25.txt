After Detonating Five Tons of Lead Explosives in Iowa, Army Says Health Risks Are ‘Close to Zero’
Mitigation measures helped limit the release of lead from Wednesday’s explosion, Army and EPA officials say.

Anika Jane Beamer

The U.S. Army detonated five tons of degraded lead azide explosives in southeast Iowa this morning, following delays and legal challenges over concerns about toxic dust generated by the blast.
Shortly after 9 a.m. local time, a series of explosions ignited the bomb-making material, sending two plumes into the air that were visible for miles. Cars passed along the north end of the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown
a minute
before the detonation, with more traffic passing soon afterward.
After the blast, Environmental Protection Agency administrator Lee Zeldin said initial air samples collected downwind of the explosion showed no detectable lead. “It looks like it caved inward perfectly,” he told CNN. At an 11 a.m. briefing, Army officials also said initial air monitoring had detected no airborne lead.

But some scientists cautioned against drawing broad conclusions from the initial results, questioning whether the Army’s monitoring could totally rule out lead exposure from a blast of this scale.
Measuring airborne lead is more complicated than a single reading might suggest, said Bruce Lanphear, an expert on lead exposure and professor of health sciences at Simon Fraser University in British Columbia. The monitoring methods used, the size of the particles being measured and the height and location of monitoring equipment can all affect the results.
“The failure to detect an increase in airborne lead from preliminary samples does not establish that no lead was released or that no one was exposed,” Lanphear said in an email exchange with Inside Climate News on Wednesday. Lanphear submitted an expert witness statement in support of an
emergency lawsuit
filed last week on behalf of the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club’s Iowa Chapter.
“We need to know how lead was measured and the detection limits of the sampling methods. We should also await the results of other environmental samples before drawing conclusions.”
After the blast, wind gusts traveled southwest.
Members of the U.S. Army and the Des Moines County emergency services go door-to-door checking on the welfare of residents after the detonation on Wednesday in Middletown. Credit: Scott Olson/Getty Images
The force of the blast could be felt at least
four miles away
, according to a KWQC-TV reporter observing the detonation from a field north of the ammunition plant. The haze from the plumes dissipated quickly, but officials are tracking air quality into Missouri.
The Army says that extensive mitigation reduced the release of lead.
“I would characterize the controlled detonation as exceeding expectations,” said Major General Eric Shirley. “I believe, absolutely, that we reduced as close to zero as is absolutely humanly possible the risk to the health and safety of the people of Middletown and the extended communities throughout Iowa.”
The EPA’s air monitoring
results
are being rounded to one decimal place, said Alexander More, a professor of urban public health at the University of Massachusetts Boston and a researcher at Harvard University’s Initiative for the Science of the Human Past. In an email to Inside Climate News, More said the website’s code shows values less than 0.05 micrograms per cubic meter, one third of the federal air quality standard for lead, are rounded down to 0.0.

More added that seven non-zero readings registered in the EPA’s website code as of noon on Wednesday after the blast. There were three non-zero readings before the detonation.
Soil monitoring in the days ahead will reveal how much lead dust settled in the environment, and where. Blood lead levels in children will also be important to monitor.
The EPA and Army will continue monitoring the blast site and surrounding communities.
After delaying the detonation by five days while a court weighed a challenge to the military’s plan, the Army and EPA say they used the extra time to collect more than 1,000 soil, dust, water and air samples and take additional mitigation measures.
In a post early Wednesday morning, the Army wrote that personnel in Middletown implemented measures including “a water wall filled with more than 100,000 gallons of water, earth berms, sand filled blocks, expert dispersion modeling, and temperature-regulating tarps.”
The Army also used a PFAS-free retardant foam to contain the blast. General Shirley attributed the plumes visible for miles following the explosion to the 3,600 gallons of liquid foam they’d applied to the site.
The Army believes these actions limited the spread of lead after the detonation.
Lanphear said detecting little or no lead is a sign that the delay and additional safeguards the Army took ahead of the operation were necessary and successful. “The public outcry prompted additional safeguards, and we should commend the Army, EPA and other agencies for taking steps to better protect surrounding communities,” he said.
Lanphear hopes the situation in Iowa will help draw more attention to other sources of lead exposure for Americans, such as the use of leaded aviation fuel by small aircraft.
Ahead of Wednesday’s detonation, the Army encouraged residents up to five miles southwest of the site to evacuate with support from the Federal Emergency Management Agency, a change from earlier plans.
While Middletown residents learned of the planned explosion at an October town hall, most Iowa residents, including state agencies and Gov. Kim Reynolds, weren’t notified until just five days before the operation was set to take place. The detonation was originally scheduled for Friday, Nov. 20.
At the time, the Army advised members of the public in the plume’s projected path to “consider avoiding the affected area” and keep windows and doors closed. It also suggested cleaning indoor and outdoor surfaces after the air exposure period ends.
Health and safety advice took on a more urgent tone this week. Ahead of this morning’s detonation, federal officials strongly urged community members to evacuate the affected area and advised those in areas with lead deposition to wet-clean surfaces that infants and children may touch.
On Tuesday, the EPA reported dispatching 200 staff to Iowa with plans for over 300 personnel on the ground Wednesday. As of late Tuesday night, the EPA said it had collected over 1,200 environmental samples.
The agency will compare those with others taken during and after the blast to track the range and amount of lead generated by the detonation. Minutes after the detonation, NASA aircraft circled the area to collect air samples.
