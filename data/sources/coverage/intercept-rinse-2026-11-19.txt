Just Rinse It Off, Army Says Before Blowing Up Five Tons of Lead Compound

When the U.S. Army blows up nearly five tons of unstable lead azide in southeastern Iowa on Friday, blanketing communities in lead-containing particles up to 100 miles away, it has a simple solution for concerned Iowans: Just rinse it off.
“The good thing is, if people have concerns about it being on their property, all they have to do is hose that off, and it’s gone,” Army spokesperson Justine Barati told Radio Iowa earlier this week.
This won’t be sufficient to mitigate the harm of the toxic plume the Army plans to release, environmental health experts based in Iowa told The Intercept. And, while the Army’s publicly released dispersion modeling shows that airborne lead particles could travel northeast from the detonation site near Burlington, Iowa, as far as Maquoketa, the experts found the Army’s modeling incomplete.
Burlington is in southeastern Iowa, just west of the Mississippi River and the Illinois border. Because the Army’s plume modeling is a prediction based on anticipated weather conditions, changes in wind speed or direction could carry lead-containing particles into neighboring Illinois, even if the published projection does not show that path.
“They put out this plume model, which is basically a colored blob, so to speak, on a map,” said Patrick O’Shaughnessy, a professor of occupational and environmental health at the University of Iowa. “What they didn’t show was the projected or estimated concentration of settled dust within that plume.”
That settled dust will come from lead azide, a highly sensitive explosive commonly used in detonators. The Army has been storing the material at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in a water-and-ethanol solution intended to keep it stable, but dangerous crystals formed during storage, according to an Army environmental assessment The Intercept found using the military’s Digital Visual Information Distribution System. Army officials determined that the resulting instability made the material too hazardous to move or transport, leaving an in-place detonation as their best option for disposal.
Now, the Army is warning residents and workers within the areas identified on its projected airborne exposure map to remain indoors, close windows and doors, and adjust air conditioning systems to prevent outside air from entering their homes “until the plume passes.” Children and pregnant individuals are among those specifically warned to avoid outdoor exposure.
O’Shaughnessy said the airborne plume could dissipate within hours, potentially ending the immediate inhalation hazard by Friday evening. But residents may not know how long they need to take precautions.
“Should I stay indoors all weekend? You know, the public doesn’t know, and no fault of theirs,” he said.
The Army environmental assessment shows that military officials considered several alternatives to Friday’s detonation, including chemically neutralizing the explosives or transporting them to an existing demolition facility, both of which were rejected as too dangerous. The Army also warned that leaving the explosives in storage could leave them to blow up by accident — and indefinitely disrupt the installation’s ability to make new ammunition.
The assessment does not establish how long the explosives had been stored, whether their deterioration was detected before March, or whether earlier intervention could have prevented them from becoming too dangerous to handle.
The Environmental Protection Agency, which issued an emergency permit authorizing the detonation, said the risk of an accidental explosion presented an “imminent and substantial threat to human health and the environment.”
“The Army, which has unparalleled expertise in matters concerning explosives safety, concluded that this material cannot safely be handled or moved and therefore, cannot be treated by any method other than in-place detonation,” an EPA spokesperson told The Intercept.
The permit requires the Army to notify potentially affected communities, conduct environmental sampling before and after the detonation, clean up contaminated areas, and report its findings to the EPA. At the University of Iowa, which is 60 miles away and not in the predicted pathway, researchers hadn’t gotten details about how that monitoring would be conducted, O’Shaughnessy said.
“We don’t know where it’s going to be conducted. We don’t know what instrumentation they’re going to use or how they’re going to report the information,” he said. Researchers there are preparing to assess the consequences independently, including by measuring potential lead contamination and conducting their own plume modeling.
The Army’s environmental assessment also acknowledges that contaminants from the detonation could enter waterways through stormwater runoff or seep into groundwater.
It’s clear, said Peter S. Thorne, professor emeritus of occupational and environmental health at the University of Iowa, that washing away lead dust would not eliminate the contamination.
“Hosing lead dust off of cars and other surfaces will indeed move it to the soil or into surface water,” he told The Intercept. “This could lead to human and animal exposure beyond inhalation of the generated lead dust plume.”
Asked what scientific evidence supported the advice to hose lead dust off residential property, the Army did not provide any studies or technical analysis. Instead, it said the EPA had provided cleanup guidelines as a condition of the emergency permit.
In an environmental handout prepared for an Oct. 15 community open house, the Army acknowledged that the detonation could damage nearby homes and contaminate surrounding property. The military said it would conduct environmental sampling on private property with homeowners’ permission and clean up lead contamination exceeding regulatory thresholds. It also acknowledged that cleanup operations could temporarily disrupt agricultural leases.
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has found that no safe blood lead level in children has been identified. Even low levels of exposure can harm children’s brain development and ability to learn.
Asked what concentrations of airborne lead it expected in surrounding communities, the Army did not provide specific projections. Instead, it pointed to naturally occurring lead in soil and contamination associated with leaded gasoline, lead-based paint, and hazardous waste sites. Asked about plans to monitor potential long-term health effects, Barati, the Army spokesperson, said only: “We do not anticipate long-term health impacts.”
O’Shaughnessy recommended testing surfaces in communities affected by the plume, including playground equipment, every three or four days for approximately a month to determine whether lead contamination persists after airborne particles have settled. “You can’t just sugarcoat this,” he said.
The Army’s assurances come against a documented history of lead contamination at military installations, including cases in which officials failed to address known hazards.
A 2018 Reuters investigation found that children living in Army housing had been exposed to hazardous lead paint, with military medical records documenting more than 1,000 elevated blood lead tests among young children processed by one Army medical center between 2011 and 2016. The investigation also found failures to report some test results to state health authorities.
Additionally, a 2024 investigation by The Intercept found that the Army National Guard had failed to adequately remediate lead contamination at armories used by soldiers and civilians, including children. A 2020 Army audit obtained by The Intercept found that 73 of 83 indoor firing ranges examined in New Jersey, North Carolina, and Ohio had not undergone thorough remediation or the required evaluations of lead hazards.
At New Jersey armories, officials had used high-pressure washing to remove lead contamination, despite a 2020 Army audit warning that the method was prohibited because it could embed lead throughout facilities and generate hazardous waste.
Asked whether earlier intervention could have prevented the explosives from deteriorating to the point that detonation became the safest available option, the Army did not answer directly. Instead, it referred The Intercept to previously published information about the operation.
“It strikes me that this is a case of neglect leading to only bad choices available to avoid a catastrophe,” Thorne said.
