Ground stop live updates: FAA grounds flights nationwide, but no threat to US
The Federal Aviation Administration has issued a nationwide ground stop for flights across the United States on Friday, Nov. 13, citing extraordinary circumstances and safety concerns related to an air traffic control issue.
The directive instructs all departing aircraft to remain on the ground until further notice. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the ground stop followed a communication issue affecting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers.
"FAA technicians are investigating the issue," Duffy said in a post on X. He said the agency would provide updates as they become available.
Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told reporters outside the White House that a “telecommunication outage throughout FAA” prompted the nationwide ground stoppage.
“We’re wanting to assure all the American people, this isn’t a safety (threat) to the American people or another 9/11,” Mullin said. “I think what’s a lot of people are tying to make the comparison to, and that’s not the case.”
He said that "there is no prior indication as of yet that this was nefarious attack against us" and that "it’s just a telecommunication outage as of right now.”
Flight tracking company Flightradar24 reported that the FAA issued the order following an equipment outage. The FAA's advisory also cited frequency issues across the National Airspace System.
Flights already in the air are not being ordered to land immediately. International flights heading to the United States will be handled on a case-by-case basis, according to the advisory.
Airlines for America, the trade association for the leading U.S. airlines, said it is "close communication" with the FAA and Department of Transportation.
"We appreciate the patience of the traveling public and the dedication of frontline employees who are navigating this situation," the group said in a statement. "Impacted travelers are encouraged to be in touch with their carrier for real-time updates."

‘This is almost without precedent’ aviation security consultant wants answers
Aviation security consultant Jeffrey Price was on a plane from Washington DC’s Dulles airport to his home in Denver when he started to see the news about planes being grounded nationwide.
“This is almost without precedent, except 9-11, which was obviously security and then the failure we had in 2023, which was about a 12-hour outage,” Price said. “So this is the third time in aviation history we've had to bring everybody down and park them.”
Price said it’s clear from the information currently available that one of the several communication systems at the FAA failed. Why that happened, he doesn’t know, he said.
“At this point everything's on the table,” said Price, who runs Leading Edge Strategies. “It could be cyber-attack related, it could be an IT system outage, it could be failures or redundancies in the systems and not wanting to use primary systems when the backups are not working.”
Despite the unanswered questions and the frantic messages he’s getting from colleagues, Price, who is a commercially rated pilot himself, said he wasn’t worried when he saw the news about a ground stop while he was in the air.
“I know the redundancies in the system that are available and I know that there's backups and that there are flight rules even if aircraft lose all communications, there are still rules in place to maintain the safety of the system,” Price said “So I actually wasn't too concerned.”

What happens if your flight is canceled?
Travelers whose flights are canceled for any reason are entitled to a refund if they choose to no longer fly.
If they choose to continue with their travels, airlines are obligated to rebook them at no cost.
No other compensation is required for cancellations due to ground stops because they’re not within the airline’s control.
Similarly, travelers whose flights are delayed by the ground stop aren’t entitled to any compensation because it’s outside carrier control.

Does the FAA use AI?
Just three days before Friday's nationwide ground stop, the FAA announced the national expansion of SMART, a new airspace planning system. Officials have not identified any connection between SMART and the ground stop.
Initially launched in the Washington, DC area on Sept. 21, the platform combines 200 data streams, including weather, flight paths and controller staffing, to help anticipate congestion and recommend scheduling changes.
The FAA said its expansion would cover airspace managed by all 22 regional air traffic control centers. SMART supports planning alongside existing systems; it does not replace controllers or control aircraft.

What is an FAA ground stop?
A ground stop is a traffic management initiative (TMI) issued by the FAA that requires all aircraft meeting specific criteria to remain on the ground, taking precedence over other TMIs. For travelers whose flights fit that criteria, this means departures are paused until the ground stop is lifted, even if scheduled during that time.
According to the FAA, criteria could be airport specific, airspace specific or equipment specific.
The FAA will issue ground stops when air traffic control cannot safely accommodate aircraft in a particular airspace or airport, such as severe weather, security threats, or airport capacity restraints like equipment outages.
They’re typically in reaction to an event and come with little warning following the consideration of other, less restrictive TMIs.
— Kathleen Wong
What happened after the previous nationwide ground stop?
Following the January 2023 nationwide ground stop, lawmakers pressed the FAA about preventing another outage. Then-acting administrator Billy Nolen explained that contractors accidentally deleted a file, and the error spread to the backup database through synchronization, disabling both systems.
The FAA subsequently introduced a one-hour delay between database updates to prevent a repeat. Nolen also outlined modernization plans, including retiring an older NOTAM interface by 2025, and a broader safety review.
"We built a brand-new NOTAM service from the ground up in record time," FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said on Sept. 29, 2025. “It is resilient, user-friendly, and scalable, and will significantly improve airspace safety and efficiency."
Nolen emphasized in 2023 that aviation remained safe but acknowledged improvements were needed, declining to guarantee that the system distributing critical safety notices would never experience another technical problem.

Is this the first nationwide ground stop since 9/11?
No. On Jan. 11, 2023, the FAA halted departures nationwide after its Notice to Air Missions (NOTAM) system failed.
The system provides flight crews with essential safety information, including runway closures and other hazards. The FAA’s preliminary review found that contractors accidentally deleted files while trying to synchronize the primary database with a backup.
The agency paused departures while technicians restored service and verified the system was working properly. The ground stop lasted nearly two hours, but flight disruptions continued throughout the day. The FAA said investigators found no evidence of a cyberattack or other malicious intent.

How long will the FAA ground stop last?
The FAA advisory lists an effective period beginning at 3:54 p.m. ET on Nov. 13 and extending to 11:30 p.m. ET.
However, the directive instructs departing aircraft to remain grounded until further notice. The listed expiration time does not guarantee that flights will resume by then.
The FAA has not provided an estimate for when normal flight operations will return.
What does a nationwide ground stop mean for travelers?
A ground stop is an FAA directive that temporarily prevents affected aircraft from departing their airports. It does not necessarily mean all flights have been canceled.
The disruption could lead to delays, missed connections and schedule changes as airlines adjust their operations.
Passengers scheduled to fly Friday should check their airline's website or mobile app for the latest updates before heading to the airport. Travelers should also monitor notifications from their airlines for changes to departure times or cancellations.
The FAA advisory does not specify how many flights or passengers could be affected by the nationwide ground stop.
