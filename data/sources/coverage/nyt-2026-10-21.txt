Trump Says He’ll Decide Who Gets Rewards From New Whistle-Blower Program
President Trump’s announcement raised concerns that he would use the program seeking information on fraud for political purposes.
President Trump this week announced a new $25 million whistle-blower incentive program to provide information to combat fraud. But the program has raised concerns after Mr. Trump said he would personally determine who would get the money, seeming to bypass the traditional processes around the awarding of federal funds.
“I will be the arbitrator of all of these decisions,” Mr. Trump told reporters in the Oval Office. “In other words, I’ll determine whether or not the person is deserving, and I’ll determine, and I will be very generous.”
Whistle-blowers already receive hundreds of millions of dollars annually for reporting fraud through existing legal avenues, including filing complaints under the False Claims Act and whistle-blower programs at the I.R.S., the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. Payouts under such programs are typically determined by a commission or by a federal court, not the president.
A White House spokeswoman, Lauren Bis, said that “every day, President Trump’s task force to eliminate fraud is zeroing in on the rampant abuse of taxpayer dollars.” She added: “This administration has zero tolerance for individuals who steal from Americans.”
Mr. Trump’s first award, of $10,000, went to Nick Shirley, a conservative YouTube personality who promoted allegations of fraud among the Somali community in Minnesota. Mr. Trump and other Republicans have used those allegations to attack Democrats in the state.
Joe Spielberger, senior policy counsel for the Project on Government Oversight, said what Mr. Trump laid out was “not a serious anti-fraud or whistle-blower incentive program.”
“The administration and their allies have continuously cited combating fraud as a cover to persecute political opponents and immigrant communities,” he said. “There is no evidence that tips generated from the public will be treated similarly and consistently investigated. This only incentivizes political provocateurs to target anyone the administration deems an enemy.”
Here is a look at the whistle-blower program and Mr. Trump’s announcement.
What is the new program?
According to the White House, the “Fraud Whistle-blower Incentive Program” will be administered by the Justice Department. It would award up to $30,000 to anyone who submits a tip that leads to a successful conviction for fraud or a significant federal enforcement action.
But Mr. Trump described the program differently. The president said he would award a “1 percent commission” to the tipster based on the value of the fraud found.
“You, let’s say, report and you find a billion dollars, we are giving a commission to that person of 1 percent — a lot of money,” Mr. Trump said. “And if it’s $10 billion, $200 billion, $400 billion, we’re giving a 1 percent commission to that person.”
Under the president’s description, a whistle-blower could receive billions of dollars personally approved by Mr. Trump.
Are there existing programs for whistle-blowers?
Yes, there are several programs already awarding millions annually to whistle-blowers through systems approved by Congress.
Under the False Claims Act, for instance, whistle-blowers file civil suits confidentially alleging wrongdoing. The Justice Department evaluates the claims and decides whether to join them. If a settlement is reached in which the wrongdoer repays the federal government, the whistle-blower receives up to 30 percent of the funds. Last year, after going through the court process, whistle-blowers were awarded more than $330 million.
Likewise, in fiscal year 2025, the Securities and Exchange Commission awarded $60 million to 48 individual whistle-blowers.
What are the concerns about Mr. Trump’s new program?
In announcing that he would make determinations about the payouts, Mr. Trump raised concerns that he would use the program for political purposes.
“Generally speaking, incentivizing whistle-blowers is a good thing, but is this going to be manipulated for political purposes?” said Mark Greenblatt, a former inspector general for the Interior Department. “When you concentrate discretion for spending federal dollars into one person’s hands who can make unilateral decisions, you run into potential problems.”
David Rosenfeld, a law professor at Northern Illinois University and the former co-leader for enforcement at the S.E.C.’s New York office, said there was an extensive process within the S.E.C. for awarding whistle-blower payouts, including several layers of review.
“It’s not up to one person to do it in a discretionary manner,” he said. “I’ve never heard of a program where it’s just up to the president.”
Didn’t Trump pardon lots of allies convicted of fraud?
It’s true that Mr. Trump has not been uniformly tough on fraud. He has granted clemency to more than 70 allies, donors and others convicted in fraud cases, often involved high-dollar totals.
He has shown no such leniency with regard to the fraud cases in Minnesota, where immigrants and Democratic politicians are his political targets.
The president used the announcement of the fund to attack Gov. Tim Walz of Minnesota, a Democrat, as “100 percent guilty,” and the state’s attorney general, Keith Ellison, also a Democrat, as a “total thief.” And he has portrayed the lieutenant governor of Minnesota, Peggy Flanagan, who is the Democratic nominee in a Senate race, as a “liar.”
Mr. Trump also lashed out at Representative Ilhan Omar, a Minnesota Democrat and Somali American, suggesting she could “go to jail” and calling her a “sick, very dishonest person.”
Federal data does not support the suggestion of the president and his allies that fraud occurs more prevalently under Democrats than Republicans.
