What MS Secretary of State said about Senate election, Experian data

MS secretary of state stands by Senate election, Experian data use
All eyes were on Mississippi for weeks. The midterm U.S. Senate election that pitted Scott Colom, the Democratic challenger and eventual winner, against Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith seemed like it was going to last forever.
Civil rights advocacy groups sounded the alarms about tens of thousands of voters who had been marked inactive ahead of the election, and Democratic leaders compared it to the difficulty of voting when poll taxes were still in place.
The Mississippi Republican Party, too, raised concerns about "abnormal" affidavit ballot counts and promised it was poised to dispatch lawyers for litigation if necessary.
Secretary of State Michael Watson told the Clarion Ledger on Nov. 18 that the 2026 election, despite public concern, was "a good, fair, clean election" and his office "worked really hard to get the results correct."
"There is no doubt in my mind that this election was fair and just," Watson said. "It came under extreme scrutiny and pressure, and it held up."
Did the secretary of state purge thousands of voters before Election Day?
Chief among the criticisms of the office's election process is a claim from several voting rights groups that Watson purged over 230,000 registered voters from the voter rolls ahead of the election.
The secretary dismissed that as an outright lie for multiple reasons. First, he said, it was important to outline the difference between an inactive voter and a purged voter. A Mississippian can be moved to the inactive voter list for lack of consistent voting in the state, but they are still eligible to file an affidavit ballot.
A purged voter has been entirely removed from the voter rolls and needs to register with their county office to be reinstated.
Though he acknowledged that hundreds of thousands of voters were reclassified as inactive, he said it was a process conducted over the last several years that many people likely only noticed during this election cycle. His office tries to verify whether someone still lives at their last-known address by sending notecards to residents, he said, and voters would only be marked as inactive if they didn't reply.
Watson also said that voter statuses cannot be changed in the 90 days, or around three months, before an election.
He acknowledged that, for one reason or another, voters can be wrongfully moved from active to inactive status, but the "safety net of affidavit voting" ensures that their votes are still counted.
Watson campaigned on cleaning Mississippi's voter rolls in his bid to become the secretary of state, and a large part of that, he said, was fueled by counties going too long without updating their numbers. His office compares the number of registered voters in a county with census estimates of the citizen voting age population, using numbers from both the decennial census and annual community surveys.
Around the time that he began to weigh using commercial credit bureau information for voter roll maintenance, he said, seven of Mississippi's 82 counties had more registered voters than estimates indicated they should. In October 2026, the most recent data from the office available online, only three counties had more voters than estimated.
What role did Experian play in the Mississippi midterm election?
Toward the end of 2023 and beginning of 2024, after Watson had been reelected, he started talking with the credit reporting agency Experian about a long-standing program that it had been conducting for voter roll maintenance.
Since 2012, Watson said, the agency had helped the federal government with voting information, and it came highly recommended by the bipartisan U.S. Election Commission Assistance. Mississippi signed onto a pilot program starting in Lafayette County, where Watson said around 3,000 voter files were flagged.
Experian used federal information such as change-of-address filings alongside its commercial data on car and home title registrations to identify files that needed a closer look, and Watson said it worked exactly as intended.
"The election happened, and you didn't hear a word," Watson said.
He inked a $50,000 contract with the agency to cover the whole state in 2025. But as election workers trudged through thousands of affidavit ballots in 2026, Experian backed away.
"The solution provided at that time consisted of supplemental address information and was not designed or intended to validate voter records," a company spokesperson told outlets on Nov. 11. "Decisions regarding voter registration policies, procedures and record updates are made solely by election officials in accordance with applicable local, state and federal laws."
Watson took to social media the next day. In a now-deleted post on X, he asked the Election Assistance Commission whether the two "should consider a lawsuit."
"@Experian, can you square this with the statement you put out crawfishing away from MS?" Watson wrote. "[Experian} marketed a product for a specific reason and are now saying it wasn't designed for that reason. More to come …"
Watson did not confirm on Nov. 18 whether he planned to sue the bureau. When asked about his social media post and experience working with Experian, he said that he and the secretary of state's office "have all the receipts."
"What was interesting, after Tuesday … I started reaching out and said, 'Hey, it looks like there might be some, not a storm brewing, but some information that we're going to need from you guys to help tell the full story,'" Watson said. "I had a hard time getting a response, and then we saw that statement come out that said, 'This was not designed for this purpose' … What it felt like was they were getting some heat, and they wanted to back away from it. They couldn't stand the heat in the kitchen."
The Experian executives are not the only ones who have criticized Watson's use of the system for voter-roll maintenance.
Senator-elect Colom slammed Watson on Nov. 10 as workers continued counting votes, telling reporters that the secretary was responsible for the delay in results and confusing voting process.
"He is pointing the finger at the election commissioners, but it was his job to make sure those election commissioners had the information they needed," Colom said. "He decided to use Experian even though reporting I read said Experian told him they weren't reliable."
Mississippi Democratic Party Chair Rep. Cheikh Taylor told the Clarion Ledger on Nov. 14 that Watson had made "catastrophic blunders" in the election.
The secretary maintained that "the system worked as designed." His office has since contracted with IDI Data instead because it has "a better product."
What are the next steps for elections and voting in Mississippi?
For some, the long lines at voting precincts and drawn-out counting process made the case for Mississippi to join the 47 states with early voting. Even President Donald Trump encouraged Mississippians to get out and vote early.
Watson is not convinced.
"I'm not a huge fan, per se, of early voting," Watson said. "I think what we have now is early voting,… you just have to have a reason to go do it. I'm old-school in that I love Election Day. I think that part of our communities coming together to make a decision for the future of our state and our country, it's a beautiful thing."
Watson understands the arguments centered on inclement weather, he said, but expanding early voting is not among his focuses. He said many of the issues seen in this election could be resolved with more machines at voting precincts to cut down on wait times.
The precinct at Jackson State University where nearly 400 students voted on Election Day had only one machine, and the line climbed to nearly 100 people around noon. Some students told the Clarion Ledger that they waited for nearly an hour to cast their ballot.
What is at the top of Watson's priority list is campaign finance reform. Several lawmakers have pushed to require digital campaign finance filings for all elected officials, which would make the data much easier to search, but members of the elections committee have shut the proposal down for years.
Watson also wants a "comprehensive rewrite" of Mississippi's entire election code with a "good prosecutable crime section." He was planning to take it easy in his final year as Secretary of State, he said, but his plans changed.
He said he wants the next lieutenant governor to see "the good work" that the secretary did. Watson is running for lieutenant governor next year and was the only candidate in the race as of mid-November.
