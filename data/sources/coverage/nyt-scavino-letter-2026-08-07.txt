Trump Restarts Battle to Fire Sitting Fed Governor
The Trump administration, rebuffed by the Supreme Court in its first attempt to fire Lisa D. Cook, sent the Federal Reserve governor a letter saying it was “considering” removing her from the job.
President Trump opened a new front in his battle with Lisa D. Cook, the Federal Reserve official he tried — and failed — to oust from her position last year.
In a letter sent on Wednesday, the White House informed Ms. Cook, who serves on the Board of Governors, that it was “considering” removing her from her position. The letter, which was signed by Dan Scavino, the White House deputy chief of staff, gave Ms. Cook 21 days to respond to previous allegations that she had committed mortgage fraud. Ms. Cook has not been charged with a crime, even though it has been a year since the administration first made the claims, nor has she been convicted of any wrongdoing.
The escalation comes after Mr. Trump suffered a significant legal setback in June when the Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that Ms. Cook, whom Mr. Trump tried to fire last August, should have been given the formal ability to contest the accusations against her. In siding with the lower court, the Supreme Court granted Ms. Cook the ability to continue serving out her term, which is set to end 2038.
But the Supreme Court left much unresolved, providing an opportunity for Mr. Trump to revive his attacks. The justices did not clearly articulate the full legal criteria that would allow Mr. Trump to fire Ms. Cook. The Federal Reserve Act stipulates the president can remove a sitting Fed official only “for cause,” which has long been interpreted to mean gross negligence or a dereliction of duty. The justices in June also did not offer an opinion on the exact allegations against her, which revolve around her taking out mortgages on a range of properties that were all listed as a primary residence. Ms. Cook has in the past suggested that these were clerical errors.
“These allegations are as baseless now as they were a year ago when President Trump tried to remove Governor Cook and interfere with the independence of the Federal Reserve,” Abbe Lowell and Norm Eisen, Ms. Cook’s lawyers, said in a statement. “No matter what President Trump tries to do next, this much is clear under the facts and Supreme Court precedent — there is no valid cause for removing Governor Cook. As we did before, we will challenge this latest pretext and preserve her position and the historic role of the Fed.”
In the letter, which was earlier reported by ABC News, Mr. Scavino said the Fed governor’s actions “may be sufficient to demonstrate that you committed a crime, as you appear to have acquired mortgages that do not meet certain lending requirements and could have received favorable loan terms under fraudulent circumstances.”
“Based on these facts, a jury could find that you intended to defraud these institutions for your own benefit, but at a minimum, this conduct was grossly negligent and demonstrates that you are unfit for the office in which you serve as a controlling member of the Federal Reserve,” Mr. Scavino added in his letter.
Mr. Trump has long sought more sway over the Fed. In May, he had the opportunity to appoint a new Fed chairman — Kevin M. Warsh — after Jerome H. Powell’s term leading the central bank came to an end. However, Mr. Powell, who has been one of the president’s main targets, opted to stay on as a governor, as he is able to do until January 2028, because of his concerns about Mr. Trump’s attempts to undermine the Fed’s independence.
Those fears stemmed in large part from the Justice Department’s decision to begin a criminal investigation into Mr. Powell over his handling of costly renovations at the Fed’s headquarters in Washington. After numerous legal setbacks, the Justice Department dropped the inquiry, although the U.S. attorney for the District of Columbia, Jeanine Pirro, has maintained that she could reopen it at any point.
Mr. Trump wants lower borrowing costs and has accused the Fed in the past of holding back economic growth by not complying. If he is able to oust Ms. Cook, Mr. Trump would have the opportunity to appoint an ally to replace her. Members of the board must be confirmed by the Senate, however.
The president has changed tack in his criticism of the Fed now that Mr. Warsh is in charge. He has stopped short of going after Mr. Warsh directly even as the Fed begins to more actively consider raising interest rates to tame inflation.
Mr. Trump instead has attacked Mr. Warsh’s Fed colleagues, calling them “hostile” and “political.” Rate decisions are made by not only the seven members of the board of governors, but also the president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York and a rotating set of four presidents from the 12 regional Fed banks.
In an interview with Punchbowl News released on Friday, Mr. Trump reiterated his preference for lower rates but acknowledged that it was not Mr. Warsh’s decision alone.
“It’s up to him a little bit, but not completely. And he’s got a board that’s very political,” Mr. Trump said.
In her most recent comments on the economic outlook, Ms. Cook signaled that she would support higher interest rates if inflation did not soon moderate.
Colby Smith covers the Federal Reserve and the U.S. economy for The Times.
Maggie Haberman is a White House correspondent for The Times, reporting on President Trump.
