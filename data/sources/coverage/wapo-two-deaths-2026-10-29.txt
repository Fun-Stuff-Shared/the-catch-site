Prediction market Polymarket halted trading on its international platform for roughly an hour Thursday after a third-party software vendor was compromised.
Users in the prediction market’s official Discord, an online chat forum, began panicking after reports around 1:30 p.m. that some wallets had been drained of funds. Polymarket said in a statement that any users who had been impacted “will be made whole.”
Polymarket, which operates a separate U.S. market, allows users to wager on the outcome of any event, from the midterm elections to when the video game “Grand Theft Auto VI” will be released. It said around 2 p.m. that it had moved into cancel-only mode, which allows users to cancel transactions but prevents them from making new trade orders.
The issue concerned a third-party performance and debugging platform called DebugBear, according to a person familiar with the matter who spoke on the condition of anonymity to share sensitive details. Polymarket has reported the incident to authorities in the United Kingdom, where DebugBear is based.
DebugBear did not immediately respond to a request for comment. But the company acknowledged on social media that it was being impacted by an ongoing security incident and asked anyone using one of its embedded web-monitoring tools to remove it from their sites.

Ohio Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy has discontinued airing a widely denounced television ad featuring the estranged mother of his opponent, Democrat Amy Acton, who called her daughter “a liar.”
Acton’s mother, Donna Arthur, lost custody of her two children in 1978, after Arthur’s husband was indicted on a charge of raping her daughter, who was then 12 years old. Amy Acton and her mother have not had contact since then.
“It ran to completion, and we put up four other ads,” Ramaswamy said at a Thursday campaign stop in Vandalia, Ohio, according to the Columbus Dispatch.
A Ramaswamy campaign official confirmed to The Washington Post that the ad was no longer on the air. “It rotated off like any ad would,” the official said.
Ramaswamy’s campaign spent about $1 million to air two versions of the ad, just a quarter of the amount it had initially said it would put behind it, according to AdImpact data as of Thursday.
The ad had provoked a firestorm of bipartisan criticism. Ohio Gov. Mike DeWine (R) was among those who had called for it to be taken down. The blowback was such that many Republicans worried that it would damage the prospect of others on their Ohio ticket, including Sen. Jon Husted, who said he “would not have run” it.
However, the ad was defended and promoted by President Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance. Trump had called the criticism of Ramaswamy and the ad “very unfair.”
“Who wouldn’t use such an Ad? I would, it’s called, ‘hitting pay dirt,’” the president wrote in a social media post.

Patrick Stewart, who rose to prominence as an actor of Shakespeare’s works before becoming a household name as Capt. Jean-Luc Picard on the TV series “Star Trek: The Next Generation,” died Wednesday in Los Angeles. He was 86.
He died after a “brief and poignant journey with” Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia, Mr. Stewart’s social media accounts shared in a statement.
Mr. Stewart spent almost two decades as a member of the Royal Shakespeare Company, appearing in productions of “Hamlet,” “The Tempest” and “Much Ado About Nothing.” But the English actor experienced a profound breakthrough to American audiences with his role as the serene Picard in the Star Trek spin-off.
“For a lot of people, I will be locked for all time on the bridge of the Enterprise in my spacesuit,” he told The Washington Post in 1997. “Not only am I reconciled to that, I’m quite proud of it. The show represents quality television. It was intelligent, thoughtful, argumentative, and it also told wonderful stories.”
He kept one foot in the theater world, taking on the title role of “Othello” at Washington’s Shakespeare Theatre in 1997, while also appearing in other science-fiction universes. In Hollywood, Mr. Stewart is perhaps synonymous with his role in the X-Men series as Professor X, who supported the powers of other superheroes.
Mr. Stewart’s marriages to Sheila Falconer and Star Trek producer Wendy Neuss ended in divorce. In 2013, he married singer-songwriter Sunny Ozell. He has two children from his first marriage. Information on survivors was not immediately available.
No matter the role, Mr. Stewart brought his training from the Bristol Old Vic Theatre School, where he learned to draw on his challenging and rewarding experiences in his personal life.
“We were taught how to store emotions,” he said of the experience in 2014. “It means no experience is ever wasted.”

Two U.S. service members die amid Iran war, Pentagon tally shows
The Pentagon added one death to its public database of Iran war fatalities, bringing the disclosed total to 23. (Anna Moneymaker/Getty Images)

The Pentagon has added two noncombat deaths to its public database of U.S. military deaths that have occurred amid the war with Iran, bringing the disclosed total of fatalities to23.
Both deaths were airmen who died in nonhostile incidents, according to the Defense Department’s public casualty database. One death was the result of an illness, and the other was of unspecified causes and remains under investigation, the Air Force said.
The deaths were listed under the Defense Casualty Analysis System, or DCAS, the Pentagon’s official record of all U.S. troops who have died or were wounded in wartime or other contingency operations.
The fatalities were added to DCAS in the category of Overseas Operations, a separate accounting of service members killed or wounded in the Middle East starting in July,amid the tenuous ceasefire between Washington and Tehran. Casualties until that point were recorded under Operation Epic Fury, the Trump administration’s name for the Iran war.
In all, there have been 11 deaths from enemy fire and 12 nonhostile deaths, according to DCAS. Six of those nonhostile deaths were the result of a refueling tanker that crashed in Iraq in March, killing all airmen aboard.
Another 865 troops have been wounded by enemy fire since the war began in late February, according to DCAS. Despite the euphemism “overseas operations” suggesting a lower threat than combat operations, more than half of the wounded were the result of attacks after the ceasefire, which produced rounds of retaliatory strikes on both sides.
Federal debt surpasses size of economy for first time since World War II

Federal debt held by the public has surpassed the size of the U.S. economy for the first time since World War II, raising concerns about the country’s fiscal outlook as economists warn that some forces sustaining economic growth could begin to fade.
The debt-to-GDP ratio reached 100.3 percent in the 2026 fiscal year, which means the federal government owes more money than the country produces in goods and services over a year.
The milestone comes as the economy shows signs of resilience but faces growing risks. Consumer spending, a key engine of economic growth, has held up this year, in part buoyed by tax refunds and stock market gains that have benefited wealthier households. But economists expect spending to moderate in the months ahead as some of that support fades.
The economy grew at a 3.4 percent annual rate between July and September, according to data released Thursday by the Bureau of Economic Analysis. Consumer spending rose 0.5 percent in September, though the increase essentially disappeared after adjusting for inflation.
Veronica Clark, an economist at Citi, said the boost from larger tax refunds earlier this year has likely faded.
Lydia Boussour, a senior economist at EY-Parthenon, expects spending to moderate in the fourth quarter as inflation and elevated interest rates squeeze household budgets.
Diane Swonk, chief economist at KPMG, said she was “very worried” about the debt’s implications for long-term interest rates, which affect mortgage costs. If growth slows while borrowing costs remain elevated, households and businesses could face additional pressure, further complicating the country’s economic outlook.

Trump administration will require drug middlemen to disclose how much they are paid

Massive companies that oversee prescriptions for tens of millions of Americans will soon be required to provide detailed information on how much money they make from drugmakers, under a new regulation released Thursday by the Trump administration.
Under the Labor Department regulation, pharmacy benefit managers, which negotiate prescription drug benefits for employers and workers on their plans, will have to disclose how much they pay for pharmaceuticals and how much they are paid by drug companies.
The policy is part of President Donald Trump’s broader efforts to “lower drug costs and increase transparency across the healthcare system,” the administration said.
“The Trump administration is ensuring no one in the healthcare industry can escape the competition that comes from complete price transparency,” Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling said in a statement.
The rule will allow employers to“negotiate better deals and lower drug prices for the over 72 million Americans on self-insured group healthcare plans,” he said.
The regulation responds to criticism from advocates and lawmakers that pharmacy benefit managers exploit a lack of transparency to direct patients toward more expensive drugs.
But the rule also received pushback from large pharmacy benefit managers. The Cigna Group’s Express Scripts, CVS’s Caremark and UnitedHealth Group’s Optum Rx control roughly 80 percent of prescription drug claims in the United States, according to a 2026 report by the left-leaning economic policy think tank Groundwork Collaborative.
The rule takes effect in health care plans beginning in July.

Seabirds get jet lag, and it can throw them off course, study shows
A Manx shearwater flies around Skomer Island in Wales. (Dr. Joe Wynn)
2 min
Loading...
Seabirds get jet-lagged just like humans — and it causes navigational errors in their foraging trips, according to a study published today in the journal Science.
Researchers who study bird navigation wanted to understand better how seabirds know where they are, and where they’re going, across vast distances. Birds generally use the Earth’s magnetic fields and the position of the sun when flying, which they use in combination with their natural circadian rhythm or internal clock to gauge time of day.
When birds fly east to west they “experience time shifts, potentially causing mismatches between their internal clock and local time,” the study said.
If the birds’ clocks are thrown off by jet lag, they can misjudge where they are and their intended destination, said Patrick Lewin, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Oxford and co-author of the new study.
The study said birds experience time shifts when flying east to west. (Dr. Joe Wynn)
Lewin and his colleagues tracked the migratory seabird Manx shearwaters, which take long fishing trips to find food and then return to their nests, taking the most direct route possible.
“Shearwaters make it really easy for us,” Lewin said. “Because we know where their nests are, we know exactly where they’re aiming for. And so that means we can measure whether they’re going a little bit too far clockwise or a little too far anticlockwise and compare that to what we would expect if they’re using the sun as a compass and experiencing this time shift.”
Lewin said he hopes his research will help to better understand humans’ internal clocks, too.
“The way that our clocks work is very similar to how a bird’s clock works,” Lewin said. “It might be quite interesting to know how these animals are coping with frequently jet-lagging themselves.”
