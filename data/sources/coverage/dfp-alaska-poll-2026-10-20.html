Every Statewide Race in Alaska Is Close, as 7 in 10 Voters Worry About Affording Gas and Food

New Data for Progress polling finds close races for governor, U.S. Senate, and U.S. House in Alaska as worry about the cost of living keeps climbing.

Former Representative Mary Peltola leads Senator Dan Sullivan 51% to 49% in the simulated final round of the ranked choice election for Senate, a race that has tightened since our August survey. Independent Bill Hill has all but erased the double-digit lead Rep. Nick Begich held in August. Begich now leads just 51% to 49%. Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins leads Republican Bernadette Wilson 53% to 47% in the governor’s race. And Ballot Measure 2, which would repeal open primaries, ranked choice voting, and campaign finance disclosure rules, is tied.

The cost of living continues to be Alaska voters’ top issue by far. At least 7 in 10 voters worry about affording gas, utilities, and groceries, a share that has climbed in just the two months since our August survey. Voters want to hear far more from candidates about those costs, and far less about transgender athletes in girls’ sports.

Ahead of the Alaska Federation of Natives convention, which begins October 22, AFN is the most popular statewide institution we tested. Voters are strongly favorable toward their local police but overwhelmingly unfavorable toward AI surveillance cameras like Flock.

This survey extends Data for Progress’ ongoing research in Alaska, from the state’s 2024 legislative priorities and elections to its 2025 legislative sessions and a look at where the governor’s race stood last summer. The affordability crises Data for Progress documented last year — including in healthcare and housing — have only deepened since, as our August survey showed. Data for Progress has a track record of accurately surveying Alaska voters: Our 2024 poll anticipated President Donald Trump’s and Begich’s 2024 margins each within 2.5 points. This survey’s weighted respondents voted for Trump by a 13-point margin, matching his actual 2024 result.

Sullivan’s Favorability Falls to a New Low

Kreiss-Tomkins and Hill have the highest net favorability of anyone on the ballot, at +10 and +7, respectively, followed by Begich (+4). All three Republicans running for governor are underwater: Wilson (−7), Treg Taylor (−12), and Dave Bronson (−20), who fares worst in Anchorage, where he used to be mayor (−33).

 
 

Meanwhile, 44% of Alaska voters have a favorable opinion of Sullivan, compared with 54% unfavorable, for a −10 net favorability, his lowest in any of our six Alaska surveys since February 2024, when voters viewed him favorably on balance. Trump’s job approval has slid as well, to −6 (47% approve, 53% disapprove), down from roughly even in August, as fuel prices in Alaska have hit record highs amid the war in Iran.

 
 

Peltola and Begich Hold Narrow Leads in U.S. Senate and House Races, Respectively

The Senate ballot lists Peltola, Sen. Sullivan, Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., a retired Petersburg teacher who shares the incumbent’s name, and perennial Republican candidate Gerald Heikes. Peltola leads in first-choice votes, 47% to 44%, with Sullivan Jr. at 4% and Heikes at 1%. Once Heikes and Sullivan Jr. are eliminated in the ranked choice simulation, Peltola leads 51% to 49%. Peltola’s lead has narrowed since we last polled the race in August, when she led 53% to 47%.

 
 

In the House race, Begich leads Hill in first-choice votes, 48% to 42%, with Democrat Eric Hafner, who is campaigning from federal prison, at 5% and Libertarian Jim McDermott at 1%. Begich leads the final round 51% to 49%. The race has tightened considerably since Begich won the August primary by a +12-point margin and led Hill 47% to 37% in our August survey. In that survey, 47% of voters hadn’t heard enough about Hill to say whether they were favorable or unfavorable of him, compared with 18% now.

 
 

Kreiss-Tomkins Leads the Governor’s Race

Kreiss-Tomkins, the only Democrat in the race, faces three Republicans: Wilson, Bronson, and Taylor. He leads in first-choice votes with 46%, followed by Wilson (28%), Bronson (12%), and Taylor (9%). Once Taylor and Bronson are eliminated, he leads Wilson 53% to 47% in the final round. (Trump endorsed Wilson at a Wasilla rally on October 13, while the survey was in the field.)

 
 

Exhausted Ballots of Eliminated Candidates Could Make the Difference in All Three Races

Two controversial candidates could affect the outcome of Alaska’s congressional races: a Senate candidate who shares the incumbent’s name, and a House candidate campaigning from federal prison who has never been to Alaska. Outside groups on both sides have spent millions of dollars boosting lesser-known candidates across Alaska’s races.

In ranked choice elections, a ballot is “exhausted” when every candidate a voter ranked has been eliminated. To estimate how much these ballots could matter, we ran a hypothetical scenario that transfers them to the most aligned remaining candidate (or, in the Senate race, the most similarly named).

If exhausted ballots from Sullivan Jr. voters were transferred to Sen. Sullivan, the Senate race would be tied. And if exhausted ballots from Hafner voters went to Hill, the House race would be tied, too. (In our survey, no ballots from Heikes or McDermott voters were exhausted.)

In the governor’s race, the three Republican candidates split the Republican vote in the first round, and when Taylor and Bronson are eliminated, some of their voters’ ballots exhaust rather than transferring to Wilson. If every exhausted ballot from a voter who ranked a Republican went to Wilson, the governor’s race would also be tied.

 
 

Voters Are Split on Ballot Measure 2, But Back Open Primaries and Disclosure

Ballot Measure 2 would repeal the open primaries and ranked choice voting Alaskans adopted in 2020, along with the campaign finance disclosure rules passed in the same measure. Shown the official ballot language, voters split evenly, 48% Yes to 48% No. Republicans back it (89% Yes), while Democrats (87% No) and Independents (59% No) oppose it.

 
 

Ballot Measure 2 would repeal three things. Asked about each separately, voters support keeping two of the three. Two-thirds (66%) of Alaska voters prefer open primaries, where everyone gets the same ballot, to party primaries (30%). Ballot Measure 2 would repeal Alaska’s current open primary system and return to party primaries.

 
 

The second thing that Ballot Measure 2 would repeal, ranked choice voting, is the exception, where Alaska voters are on the side of repeal: A narrow majority (53%) prefer single choice voting, compared with 45% who prefer ranked choice. Republicans overwhelmingly prefer single choice voting (93%), while Democrats (83%) and Independents (56%) prefer ranked choice.

 
 

The final thing that Ballot Measure 2 would repeal, campaign finance disclosure rules, is the most popular of all. Nearly all Alaska voters (91%), including 88% of Republicans, say groups that pay for political ads should have to tell the public who gives them money. Despite this almost universal sentiment, if Ballot Measure 2 were to pass, groups that pay for political ads would no longer have to tell the public who gives them money.

 
 

The Cost of Living Dominates Alaska Voters’ Concerns

Across Alaska, the cost of living continues to dwarf every other issue. A majority of Alaska voters (54%) name the economy, jobs, and the cost of living as either their most important or second-most important issue. Next comes political corruption (21%), followed by taxes and government spending (19%) and the state of democracy (18%).

 
 

The economy tops the list for every party, named first or second by 50% of Democrats, 56% of Independents, and 50% of Republicans. After that, Democrats and Independents prioritize political corruption and the state of democracy, while Republicans prioritize taxes and government spending and immigration.

 
 

The cost of living tops the list in every region, too, named first or second by at least half of voters in all parts of Alaska.

 
 

Among voters who name the economy as their first or second issue, most (73%) point to inflation and the cost of living. For those voters, food and groceries are the top concern, ahead of utilities, housing, and gas.

Worry about affording the basics keeps climbing. Almost 3 in 4 Alaska voters (73%) worry about affording gas and transportation over the next year, up from 67% in August and 54% a year ago. Worry about affording utilities has risen to 71% (from 66% in August and 54% a year ago), and worry about affording groceries to 70% (from 66% and 61%, respectively). In Fairbanks, 85% worry about affording utilities heading into winter. Among voters who are SNAP recipients, at least 90% worry about affording gas, utilities, and groceries.

 
 

Voters Want Candidates to Talk More About the Cost of Living

Outside groups have poured tens of millions of dollars into Alaska’s races, and only 4% of voters we surveyed say they haven’t seen or heard a campaign ad. Asked whether they want to hear more or less from candidates about a range of topics, majorities want to hear more about gas and fuel prices (57%), grocery prices (57%), healthcare costs (54%), and political corruption, like insider stock trading (55%).

 
 

Independents, who make up more than half of voters in our survey, most want to hear more about grocery prices, gas prices, and healthcare costs. Majorities of every partisan group want to hear less about transgender athletes in girls’ sports, including 58% of Republicans.

 
 

Voters Back State Corporate Income Tax on All Major Oil and Gas Companies, and Are Divided on Personal Income Taxes

An overwhelming 84% of Alaska voters, including 72% of Republicans, support requiring all major oil and gas companies to pay the state corporate income tax, which only some pay now. Support for this proposal has risen in each of our surveys, from 77% support in March 2025 to 80% in July 2025 and 82% in August 2026.

Recent budgets have leaned on smaller Permanent Fund Dividends, and the candidates for governor are split over whether Alaska needs new revenue. We tested several other ways to pay for state services and balance the budget. Cutting the PFD is the least popular by far, with only 22% in support and 70% opposed. Voters are evenly split on a state income tax meant to fund public schools and smaller class sizes (48% in support, 47% opposed), and on an income tax where people with higher incomes pay a higher rate (48% in support, 48% opposed). Other options the University of Alaska’s Institute of Social and Economic Research studied this year are less popular, including a state sales tax that is higher in the summer than in the winter (43% in support), a year-round sales tax (35% in support), and a flat income tax (32% in support). An income tax that gives residents a tax break equal to the full statutory PFDs they missed in recent years is also underwater (38% in support).

 
 

What Alaska Voters Want From Their Next Governor

We asked voters what one sentence they would write to the next governor. The top request, from 14%, is to fund schools and teachers, followed by developing resources (10%), being honest and listening to Alaskans (10%), protecting the PFD (8%), and working across party lines (8%). Local Police Are Popular, While AI Surveillance and Data Centers Are Not

Ahead of its 60th annual convention, AFN is the most popular statewide institution we tested, with a +43 net favorability (63% favorable, 20% unfavorable). The Alaska Oil and Gas Association has a +22 net favorability, the Alaska Republican Party −9, and the Alaska Democratic Party −22. Local police departments are the most popular institution tested (+52).

Voters are far less favorable toward AI (−20) and data centers (−38), and most unfavorable of all toward Flock Safety cameras and similar AI surveillance technology (−60), which 74% of voters view unfavorably, including 60% of Republicans. In Anchorage, where police are expanding a real-time crime center through a contract with Axon (formerly known as Taser), a Flock competitor, 79% of voters view Flock and similar AI surveillance technology unfavorably. And voters in every region of the state view data centers unfavorably, even as Alaska has seen as many as 15 data center proposals.

 
 

Alaska voters view their local mayor favorably overall (+16), but not in Anchorage (−4). Anchorage voters disapprove of their mayor’s handling of homelessness (−37) and crime and public safety (−22), even as the mayor’s administration points to a 28% drop in unsheltered homelessness. Statewide, voters approve of their local mayor on crime and public safety (+11), but not on affordability (−14) or homelessness (−17).

Conclusion

With two weeks to go, Alaska’s races for governor, U.S. Senate, and U.S. House, as well as Ballot Measure 2, are all close. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins leads Bernadette Wilson, while Mary Peltola and Nick Begich hold narrow leads over Dan Sullivan and Bill Hill, respectively, and each race may come down to how fully voters rank their ballots. Ballot Measure 2 is tied, even though voters favor keeping open primaries and requiring groups that pay for political ads to disclose their donors. Above all, Alaskans are worried about the cost of living, more so than in any of our recent surveys, and they want candidates to talk more about it.

Survey Methodology

From October 8 to 16, 2026, Data for Progress conducted a survey of 694 likely voters in Alaska using SMS and web panel respondents. The sample was weighted to be representative of likely voters by age, gender, education, race, geography, and recalled vote. The survey was conducted in English. The margin of error associated with the sample size is ±4 percentage points. Results for subgroups of the sample are subject to increased margins of error. Partisanship reflected in tabulations is based on self-identified party affiliation, not partisan registration. For more information please visit dataforprogress.org/our-methodology.
