US reacts cautiously to Myanmar genocide ruling as EU praises ‘critical’ role of UN’s top court | International court of justice | The Guardian

The US has reacted with caution to the world’s highest court ruling that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya minority, while the EU more effusively framed the decision as a “landmark” judgment.
The historic ruling from the international court of justice (ICJ) – which has never previously found a state responsible for genocide – could set a precedent for future genocide claims, notably a case against Israel.
The US state department said on Thursday Washington was examining the judgment while stressing the need to avoid “politicisation” of the ICJ.
Donald Trump’s administration has taken a dim view of international law, in particular vowing to dismantle the international criminal court (ICC), which it regards as a renegade globalist institution. The ICJ and ICC are separate entities and the administration has yet to question the legitimacy of the ICJ.
The state department reiterated US support for the people of Myanmar and said it shared “grave concerns over the continued abuses of human rights against Rohingya Muslims and members of other minority groups”. It noted the US is the largest single humanitarian donor to the Rohingya humanitarian crisis response.
In Brussels, the EU said Myanmar must fully implement the ICJ’s ruling “without delay” and that “compliance with ICJ’s decisions is not optional, it is an obligation under international law”.
“The European Union holds the ICJ in the highest regard and remains steadfast in its support for its critical role in the peaceful resolution of disputes and the upholding of the rule of law at international level,” an EU spokesperson said in a statement.
“The Rohingya people have endured immense suffering, including displacement and violence, and their plight must not be forgotten. This landmark ruling underscores the imperative of justice for victims and the necessity of upholding international law.”
The UN secretary general, António Guterres, called the ruling “a demonstration of international justice and a reaffirmation that impunity for such horrific acts must not stand”. Shortly after the ruling was read, the UK foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, said his government was expelling Myanmar’s defence attache from the UK and “calling urgently for a UN security council session to discuss the judgment”.
Australia’s foreign minister, Penny Wong, called for those behind the Myanmar military’s grave human rights abuses to face justice.
In August 2017, Myanmar’s military launched what it called a clearance campaign in Rakhine state in response to an attack by a Rohingya insurgent group. The campaign led to accusations that security forces committed mass rapes and killings and burned thousands of homes.
The court said evidence showed Myanmar forces killed about 10,000 Rohingya.
Under the 1948 convention, to establish that genocide occurred, the court had to find that there was an intent to destroy the Rohingya “in whole or in part”.
About 1.2 million Rohingya refugees now live in Bangladesh, primarily in crowded camps across Cox’s Bazar district, where they endure harsh conditions marked by fire hazards, infectious diseases, monsoon landslides, inadequate sanitation and strict work restrictions.
Judges at the court based in The Hague have also been asked to rule on other allegations of genocide.
South Africa has accused Israel of breaching the genocide convention during its offensive in Gaza, a claim Israel vehemently denies. That case, which the US has pushed back on, is years from completion. An interim ICJ judgment in 2024 ordered Israel to ensure its forces do not commit acts of genocide against Palestinians in Gaza.
Ukraine also has a case pending against Russia. Shortly after Moscow’s full-scale invasion in February 2022, Kyiv accused Russia of fabricating genocide claims against Ukraine to justify the attack.
