Are thousands of absentee ballots in North Carolina being rejected? Here’s what we know

By Naomi Kowles
Published: Oct. 21, 2026 at 5:16 PM EDT
Add Us On GoogleAdd as a preferred source on Google

CHARLOTTE, N.C. (WBTV) - Claims that absentee ballots in North Carolina are being rejected at a higher rate in this cycle than in the 2022 midterm elections appear to agree with official statewide data.

However, claims that the rate of rejection is double that of 2022 may be premature, according to a WBTV analysis of available state and county-level numbers.

Tuesday evening, Gov. Josh Stein tweeted that “thousands more absentee ballots” were being rejected in this election cycle, or at a rate “twice as high” as in 2022’s midterm cycle.

That rejection rate, Gov. Stein said, was due to new rules set in September by the North Carolina State Board of Elections with a vote along party lines.

The State Board of Elections’ last-minute changes to voting by mail are leading to thousands more absentee ballots being rejected this election - at a rejection rate twice as high as in 2022. It’s infuriating. North Carolinians, please make a plan to vote this year, in person if…

— Governor Josh Stein (@NC_Governor) October 20, 2026

The rules effectively set stricter requirements on how voters filled and sealed their ballot envelopes as well as signature verification.

If those requirements are not followed, the ballot would not be counted. The new rules do require local election officials to reach out to the voter with a reissued ballot package with instructions on how to fix their vote.

The rules stemmed from a legal challenge to previous envelope sealing practices, brought by the North Carolina Republican Party and the Republican National Committee.

Where the claims come from

Gov. Josh Stein’s tweets came amid a Tuesday report from an organization founded by an elections attorney affiliated with the Democrat Party. A spokesperson confirmed his statements were based in the reporting from that organization.

That report from the Democracy Docket cited data from Common Cause NC, along with analysis that extrapolated out how many absentee ballots could be spoiled over the course of the election cycle based on current rejection rates.

The report also cites county-level data from a Wake County board of elections member, who tweeted that 2.5% of their absentee ballots were being spoiled because of the new sealing requirements.

In a statement to WBTV, a spokesperson for Common Cause NC said that their data is gathered based on on-the-ground monitors who track county absentee review meetings.

“Volunteer reports are not official results of absentee ballot counts, spoiled ballot counts, or reasons for ballot rejection,” the spokesperson said.

“Data reports shared by Common Cause and its partner organizations are informed by eye witnessing county board meetings and pulling information from meeting minutes.”

What to know about state, county-level official data

In a statement on Wednesday, the spokesperson for the North Carolina State Board of Elections said that their absentee ballot spoiling rate was sitting at 3.1% statewide, up from 2.6% at this point in time in 2022.

WBTV has requested the underlying data for those statistics.

That data appears to differ from reports claiming that the absentee ballot spoiling rate is double that from the previous midterm cycle.

“We do not immediately have the data that the published reports are based on, so there is no way for us to confirm or deny them,” spokesperson Jason Tyson wrote.

Not all of the spoiled ballots would be for reasons directly related to the new sealing and signing rules.

However, data that WBTV obtained from Mecklenburg County -- as well as the data that the Wake County election board member tweeted -- indicates a high percentage of spoiled ballots are due to the sealing requirements.

In Mecklenburg County, elections director Mike Dickerson told WBTV that about 250 out of 572 spoiled absentee ballots so far had been rejected because of the new signing requirements.

Of those 250, all voters had been contacted and 50 so far had either returned new ballots that complied with the requirements, or in one case voted early in person instead.

Over the lifeline of the election cycle in 2022, Dickerson said 9% of total absentee ballot requests were spoiled (3,113 of 33,403).

As of this point in the election cycle for this year, the spoiling rate so far is at 3% of total absentee ballot requests (572 of 18,900).

Bottom line

The bottom line: more absentee ballots are being spoiled in this cycle than in the previous midterm cycle, according to the state’s rate of just over 3%.

Many of those spoiled ballots are due to the new sealing requirements, when examining county-level data in Mecklenburg and Wake Counties.

However, it is not clear at this time whether the spoiling rate is truly double that from the 2022 midterm cycle at this point, or whether thousands of absentee ballots will be spoiled over the lifetime of this election cycle because of those rules.

Voters can still cast ballot if absentee is rejected (spoiled)

If your absentee ballot has been rejected, you can still vote using early in-person voting or at your local polling place on Election Day.

Additionally, if the spoiled absentee ballot was identified by the Friday before Election Day, a voter is also issued a new absentee ballot and could vote in that way.

County boards of elections are directed to contact voters when there are deficiencies with their absentee ballot, according to the NCSBE.

They recommend you provide your phone number or email address on the request form to make that contact easier.

You can check the status of your ballot here or here.

“We continue to stress to all absentee voters the importance of closely following all instructions that come with their ballot to ensure they comply with the law, and to return their ballots as soon as possible to ensure there is time to cure any deficiencies or receive a new ballot,” NCSBE spokesperson Jason Tyson noted.

Federal judge ruling clears way for new rules

This week’s controversy over ballot rejection rates comes after a federal judge issued a ruling late Monday evening, Oct. 19, that allowed the new rules to stand for the treatment of absentee ballots.

The N.C. Alliance for Retired Americans and other voting advocacy groups brought the lawsuit, arguing that the new rules would prevent otherwise-valid ballots from being counted.

Judge Thomas Schroeder, nominated in 2007 by former President George W. Bush, held that both the lawsuit and then the motion for preliminary injunction came too late for the rules to be stopped for this midterm cycle.

More than 31,000 absentee ballots had already been cast by the time a motion hearing was held.

“The Supreme Court has repeatedly admonished lower courts that they ‘should ordinarily not alter the election rules on the eve of an election,’” Judge Schroeder wrote.

Additionally, Judge Schroeder’s ruling stated the plaintiffs did not make a ‘clear showing’ that the lawsuit would succeed on its merits if allowed to continue to trial.
