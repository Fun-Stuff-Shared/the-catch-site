Lead Explosion: experts question emergency as Army vows blast will proceed

Army has yet to give an exact timeline for the planned detonation; it previously said it would happen sometime on Monday
DES MOINES, Iowa (KCRG) - With the EPA and Army saying they will move ahead with a controlled explosion of lead azide, environmental groups are pushing courts to stop them claiming the danger is not as great as the Army has claimed.
After the U.S. Court of Appeals 8th circuit put a hold on the permit for the blast, the Army said it will move forward anyway calling it an emergency. A new court filing Saturday from experts questions the emergency status. It is unclear when a judge might issue an order on the issue or whether the court would have the power to block the Army’s action.
In court filings on Saturday, the US Army and EPA said it planned to go ahead with the controlled demolition of about 5 tons of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown in southeast Iowa. That plan set off public panic last week as the Army warned the blast would send a plume of lead particles drifting for miles across parts of Iowa and Illinois.
The Army said it plans to carry out the blast by Tuesday but, unlike the initial plan, it has not provided an exact timeline, plans for evacuations or shelter and has not released any maps detailing the expected fallout from toxic lead dust and airborne particles.
The Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club filed the lawsuit to block the explosion. A judge issued a temporary stay on the EPA permit allowing the blast. However, the EPA and U.S. Army said it does not need a permit, saying it needs to protect the public from dangers of an uncontrolled explosion. It claims the colder weather increases the risk of the lead azide becoming more unstable and exploding without warning.
In response on Sunday, the environmental groups pushed back on that claim of an imminent danger. The appeal includes affidavits from two scientific experts claiming the Army is overstating the emergency nature of the lead azide.
The Army has warned the lead azide, which has sat in storage on the site for years, showed crystalization as it deteriorates. That makes it prone to an unplanned and uncontrolled explosion. The Army says cold weather makes it more unstable and prone to exploding.
Dr. Mark Thomas, a retired EPA official, pointed to the Army saying the lead azide is buried in earthen bunkers. He says that soil would provide enough insulation to protect it from cold weather.
“The forecast cold period is therefore comparable to conditions the stored material has already experienced this month without incident,” Dr. Thomas said in court filings.
The legal response notes the Army had sought a contract for lead azide neutralization for the ammunition plant in September 2025. The Army has said it has been working on the detonation plan since March 2026. The environmental groups’ experts note that long waiting period undermines the Army’s claim of an impending explosion.
Dr. Ranajit Sahu, a mechanical and chemical engineer, also writes in the lawsuit that the Army and EPA appear to have overlooked the risk of a controlled explosion to the public and overlooked alternative methods to destroying the lead azide.
“Given the lack of detail provided to the public, it is clear that the potential release of such dangerous chemicals and their impacts have not been fully assessed in planning for this detonation, ” Dr. Sahu writes in court filings, adding that explosive demolition is rarely 100% effective in destroying material.
“The Army and EPA should engage with independent experts to comprehensively and transparently consider all alternatives to detonating the crystalized lead azide,” Dr. Sahu adds.
The Army originally planned to detonate the stockpile Friday, noting the lead azide had become too unstable to move after sitting on the site for years. It delayed that plan until Monday, citing public safety concerns and better public communication with many officials, including Iowa’s Governor, only learning of the plan this week.
When the detonation happens will determine the areas that could face potential fallout from the lead plume as the wind and weather conditions will dictate where the plume goes.
The EPA responded Sunday, asking the court to reject the Thomas and Sahu declarations the environmental groups attached to their stay motion.
The Justice Department contends that allowing outside experts to second‑guess EPA’s science now would violate core Administrative Procedure Act (APA) principles and undercut judicial deference to agency expertise on highly technical matters like explosives safety, dispersion modeling and toxicology.
The lawsuit from the environmental groups challenges an emergency permit the Environmental Protection Agency granted to the U.S. Army for the detonation.
The groups allege in their lawsuit that many Iowans did not receive information about potential lead exposure.
“Our federal government needs to do better at communicating the potential health risks of lead contamination in our communities and providing more time to keep our communities and vulnerable groups safe, like children and pregnant women,” said Sarah Green, the Executive Director at the Iowa Environmental Council.
The groups also criticized the Army’s decision to proceed with the detonation.
“Iowans deserve to see an Environmental and Health Impact Statement and investigation into better alternatives than this extreme planned explosion. We’re hoping the Judge halts this action before it takes place tomorrow,” said Wally Taylor, Attorney and Legal Chair for the Sierra Club Iowa Chapter.
On Friday evening, Governor Kim Reynolds activated the State Emergency Operations Center (SEOC) in order for state agencies to better coordinate ahead of the planned detonation.
