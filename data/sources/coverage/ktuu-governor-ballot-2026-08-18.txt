‘This should be it… for now😊’: Redacted public records reveal lead-up to Petersburg Sullivan party removal, Gov. Dunleavy involved

ANCHORAGE, Alaska (KTUU) - Newly released records show Alaska’s governor convened the state’s highest election officials hours after a court forced the state to put Dan J. Sullivan back on the ballot. His office won’t say what happened in that meeting.
Dan J. Sullivan, the Petersburg man with the same name as the incumbent senator, has made headlines time and time again during the primary election season. He’s the only candidate without a party listed on the ballot, despite being a registered Republican.
Alaska’s News Source has been trying to learn why, an effort that has been met with opposition and pushback from the state. Elections staff have not answered questions, delayed public records requests and grabbed a KTUU camera when asking Elections Director Carol Beecher how the decision was made.
In records received Friday, Alaska’s News Source learned Gov. Mike Dunleavy, R-Alaska, was involved in the decision.
“The Governor has asked that the Lt. Governor, Carol [Beecher], and Acting AG [Cori Mills] join teleconference this afternoon, Monday, 06.29.26 ~ 4:45 p.m. re: Dan Sullivan Case,” an email from Dunleavy’s Director of Scheduling Janice Mason reads.
It’s not clear what role Dunleavy played in those conversations or why he called the meeting.
Alaska’s lieutenant governor is in charge of overseeing elections and the elections director reports to her. Dunleavy’s office has frequently asserted to Alaska’s News Source that election-related issues should be directed to her office.
Alaska’s News Source reached out to Dunleavy’s office Friday evening with a request for comment. As of Monday’s deadline, a response has not been returned.
More than 70 documents were completely redacted from the public records request, many of them from the day the decision was made.
Fifty-three documents were redacted on June 29, the day of the Supreme Court decision, and nine documents the following day. Forty-five of those documents were redacted for “deliberative process,” which a 1992 Alaska Law Department memo describes as “internal communications made within the executive branch with the expectation of confidentiality.”
Dunleavy’s email to those in charge of overseeing the state’s elections came hours after the Alaska Supreme Court ruled the state had to reinstate Sullivan back on the ballot. The division argued Sullivan did not meet a “good faith” requirement, which the court said did not exist in statute.
In the court hearing, the state’s Chief Justice Susan Carney scrutinized the state’s argument, saying a remedy for the issue would be to exclude Sullivan’s party altogether.
“The [election] division’s proposal not to list him as Republican after certifying his declaration of candidacy as a Republican would appear to be a little outside its discretion, wouldn’t it?” Carney asked during the hearing. “Don’t they have to list him as a Republican?”
After the court ruled, the division began working on a condensed timeline to differentiate between the two, with ballots just hours from being printed.
At 4:30 p.m., 15 minutes before the meeting Dunleavy requested, Beecher emailed Elections Program Manager Brian Jackson, writing “Here are the same ballots to prepare, please.”
Beneath that, several lines of black, redacted text — presumably the differentiations between the two candidates — Jackson later asked in the thread, “shall update his listing on the web to certified and as it’s listed on the sample?”
After Beecher sent the message with redacted text, Jackson said hours later he was going to “call it a night” and would prepare “the final database tomorrow.”
Beecher replied, “This should be it… for now 😊.”
Lt. Gov. Nancy Dahlstrom told Alaska’s News Source on July 27 that the decision to remove Sullivan’s party was to “maximize differentiation.”
“When I look at different media reports, I’ve seen people say this favors one Dan, or this favors the other Dan, or this favors Peltola,” she said. “Again, we just want voters to know who they are voting for so they can make their choice.”
When asked whether removing the Petersburg man’s party and adding the words “incumbent” next to the senator would favor the two-term senator — as state lawmakers suggested — she said that the Petersburg Sullivan “has not complained about that.”
But Dan J. Sullivan has complained about the issue, albeit not enough to litigate it again.
“This ballot, instead, is another example of how they are stacking the deck for the Senator,” he said on social media. “The current ballot is not equitable in fashion but I am more than ready to move past this and focus on campaign issues.”
Transparency in record request
Also unveiled in the records request, a plethora of emails from members of the media asking for comment and not getting answers from the division.
In several emails, including emails sent by a KTUU reporter, Beecher and Division Spokesperson Steve Kirch forwarded the emails to each other, but what they spoke about is redacted.
Several publicly released emails were from reporters requesting clarification on how the state made its decision. In most cases, those requests were not returned. One request on June 29 from Newsweek Night Editor Anna Commander shows Beecher forwarded the message to Kirch saying she had “No plan to respond at this time – but for your list.”
With no answers on how the state made the decision, Alaska’s News Source kept pushing by going to the elections division, filing public records requests and attempting to interview the elections director.
In an email on June 30, Kirch — a former KTUU reporter — said he thought Alaska’s News Source asking the Elections Division questions was “unethical reporting.”
How we got here
The two Sullivans — one a two-term senator (Dan S. Sullivan) and the other a retired teacher from the small southeast town of Petersburg (Dan J. Sullivan) — have been a flashpoint in the 2026 race for Senate.
Dan J. Sullivan entered the race at the end of May and was removed from the ballot on June 15 by the elections division, citing the following reasons:
- Choosing the name “Dan Sullivan” was intended to “confuse” himself with another candidate.
- Sullivan’s party choice “strongly suggests an intent to confuse” himself with the incumbent Senator.
- Sullivan’s campaign imagery “appears to be [a] deliberate” copy of the senator’s.
- Sullivan’s use of a political consultant who has previously advocated for Democrat Mary Peltola, also running for the same Senate seat.
That case went to a Superior Court and the State Supreme Court. In both cases, the courts sided with the Petersburg Sullivan, ultimately reinstating the candidate on the ballot — one day before the state’s deadline to print ballots.
The state argued candidates must file in “good faith,” which the Division of Elections used to justify Sullivan’s removal. The Supreme Court’s order does not address that standard directly.
Chief Justice Susan Carney said in a hearing before the decision that a full rationale would come at a later date. Almost a month later, the court still has not issued an explanation for the ruling.
National Republican groups like the NRSC have disputed that his party choice, Republican, was chosen to confuse voters, something Sullivan has denied. The argument made to the State Supreme Court from the Division’s attorney, Christopher Murray, cited an election law provision saying a candidate must be registered “as affiliated with a political party or group.” Their solution was to nix his party affiliation altogether.
“If ... he ever registered with the Republican Party, he did it when he filed his declaration of candidacy,” Murray said.
“But he’s registered,” Justice Jennifer Henderson responded.
Concern continued.
“The [election] division’s proposal not to list him as Republican after certifying his declaration of candidacy as a Republican would appear to be a little outside its discretion, wouldn’t it?” Chief Justice Susan Carney asked during the hearing. “Don’t they have to list him as a Republican?”
The one-paragraph order from the court directed the Division to determine how Sullivan would appear on the ballot “within the confines of existing Alaska ballot design law.”
But when the ballot was printed, the concerns of the justices in removing the candidate’s party didn’t make its way onto the ballot. Sullivan’s name was altered on the ballot to “Sullivan, Daniel J. Jr.” and he was suddenly the only candidate in the race without a party.
Since the ballot was printed, Alaska’s News Source has repeatedly asked the Division of Elections why his party was removed.
On June 30, elections division staff did not return the request for comment, so Alaska’s News Source went to the Division’s Anchorage offices. Kirch spoke with a reporter briefly, then hung up when pressed for an explanation.
Alaska’s News Source filed a public records request that day. On July 16, Beecher responded, saying she wouldn’t say how or why Sullivan’s party affiliation was stripped from the August primary ballot and delayed the public records request.
“DOE will not identify the records ‘sufficient to show’ how, why, when, or where decisions or actions occurred,” Beecher’s letter reads, citing election statutes.
Beecher interpreted KTUU’s formal records request to fall under an “electronic services and products” request, which is a more time-consuming and costly interpretation for delivery, versus a standard records request for the specific documents as filed by KTUU.
On July 22, Beecher and Kirch appeared at a legislative hearing over “Candidate Eligibility Criteria.” The hearing largely centered on how Dan J. Sullivan was formatted on the ballot, as well as how Sen. Dan Sullivan appeared, particularly over the word “incumbent” next to his name.
Beecher said the decision was made to “maximize differentiation,” and “we did not want any voters to be confused between the two candidates.”
As she left the building, Alaska’s News Source asked her the same question, but she did not answer as Kirch grabbed a KTUU camera and covered it with his hand.
