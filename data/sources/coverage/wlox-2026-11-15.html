Secretary of State Michael Watson talks about 2026 election, use of Experian data

By WLOX Staff
Published: Nov. 15, 2026 at 3:41 PM CST
Add Us On GoogleAdd as a preferred source on Google

BILOXI, Miss. (WLOX) — Mississippi Secretary of State Michael Watson said the state’s process for the midterm elections followed state law after the mail-in and affidavit ballot count took more than a week to complete.

Thousands of people voted by affidavit ballot after being deemed inactive on the voter rolls.

The election has raised questions about how voters were flagged as inactive and whether they received notices.

Watson said the affidavit process was designed to catch errors and ensure eligible voters’ ballots count. He said any voter can cast an affidavit ballot.

“It is a catch-all,” Watson said. “If we made any mistakes, any errors, we were caught by this affidavit process, we then, again, reactivate the voter. If they should have been counted, if they’re a legal voter, their vote counts.”

While anyone can cast an affidavit ballot, Watson said the information on the voter rolls, whether the voter is on the active list or the inactive list, has to align with the voter’s polling place. As an example, he said a woman called him and said her precinct was full, so she planned to vote by affidavit ballot at another precinct.

“I said, ‘Ma’am, you can vote affidavit ballot under the precinct, but your vote will not count because you’re not voting in your precinct,’” Watson said.

He added that the whole process allows for several checks in order to make sure voters who can vote are able to cast their ballot.

“Look, Mississippi law didn’t change. The process didn’t change. We did it exactly how we normally do it,” Watson said. “We do voter roll maintenance, and then on the back end, we make sure the affidavit process is there to catch any errors that were made.”

Watson said the process takes time. He said the timelines are set by law.

“It takes some time, but we got it right, and so I’m really thankful for our clerks and our commissioners for the work that they’ve done,” he said. “Counties have 10 days to upload their official results. You’ve got five days to come in to show your ID if you forgot it at the poll. The 10-day process is by law. Nothing changed.”

He said the Election Assistance Commission, under the Biden administration, brought together the commission and Experian for a pilot program on cleaning up voter rolls.

“Lafayette County, Mississippi, was in the pilot program for the country. They came back with some really good results,” Watson said. The EAC’s report on the program is online.

Watson said the state downloaded Experian information in July 2025. He said 42 or more counties had special elections that fall.

“We uploaded the rest of it to finish it up in 2026,” he said.

He said Experian information came from sources including the U.S. Postal Service and credit card bureaus, but his office now uses different sources.

Watson also said federal law requires him to provide election commissioners with actionable data, but that the responsibility to determine which data was valid falls on the election commissioners.

“But we have moved on. We’re now using IDI. They draw information from all three credit bureaus. They then compare the information, and they score the data. Is this actionable or not?” Watson said. “I have to provide election commissioners with actionable data. Their job is to take that data and say, ‘You know what? This is good data. This is bad data. If it’s good, I’m going to move someone into the inactive status.’”

The EAC report essentially said that using third-party sources in addition to traditional records to maintain voter rolls is advised. The report also suggested that voters be sent a notice telling them that their registration requires attention.

The secretary of state’s website says sending those notices is its procedure.

If a voter’s address on file was incorrect, however, a mailed notice might not have reached them.

Watson also disputed the number Mississippi Today reported as Mississippi voters purged from voter rolls. He said voters were not removed, but instead marked inactive, which would still allow their information to be verified after they cast an affidavit ballot.

“They said 230,000 folks were moved in 2026. That’s a lie. That is the total number of inactive voters over the last four to six years, not just 2026,” Watson said.

Watson said some people have said precincts were too small or there were not enough machines. He said ultimately the Board of Supervisors selects the precincts.

“I’m not pointing fingers. I’m just saying, and this is an important piece, I just want people to understand what the law says. Whose job is what?” he said. “I want to make sure people understand it takes a team to run elections. We train our commissioners. We train our clerks. Our commissioners then train our poll workers. We’ve got additional training online for our poll workers. We’ve done everything we can to make sure that we’ve done this thing correctly.”

He reiterated that he thought commissioners did a good job conducting the election, but that post-election audits will give a better idea of how well everything went.

“The phone was ringing off the hook, and it was our clerks and our commissioners asking, ‘Hey, we want to make sure we’re following the law. Here’s a question that we have,’” Watson said. “Those post-election audit reports will come out, and I feel pretty confident that we’re going to see a good job done by all of our election officials,” he said.

Watson said voters should always check their voting status before an election.

“I’ve told people time and time again, check your status. It’s very easy on our website to do so.”

He said some people say they should not have to check their status.

“Well, what if that poll worker or what if that election commissioner or what if that circuit clerk made an error? Humans are involved, so sometimes we mess up,” Watson said. “So it’s key to check your information going into an election to make sure, again, I’m on the active status. I can go cast my vote, and it’s going to count.”
