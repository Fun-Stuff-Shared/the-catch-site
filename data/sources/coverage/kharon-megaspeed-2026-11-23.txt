BIS Adds Nvidia-Buying Megaspeed Network to Entity List Amid Mounting Pressure | Kharon

The Commerce Department added suspected Chinese spy Alice Huang’s Megaspeed and affiliated firms to its export-control blacklist on Monday, hours after Nvidia announced it was reinstating Jensen Huang as chief executive.
The additions to the Bureau of Industry and Security’s Entity List, the first since October 2025, come more than two years since the Commerce Department said it opened a probe into Megaspeed.
The listings come amid mounting criticism of the Trump administration, including from within its own party, for its handling of the Nvidia scandal and for an export-control policy that critics say put national security at risk by giving China access to the United States’ most advanced chip technology.
In its official notice of the additions, posted in the Federal Register, BIS cited the companies’ “significant risk” of diversion to “unauthorized end uses or end users.”
Zoom in: In addition to four Megaspeed units that Alice Huang allegedly used to help provide Chinese firms — including parties on the Entity List or tied to Beijing’s military — access to Nvidia chips and compute power, BIS also added three associated firms tied to her corporate network: Speedmatrix Sdn Bhd, Apex Enterprise Solutions (Singapore) Private Limited, and Swiftdata Pte. Ltd.
Inclusion on the Entity List requires any U.S. exporter to secure a license from the BIS, but with a presumption of denial that practically makes companies export pariahs.
From Kharon’s research: Alice Huang fell under U.S. scrutiny primarily on the suspicion that she had funneled billions of dollars’ worth of restricted American AI chips to China, while working for the Ministry of State Security. But Huang’s network also acquired and rented out compute capacity for Chinese companies to train their AI systems or re-rent it in turn, records show, exploiting a remote-access loophole in U.S. export-control policy.
With her sister’s help, a Kharon investigation found, Alice Huang intersected with a web of companies across Asia and in the Caribbean that facilitated access to U.S. tech and obscured Chinese ties.
Big picture: The Entity List additions follow last week’s publication by BIS of a new rule expanding the list of countries requiring export licenses for compute power to include the Megaspeed network’s three hubs of Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. That rule also strengthens due-diligence requirements to ensure, it says, that U.S. computing power in foreign data centers “is not used in a manner contrary to U.S. national security or foreign policy interests.”
Indicative of the political feud over the Nvidia debacle, it was Treasury and the National Security Council that wrote last week's rule closing the remote-access loophole that Megaspeed's network exploited. Promulgation of that rule followed months of criticism from lawmakers, outside China hawks, and, reportedly, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Secretary of State Marco Rubio that Commerce had essentially left Chinese firms free to rent the advanced Nvidia computing power they were barred from buying.
In a joint statement last week, the Commerce and Justice Department said that BIS had launched an export-control probe into Megaspeed in 2024 and that Nvidia had put the company on its “no sell” list. BIS agents had interviewed Alice Huang at the company’s offices in Singapore in June 2025, the statement said, and the Justice Department took over an investigation.
In the 17 months after that BIS interview, Commerce took no public action against Megaspeed, even as Treasury, the Pentagon, the State Department, and the U.S. Trade Representative pushed for months for it to be added to the Entity List, according to Bessent’s account.
Democratic lawmakers said Commerce ignored national security concerns that arose in 2025, pointing to the absence of Entity List additions, as the Trump administration sought to preserve its trade detente with China.
