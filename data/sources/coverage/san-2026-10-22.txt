Fake pollster earlier said ‘We encourage reporters to ask us any questions’

In August, Straight Arrow exchanged messages with a person using the email address on a website for Wedgewood Polls, which had no phone number, employees or address.
In response, the person who did not identify themself said he and the other person at the firm were anonymous in order to avoid online harassment and doxing. Then, they invited scrutiny of their work.
“Because we have chosen not to make our identities public, we believe it is especially important to be fully transparent about our methodology, how our polls are conducted, and how our organization operates,” this person wrote.
On Tuesday, Wedgewood posted a message acknowledging it fabricated its polls, calling it a test to demonstrate how easy it was to inject fictitious data into coverage of American politics.

Wedgewood’s admission came after it submitted what it claimed was the underlying data of a poll to the website FiftyPlusOne, which was investigating the firm’s work on suspicion of forgery. It was exactly the sort of scrutiny Wedgewood had invited months earlier in the messages to Straight Arrow:
“We encourage reporters to ask us any questions about our methodology, procedures, or operations that they believe would help them independently assess our legitimacy.”
In another message, the person wrote, “We are happy to answer detailed questions about any of those areas.”
The latest
Wedgewood’s admission Thursday is the latest unmasking of a dubious pollster.
In August, a 21-year-old college student acknowledged making a fake poll about the Los Angeles mayor’s race for his fictitious firm, Median Strategies, The Guardian reported this summer. FiftyPlusOne on Thursday said it would no longer include data from SoCal Strategies amid the site’s investigation into their work.
Some close poll-watchers have been wary of data coming from new and anonymous sources. Bill Scher, politics editor of Washington Monthly, identified Wedgewood months ago as a firm with a dubiously thin profile. Some people on X were skeptical of the firm too. Polling Director Scott Clement of The Washington Post last week flagged Wedgewood as suspicious.
The blast radius
Democrat Mallory McMorrow said Tuesday night she dropped out of her party’s Senate primary in Michigan this summer after a Wedgewood poll showed her support cratering, contradicting her campaign’s internal data.
“Donations dried up,” and “volunteers were devastated” and “Reporters hounded me with calls to drop out,” McMorrow wrote on X.
After listing many grievances with politics today, McMorrow wrote, “And media – who loves to ride the drama of a pollercoaster – needs to take a hard look at what’s legitimate and what’s not.”
McMorrow dropped out, and progressive Dr. Abdul El-Sayed narrowly defeated a moderate in the primary. He is now in a close race with the Republican nominee, former Rep. Mike Rogers.
Broadcasters may also be impacted by Wedgewood’s admission. Chairman Brendan Carr of the Federal Communications Commission wrote Wednesday, “there has been a significant surge in entirely Fake Polls and Suppression Polls designed to do nothing more than interfere with the regular functioning of the electoral process and alter the normal patterns of voter turnout and enthusiasm.” Without evidence, he said “Many broadcasters” shared bogus polls and encouraged them to provide “additional context” in order to be “consistent with FCC regulations.”
MS Now and NewsNation each cited a Wedgewood poll once, according to a Straight Arrow review of recent television archives. Messages sent to the outlet Thursday afternoon were not immediately returned.
Other media outlets are also dealing with the fallout.
Wedgewood had fabricated its work convincingly enough to be included in some major poll-tracking sites at The New York Times, FiftyPlusOne and VoteHub, each of which relegated the firm to a tier for non-elite sources.
In the August exchange with Straight Arrow, Wedgewood specifically cited their inclusion in high-profile websites as validation of their work.
“We also encourage reporters to speak with The New York Times and Fifty Plus One about their respective processes for reviewing and verifying Wedgewood’s polling before including our work in their aggregations,” the person wrote.
When asked about not being included in The Times’ list of “Select Pollsters,” Wedgewood said they and other newer and credible pollsters have not been included and that it “does not mean these pollsters are suspected of being illegitimate.”
In an editor’s note Wednesday, The Times wrote that Wedgewood underwent routine vetting before inclusion, and “answered a series of questions regarding the identities of the pollsters; the methods they were using to draw their sample, contact respondents and weight their polls; and the source of their funding.”
The Times also noted that Wedgewood “responded to similar vetting questions from FiftyPlusOne and VoteHub, both of which subsequently included Wedgewood’s releases in their trackers.”
Zachary Donnini, head of data science at VoteHub, wrote Tuesday night on X that he had previously spoken to the head pollster at Wedgewood. He said that person and the firm’s one other pollster were both college students. “They struck me as nice kids with at least some understanding of polling, but obviously no professional experience,” Donnini wrote. “With that in mind, we assigned them a D grade and included their polling data on our site.”
“A few years ago, I was a college student” with an interest in politics, Donnini wrote, before lamenting, “it’s going to be a hell of a lot harder for the next college kid who’s actually well-intentioned and super into politics to break into the space after this.”
In an email to Straight Arrow, Donnini said the site handles polls from 281 pollsters and they may revise their vetting after the elections.
“Wedgewood was always a red flag for me because they didn’t name themselves,” Scher, the editor from the Washington Monthly, told Straight Arrow Thursday. “Seems to me that there should be a standard here where you sign your name on your work to be considered credible. That should be one of the criteria that we use.”
Clement, from the Washington Post, said his outlet has 13 questions they look at when deciding whether to include a poll in their reporting. He also said one way Wedgewood may have been discovered earlier was to vet not just what they did, but simply who they are.
“That’s not technical. That’s good basic journalism,” he told Straight Arrow. “Those types of basic nuts and bolts of reporting — those work.”
