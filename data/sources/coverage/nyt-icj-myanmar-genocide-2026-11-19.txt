Myanmar Committed Genocide Against the Rohingya, Top Global Court Finds
In a decision with potentially broad implications, the International Court of Justice found a state culpable of genocide for the first time.
The International Court of Justice ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya Muslims, a landmark decision years in the making that makes the Southeast Asian nation the first state to be held to account for violating the 1948 Genocide Convention.
The ruling covers Myanmar’s deadly military campaign against the Rohingya minority, which killed at least 10,000 people in a few weeks in 2017 and forced three quarters of a million people to flee during that same period. More deaths and expulsions followed.
“The Republic of the Union of Myanmar has committed genocide against the Rohingya group in Northern Rakhine state,” Judge Yuji Iwasawa, the president of the I.C.J., said while reading the court’s decision on Thursday. The decision was made by 9 votes to 6.
The decision could have sweeping implications, including for another genocide case before the I.C.J., the United Nations’ highest judicial body: the accusation of genocide by Israel against the people of the Gaza Strip. The ruling opens the way for the court to consider restitution, and it would be up to the U.N. Security Council whether to enforce such measures.
A spokeswoman for the government of Myanmar, in the capital Naypyidaw, did not answer a phone call seeking comment after the verdict was handed down. Myanmar authorities have previously disputed the charge.
Among other findings, the court determined that Myanmar had failed to prevent genocide. While it was not found to have incited private people to commit genocide, the court found that it also failed to punish genocide.
“This is a case a decade in the making,” said Juliette McIntyre, a Senior Lecturer in Law at Adelaide University in Australia. “From the point of view of the Rohingya people, who have been waiting, and waiting, and waiting for justice, this is very important.”
Gambia filed suit against Myanmar in 2019 in the court, which is based in The Hague. While the West African country is thousands of miles from Myanmar, international law gives countries the ability to act against genocide anywhere in the world — even if they are not affected.
In the Jamtoli camp of Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh, people thronged to a public viewing party to watch the hourslong decision, cheering and weeping as the decision was read. Some held signs that said “thank you, Gambia.”
In The Hague, Dawda Jallow, the attorney general for Gambia, welcomed the decision to find Myanmar culpable of the “crime of crimes.” Speaking to journalists on the main staircase of the I.C.J.’s Peace Palace shortly after the ruling was read, he said, “we will stand with the Rohingya until justice is served.”
The violence at the core of the case took place in Myanmar’s far western Rakhine state, starting in 2016 and worsening in 2017. U.N. officials and human rights groups have called it a coordinated, scorched-earth campaign to remove the Rohingya — one that involved killing, wounding and raping, razing villages, and eventually forcing the expulsion of more than a million people to neighboring Bangladesh.
The Rohingya Muslims have a long history of repression in Myanmar. After the nation’s independence from Britain in 1948, they were one of many ethnic groups in the country, which was then known as Burma. But over time, they faced increasing attacks from members of the nation’s Buddhist majority.
A restrictive citizenship law in 1982 effectively rendered most Rohingya stateless. Various Myanmar governments, including a civilian authority led by the Nobel peace laureate Daw Aung San Suu Kyi, have refused to even acknowledge the Rohingya as a legitimate ethnic group, falsely referring to them instead as interlopers from neighboring Bangladesh.
Myanmar’s military called its campaign against the Rohingya “clearance operations.” Ms. Aung San Suu Kyi defended the military at the I.C.J., denying it committed genocide, before her government was deposed by the military and she was imprisoned in 2021. (After the coup, Myanmar descended into a civil war that is still raging.) About 1.2 million Rohingya now live in dire conditions in overcrowded camps in Bangladesh. Following the eruption of Myanmar’s civil war in 2021, much of the Rohingya homeland is now controlled by a milita from the Rakhine ethnic minority, which has also been accused of persecuting Rohingya.
Thursday’s decision hinged on whether Myanmar acted with the specific intent to physically destroy the Rohingya, in whole or in part, as an ethnic or religious group.
The court found that Myanmar took measures to wipe out the Rohingya people, who did constitute a protected ethnic group by the court’s judgment.
The case summary states that, among other atrocities, the court found that “widespread acts of extreme violence were perpetrated by the Myanmar security forces against children in northern Rakhine State,” that “in addition to the extreme hardship of the journey, the Myanmar security forces systematically shot at Rohingya as they were fleeing,” and that “the evidence demonstrates a deliberate, organized process” of destroying Rohingya villages.
“The only reasonable inference that can explain Myanmar’s actions is an intention to destroy a substantial part of the group,” Judge Iwasawa read from the ruling.
The court said that Myanmar must provide restitution — trying to put things back as they were, including through allowing return and reunification — and, where that fails, compensation. But the amount of compensation needed is not yet set, and will be determined in further proceedings.
The court does not have the power to enforce its own rulings, and the U.N. Security Council would need to uphold the decision. Two members of the Security Council, China and Russia, may opt to protect Myanmar. Each provides Myanmar with weapons it now uses to kill civilians in the civil war.
“Concerned governments” should “press Myanmar’s junta to comply with the judgment, and ensure the safe and voluntary return of Rohingya refugees to Myanmar,” Maria Elena Vignoli, senior international justice counsel at Human Rights Watch, said in a written statement following the announcement.
Notably, the United States has previously said that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, though that happened during the Biden administration.
“I think that you’re going to see relatively powerful states putting pressure on Myanmar,” said Ms. McIntyre, of Adelaide University. “How much they pay heed to that pressure is a question.”
Thursday’s decision was greeted by some in the international legal community as a sign that justice can still be served on a global stage, even as the Trump administration attacks international bodies. The administration plans to impose sweeping sanctions against another tribunal, the International Criminal Court, which could severely impair the court’s ability to function on a day-to-day basis.
“At a moment when international law and the institutions that uphold it face growing pressure, the Court’s decision reaffirms the vital role they play in establishing truth,” James Goldston, the executive director of the Open Society Justice Initiative, said in a statement.
For the Rohingya now in Bangladesh, the news was welcome. Yet tragedies continued after 2016 and 2017, the years ruled a genocide by the I.C.J.
Mohammed Yeasin said his wife and sister were killed in a drone attack in August 2024. The perpetrators were not the Myanmar military but the armed group from the Rakhine ethnic minority that shares its homeland with the Rohingya.
“I am happy about the I.C.J.,” he said. “But I don’t know when my sorrows will end.”
