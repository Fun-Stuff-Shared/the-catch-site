James Talarico’s Chances Against Ken Paxton—What Texas Results Show - Newsweek

Texas Senate candidates Ken Paxton and James Talarico closed out their midterm campaigns on Monday evening, with a warning tone from the Republican state attorney general and a promise to "leave it all on the field" from the Democratic state representative.
A Talarico victory would mark the first time since 1988 that Texas voters have sent a Democrat to the U.S. Senate, an outcome Paxton cautioned against. At a Tex-Mex restaurant in Collin County, he told supporters: "Understand what is actually at stake here: Texas has never been this close to slipping away. If Texas falls, we don't have another Texas to move to. Every poll shows that this is going to come down to the wire."
Over 220 miles away, Talarico told a roaring crowd at a concert venue in Austin: "There's that moment in every game, the last play of the last drive, when you have to leave it all on the field. Austin, we are in that moment."

As Texans head to the voting booth, closing polls give Talarico a slight edge over his opponent. But with such slim margins, there are no guarantees for a Democratic victory in the Lone Star State.
What Results Show: James Talarico v. Ken Paxton
Talarico, a 37-year-old Democratic state representative from the Austin area, secured 55 percent of the vote, becoming the first Democrat elected to the Senate from Texas since Lloyd Bentsen in 1988 and delivering one of the party's most significant victories of the 2026 midterms.
Talarico outperformed former Democratic candidates among Hispanic voters and in the Dallas suburbs.
James Talarico vs. Ken Paxton: What Latest Polls Show
Talarico received 51.3 percent compared with 45.4 percent for Paxton in an AtlasIntel poll, giving Talarico a 5.9-point lead going into Election Day.
The poll surveyed 1,678 respondents from October 28 to November 1 using AtlasIntel’s online Random Digital Recruitment method. It reported a margin of error of plus or minus two percentage points at a 95 percent confidence level.
Meanwhile, a New York Times/Siena College poll gives Talarico a 7-point advantage with 50 percent support to Paxton’s 43.
The Texas portion surveyed 1,215 likely voters from October 27 to November 1. With a reported margin of error of plus or minus 3.4 percentage points, Talarico’s lead is about twice that figure.
Other late polls also point to a close race. Data for Progress gave Talarico a lead, 49 percent to 45 percent, among 712 likely voters surveyed from October 23 to 26, although the margin of error was plus or minus 6.4 points. An Emerson College poll of 500 likely voters in Texas, conducted between October 31 and November 1, gave Talarico a narrow lead over Paxton—50 percent to 48 percent.
A CNN poll of 923 voters also put Talarico ahead with 49 percent to 43 percent, with a margin of error of plus or minus 4.2 points, and a YouGov survey conducted from October 26 to 29 showed Talarico leading 51 percent to 47 percent with a margin of error of plus or minus 2.9 points.
Quantus Insights was the only late poll to show Paxton ahead, 48.7 percent to 47.6 percent, among 1,221 likely voters surveyed from October 26 to 28.
Taken together, late polling generally leans toward Talarico, but there is still considerable uncertainty about the size of his advantage. AtlasIntel and NYT/Siena gave him leads of about 6 and 7 points, respectively, while Data for Progress and YouGov had a more conservative estimate with a 4 point lead—particularly noteworthy when taking into account margins of error.
YouGov had Talarico four points ahead, Texas Southern University had him two points ahead and CNN/SSRS put his advantage at six points. A Quantus Insights poll gave Paxton a one-point lead. A Trafalgar Group survey conducted October 31 to November 1 found the candidates tied at 48 percent.
Talarico, 37, is a former public-school teacher who currently represents part of the Austin area in the Texas House. Described as an economic populist, the Democrat has announced policy proposals. These include repealing tax cuts for the top 1 percent of earners, providing universal childcare and capping credit card interest rates.
Talarico supports a two-state solution to the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, health care reform and institutional reforms including term limits for members of Congress and banning congressional stock trading.
Ken Paxton, 63, is the Republican nominee and Texas attorney general; it is an office he has held since 2015 after previously serving in the Texas Legislature. Paxton reached the general election after defeating four-term Republican Senator John Cornyn for the GOP nomination.
A staunch ally of President Donald Trump, Paxton has pledged to advance the president's America First agenda in the Senate. This would be done by cutting taxes, tightening immigration enforcement and completing construction of the border wall. He opposes abortion, supports gun rights and has called for reductions in federal spending and regulation.
On the economy, Paxton has proposed new tax deductions for housing, health care and family expenses.
Stakes for Senate Control
Republicans currently hold a 53-47 Senate majority, counting the two independents who caucus with Democrats on the Democratic side.
Because Republican Vice President JD Vance can break a 50-50 tie, Democrats need to reach 51 seats to take control, meaning they must produce a net gain of four seats.
The most-straightforward route would be to retain all 13 seats on the ballot, including competitive races like Georgia, Michigan and New Hampshire, while flipping at least four Republican-held seats.
Victory for Talarico would give Democrats one of the four Republican-held seats they need, leaving the party needing three further net pickups if it successfully defended all its existing seats. While a win for Ken Paxton would preserve the Texas seat for Republicans, forcing Democrats to find all four necessary gains elsewhere.
Why the Texas Race Matters
Democrats have not won a statewide office in Texas since 1994, and the last Democrat elected to the U.S. Senate from Texas was Lloyd Bentsen in 1988.
Republicans have continued to win recent Senate races comfortably. In 2024, Republican Ted Cruz defeated Democrat Colin Allred 53.1 percent to 44.5 percent, an 8.6-point margin. The state also backed Trump by roughly 14 points in the 2024 presidential election.
Voter turnout in the state has already gone well beyond the last midterm, with more than 7.7 million early ballots cast before Election Day, over 94 percent of the total 2022 turnout.
At a rally in Lubbock on October 27, Talarico addressed an estimated 4,000 attendees where he said that "Texans are ready for change."
"We are done with the extremism and the corruption. We're done with the politics of division, and we are ready for something new," he told the crowd.
At a separate rally, also in Lubbock on the same night, President Trump addressed a crowd gathered for Paxton.
"If you go out and vote, we’re going to have a tremendous win in Texas," Trump told supporters, adding "We can’t lose the votes. It’s very close, and they can turn things."
Trump’s apparent concern about the Democrat’s ability to take Texas has been echoed by other Texas Republicans.
"Texas is excruciatingly close," Senator Ted Cruz said on an episode of his podcast last week. "We could lose Texas."
"Now, some conservatives, they say, ‘I don't trust the polls. The polls are all wrong,’" Cruz said. "Well, let me tell you that there is a bias in the polls. They do skew left, but I can tell you, having run a lot of campaigns, have examined a lot of numbers, these data are not fake. The Democrats are massively out-turning-out the Republicans."
Texas Comptroller Don Huffines, who is running for election as the state's chief financial officer, also issued a warning on X this week. "We are losing Texas," he warned. "Texas is the foundation of the Republican Party in America. When the foundation breaks, it ALL falls apart. When we lose Texas, we lose the USA."
