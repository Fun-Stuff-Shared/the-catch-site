U.S. closes loophole that let Chinese companies use top AI chips

The Commerce Department moved to further restrict the sale of top-tier American AI chips Monday morning, addressing mounting concerns about how companies in China and other countries might evade American export restrictions by taking advantage of longstanding loopholes.
As part of a new rule, data center customers in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil must now receive a special license from the Commerce Department in order to buy advanced AI chips from American companies such as Nvidia or AMD. Over the past several years, businesses in those countries have bought billions of dollars worth’ of high-end chips, raising concerns that some chips might be illegally smuggled into China.
The rule, issued by the department’s Bureau of Industry and Security, will also require existing overseas data center customers to receive a similar license if they provide “significant volumes” of computing power to companies whose headquarters are in China, Russia, Cuba or other countries banned from receiving certain types of advanced American technology. Companies in these countries have allowed Chinese businesses to access their most advanced AI chips remotely, skirting rules that prevented Chinese companies from accessing these chips on Chinese territory.
The regulation, technically termed an interim final rule, represents a major policy shift for the department, which has held off on adding new chip restrictions for the past year. Monday’s announcement was clearly framed as an effort that came from the wider Trump administration — it said the rule was “written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council.”
Several experts praised the changes, given the growing concerns about Chinese AI companies remotely using American chips.
“The measure is a long-overdue step in the right direction to address China’s remote access to huge computing hubs in Southeast Asia and elsewhere,” said Ryan Fedasiuk, a fellow at the Foundation for American Innovation who focuses on artificial intelligence. “The decision reflects that the Trump administration is taking seriously the national security risks associated with growing Chinese access to computing power.”
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had criticized the Commerce Department for its reticence to close export control loopholes last week. On Friday, he said that only the Commerce Department “has the pen” to issue new chip export regulations.
Chris McGuire, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations and key AI adviser in the Biden administration, called the rules a “major step forward.”
The update “is the first good-faith effort to actually close loopholes in export controls in a year and a half,” he wrote in a post on X.
In order to develop and serve AI systems to customers, companies need vast amounts of computing power. American AI chips, particularly from Nvidia and AMD, have long been regarded as the leading industry hardware on the market.

Chinese companies have been banned from buying these chips directly since 2022, when the Biden administration and then-Commerce Secretary Gina Raimondo clamped down on AI exports.
However, many experts posit that Chinese AI companies have simply shifted or outsourced their AI consumption to data centers located in nearby countries, including the five mentioned in Monday’s new rule. That remote access has been tolerated so far, but will be banned when Monday’s rule change goes into effect.
Countries in Southeast Asia have seen a boom in data center construction in recent years, as organizations in Malaysia and Thailand have purchased billions of dollars of Nvidia chips.
Megaspeed, the Singapore-based company at the heart of recent controversy given Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang’s alleged involvement with Alice Huang, one of its former executives, has planned to rent out its computing power to Alibaba and ByteDance, according to reports. Huang’s scandal — and the role of Alice Huang — put a spotlight on companies like Megaspeed and their activity, creating pressure on the Commerce Department to act.
The rule also formally rescinds an AI rule issued in the final days of the Biden administration. This edict, known as the AI Diffusion Rule, attempted to implement strict AI chip licensing requirements for countries around the world.
The Trump administration announced it would not enforce the rule shortly after taking office, but it had not formally rescinded the rule until Monday.
