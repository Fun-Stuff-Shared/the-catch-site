Judge allows Trump's $15 billion suit against New York Times to press forward

A federal judge on Thursday handed a major boost to President Donald Trump’s $15 billion defamation lawsuit against the New York Times and Penguin Random House by allowing it to proceed, ruling that Trump plausibly alleged two reporters lacked adequate support for statements in a book suggesting he had committed complex tax crimes.
U.S. District Judge Steven Merryday, a George H.W. Bush appointee in Tampa, Florida, allowed claims over Lucky Loser, a 2024 book by the outlet’s reporters Russ Buettner and Susanne Craig, to move forward to a potential future trial. He dismissed claims over two of the newspaper’s articles, one by Buettner and Craig and the other by Peter Baker, the paper’s chief White House correspondent.

“Knowing of the necessity for that opinion but failing to procure that opinion is a fine example of the purposeful avoidance of the truth,” Merryday wrote.
The ruling turned on the Supreme Court’s 1964 decision in New York Times v. Sullivan, which requires public officials suing for defamation to prove “actual malice,” meaning the defendants knew a statement was false or acted with reckless disregard for whether it was true. Although the case is far from being decided, legal experts say the case has the propensity to re-examine the scope of Sullivan‘s reach.
Merryday reasoned that publishing allegations of complex tax crimes without an explicit opinion from a qualified source who had examined whether the pertinent facts could support an inference of reckless disregard.
Trump’s legal team welcomed the decision.
“These Defendants will now have to answer for intentionally and maliciously defaming President Trump,” a spokesman said in a statement.
The New York Times and Penguin Random House have denied defaming Trump. In seeking dismissal, they argued that Trump failed to explain how the challenged statements were defamatory and that some were subjective opinions that could not be proven true or false.
Trump filed the lawsuit in September 2025, challenging coverage of his business career that touched on his inheritance from his father and his role in creating the reality television show, The Apprentice. Merryday struck the original complaint over its length and presentation and gave Trump’s lawyers a chance to refile. The operative complaint seeks at least $15 billion in compensatory damages, along with punitive damages and retractions.

Thursday’s ruling addressed whether Trump had pleaded enough facts for the book claims to proceed. Lawyers for both sides told Merryday in July that they had conducted limited fact-finding and had yet to take any depositions. The parties can ask the judge to rule on the evidence after discovery, and any claims that survive could go to a jury.
