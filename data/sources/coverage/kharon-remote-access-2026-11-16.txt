Congress is pursuing a separate route. The bipartisan Remote Access Security Act (RASA), which passed the House in January and awaits Senate action, would amend the Export Control Reform Act to explicitly give BIS explicit statutory authority overloud and network access to controlled U.S. items. BIS issued the new rule without that authority. Instead of regulating remote access directly, the rule relies on BIS's existing power over chip shipments: a license is required to send advanced chips to a data center that will serve restricted customers, and a data center that starts serving those customers after the chips arrive also needs a license, because BIS treats that change as a new transfer of the chips.
For chip suppliers, cloud providers, and data center operators, the compliance question now extends past where a chip ships to who can run workloads on it, and to who owns the companies running them.
What Is the Remote Access Loophole?
Remote access allows a user to run workloads on chips located elsewhere, including through rented cloud infrastructure. U.S. export controls have long barred shipping the most advanced chips to China, but they have generally not barred Chinese companies from renting the computing power of those chips in the United States or in third countries. The chip never has to cross a border. Before the new rule, the gap had three parts:
Legal: The Export Administration Regulations (EAR) control exports, reexports, and in-country transfers, and none of those terms clearly covers access over a network. In a January 2009 advisory opinion, BIS concluded that providing computational capacity through grid or cloud computing is not subject to the EAR, and it reaffirmed that position in 2014. Cloud providers built compliance programs around it. The new rule does not make providing computing capacity a controlled activity; it regulates the chips behind that capacity.
Operational: Even where license conditions apply, a provider sees the account holder, not necessarily the company behind it. Offshore subsidiaries and resellers can stand between a data center and the ultimate parent that uses its compute.
Geographic: Until the rule, exports of advanced chips to most data centers in Southeast Asia did not require a license unless the customer or its ultimate parent was headquartered in China or another Country Group D:5 destination, or in Macau. ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent have reportedly used advanced U.S. chips this way through data centers in Thailand, Malaysia, and Japan, CNBC reported in August 2026.
What Is the AI Remote Access Rule?
The interim final rule, titled "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors,” takes effect on the date it is published in the Federal Register, and BIS will accept comments for 60 days after publication. Following Executive Order 14434, the rule uses the term "Super Intelligence (SI)" in place of "artificial intelligence”; this article uses "AI." The rule has four main parts.
A data center end-use control for restricted customers
A new end-use control (§ 744.24 of the EAR) requires a license to export, reexport, or transfer (in-country) advanced computing items, including chips classified under Export Control Classification Numbers (ECCNs) 3A090 and 4A090, when there is knowledge that the items are destined for a data center that provides or will provide more than 12 million in aggregate total processing performance (TPP) to or for companies headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau, or whose ultimate parent is headquartered there. The threshold counts a single data center or "linked data centers" that share resources and workloads over networking infrastructure, including digitally linked facilities. BIS will review license applications case by case. The control also reaches indirect service: BIS states that it applies where a data center knowingly provides remote access to a customer outside Country Group D:5 that trains AI models for, or provides them to, a company headquartered there.
The control is not limited to new shipments; it also covers chips already in place at a data center. It applies to whoever holds the chips, even a party that had no role in the original shipment. If a data center begins serving restricted customers above the threshold, BIS treats that change as an in-country transfer, which requires a license. Data centers that were already supplying that level of computing power to companies headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau must obtain a license within 90 days of the rule's effective date to continue. Exporters that ship without a license must notify recipients in writing that a later change to a prohibited end use requires BIS authorization, and they cannot rely on the EAR's de minimis rules once U.S.-origin chips are incorporated into a foreign-made item destined for that end use.
The rule also sets a due diligence expectation. To support a finding that it lacked knowledge, an exporter is expected to obtain information on the foreign data center's know-your-customer (KYC) checks, including whether any customer is directly or indirectly owned or controlled by persons located or headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau. A data center that declines or is unable to provide that information presents a red flag that presumptively gives the exporter knowledge unless the exporter resolves it before proceeding. BIS states that this diligence is meant to keep companies from evading the control through corporate restructurings or spinoffs.
Computing power provided exclusively for a "commercial recommender system," an AI tool designed and used only to rank, filter, or suggest products, services, or content, is excluded if the provider reports the customer, the aggregate TPP supplied, and its due diligence to BIS. An AI system that generates text, images, audio, video, or software falls outside the exclusion, even if it also makes recommendations.
License requirements for five higher-risk destinations
The rule requires a license for exports, reexports, and in-country transfers of advanced computing items to or within Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil, which BIS describes as presenting “a higher-than-normal risk of diversion.” The Secretary of Commerce may add destinations. Applications will be reviewed case by case, including on the effectiveness of each country's export control regime and its cooperation with BIS on enforcement. Norway and South Africa, which appeared on the country list in pre-publication reporting by The Information, are not included.
The requirement does not apply when the ultimate consignee or end user is headquartered in the United States, or its ultimate parent is, provided the exporter files monthly reports to BIS. Each report must identify the parties and their ultimate parents, the products with their TPP and quantities, and the shipment dates. BIS said it will consider consequences for countries that tolerate evasion, including import restrictions, new license requirements on advanced semiconductor exports, downgrades in export control treatment, and Entity List designations of the foreign persons involved.
Tighter presumptions for foundries and chip packagers
The rule narrows the ways a front-end fabricator or outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT) provider can overcome the presumption that an advanced logic chip it produces is controlled under ECCN 3A090.a. That path is no longer available for chip designers located in, or with an ultimate parent headquartered in, Country Groups D:1, D:4, or D:5 or one of the five newly licensed countries, unless the designer is an approved or authorized integrated circuit (IC) designer. A new license requirement also covers fabricators and OSAT providers that make advanced chips for designers outside those approved and authorized categories, wherever the designer is located.
Formal rescission of the AI Diffusion Rule
The rule formally rescinds the January 2025 Framework for Artificial Intelligence Diffusion, which BIS announced in May 2025 it would not enforce. It removes the export control on AI model weights (ECCN 4E091), the country tiers, License Exception Artificial Intelligence Authorization (AIA), and the Data Center Validated End-User (VEU) authorization.
What Is the Remote Access Security Act (RASA)?
Congress is pursuing a separate route. The Remote Access Security Act (H.R. 2683) would amend the Export Control Reform Act of 2018 (ECRA) to make remote access a controlled activity alongside export, reexport, and in-country transfer. The House passed the bill 369-22 on Jan. 12, 2026, and it awaits action in the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs, where senators introduced a companion bill, S. 3519, on Dec. 17, 2025. The Senate version would reach only items on the Commerce Control List, accessed through a cloud infrastructure service by a “foreign person of concern,” meaning governments, entities, and persons tied to China, Russia, Iran, or North Korea, and its remote access authority would expire after 10 years. The House-passed bill defines remote access more broadly:
- Negligence is enough: The definition covers negligent access as well as knowing or purposeful access. The bill states that this does not lower the intent required for criminal liability, but records showing how users were vetted would carry more weight in any enforcement review.
- Foreign person, not foreign location: The trigger is who accesses the item, not where the server sits, which extends the logic of the deemed export rule to the network.
- A risk determination comes first: Controls would apply only where the Secretary of Commerce finds that use of an item could pose a serious risk to U.S. national security or foreign policy, which points to targeted rules rather than licensing for all cloud services.
- Licensing and enforcement: BIS could require licenses for remote access and bring enforcement actions against U.S. and non-U.S. parties that provide it.
How the Rule and the Remote Access Security Act Fit Together
The rule shows how far BIS can go under its current statute. It cites Sections 1753 and 1754 of ECRA, which authorize controls on exports, reexports, and in-country transfers, and it builds every remote access restriction on one of those activities: the license attaches to chips shipped to a data center that will serve restricted customers, and a data center's later decision to serve them counts as an in-country transfer. That approach reaches chips already installed, but only through the chips. Providing remote access is still not a controlled activity in itself, and the data center control applies only above the 12 million aggregate TPP threshold and only to service for companies tied to Country Group D:5 or Macau.
RASA would make remote access a controlled activity in its own right, giving BIS a direct basis to license and penalize the party that provides access and firmer legal footing for rules like this one. Until Congress acts, a rule that treats a change in a data center's customers as a transfer of the chips inside it rests on BIS's reading of its existing authority, which could face legal challenge.
Who Is Affected
The rule moves the compliance checkpoint from the shipping dock toward the account login: the party that grants access now needs to know who owns the customer using it.
What Requirements Already Apply
The headquarters-based license requirement stays in force under a different provision. BIS confirmed in guidance issued May 31, 2026, that a license is required to export certain advanced computing items to entities headquartered in China or another Country Group D:5 destination, or in Macau, or whose ultimate parent is headquartered there, even when the recipient is located elsewhere. That requirement took effect Nov. 17, 2023. In substance, the obligation does not change: the new rule simply moves it to a different section of the EAR. BIS also stated that this control has always reached shipments made on behalf of such a company, not only shipments to it, including where the company is the purchaser or uses more than 50% of a data center's total compute, and it urged parties in violation to submit voluntary self-disclosures.
The May guidance also said that bona fide data center operators otherwise acting consistently with the EAR need not stop the ongoing use or servicing of advanced computing items because of the headquarters requirement, until further notice. Operators that relied on that statement should note that the new rule requires any data center already serving restricted customers above the 12 million aggregate TPP threshold to obtain a license within 90 days of the effective date.
Separately, BIS moved license applications for certain chips, such as the Nvidia H200, to case-by-case review for exports from the United States to end users in China and Macau in a final rule effective Jan. 15, 2026, subject to conditions. Reexports to those destinations, and exports to entities headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau but located elsewhere, remain under a presumption of denial. Applicants must describe the ultimate consignee’s KYC procedures for preventing remote access by restricted parties and list any intended IaaS remote end users headquartered in China, Macau, or six other listed destinations, or whose ultimate parent is headquartered there. These are license conditions, not a general ban on cloud access by Chinese customers.
BIS has also warned that its catch-all controls may require a license when an exporter knows that an IaaS provider will use advanced chips to train AI models for or on behalf of parties headquartered in Country Group D:5 (including China) or Macau, where the models may support weapons of mass destruction (WMD) or military-intelligence end uses. Kharon analyzed that guidance when BIS issued it in May 2025. BIS’s diversion guidance treats an IaaS customer that cannot affirm that its users are not headquartered in China as a red flag, along with a customer that refuses to disclose where it or its ultimate parent is headquartered.
What Exporters and Data Center Operators Should Review Now
Practical preparation starts with the parties behind a sale or computing relationship, and the steps differ depending on which side of the transaction an organization is on.a
- Screen customers to the ultimate parent: Establish each customer's headquarters and ultimate parent, and whether it is directly or indirectly owned or controlled by persons in Country Group D:5 or Macau, including customers that train models for such companies.
- Measure the compute: Total the TPP ratings of the chips that each facility, and any data centers linked to it, makes available to companies headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau.
- Meet the 90-day deadline: If that total already exceeds 12 million aggregate TPP, prepare license applications to file within 90 days of the effective date.
- Prepare KYC records for suppliers: Maintain due diligence records that chip suppliers can review, since a refusal or inability to provide them is a red flag for the supplier.
- Obtain IaaS attestations: Obtain written attestations from IaaS customers that they do not provide access for training AI models on behalf of parties headquartered in Country Group D:5 (including China) or Macau, as BIS's diversion guidance recommends.
Organizations on both sides should record unresolved red flags and reassess relationships when ownership or customer information changes.
Assessing Remote Access and Diversion Risk with Kharon
Under the new rule, the deciding question in a remote access case is who owns a data center's customers. Whether a data center needs a license depends on whether its customers, or the companies they train models for, are headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau or have an ultimate parent headquartered there. An exporter's exposure depends on whether it can verify the data center's answer, including any direct or indirect ownership or control by persons in those destinations.
