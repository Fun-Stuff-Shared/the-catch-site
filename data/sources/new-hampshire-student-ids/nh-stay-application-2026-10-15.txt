To the HONORABLE KETANJI BROWN JACKSON, Associate Justice of the Supreme
Court of the United States and Circuit Justice for the First Circuit:

      Pursuant to Supreme Court Rule 23 and the All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1651

the New Hampshire Secretary of State and New Hampshire Attorney General respectfully request that this Court stay the orders issued by the U.S. District Court

for the District of New Hampshire, App. 15a–67a, granting preliminary injunctive

relief pending further proceedings in the court of appeals and this Court. The applicants further request an immediate administrative stay of the district court’s orders, which preliminarily enjoin the Secretary from enforcing months-old guidance

interpreting recent amendments to New Hampshire’s election laws and order that

the Secretary issue revised guidance, inconsistent with that interpretation, in the

lead up to the November 2026 general election.


                                 INTRODUCTION

      This Court has, time and again, admonished lower federal courts not to intervene in state election procedures in the lead up to an election. See, e.g., People

Not Politicians v. Onder, __ U.S. __, No. 26A274, slip op. 3 (Sept. 25, 2026); Republican Nat’l Comm. v. Democratic Nat’l Comm., 589 U.S. 423, 424 (2020) (collecting

cases); Purcell v. Gonzalez, 549 U.S. 1, 4–5 (2006). The district court initially heeded

that admonishment, relying on the Purcell principle to deny the respondent’s request to preliminarily enjoin enforcement of a recent amendment to state law—

colloquially referred to as HB 323—in advance of New Hampshire’s September 8

state primary election. App. 1a–14a. HB 323 prohibits the use of student IDs as valid proof of identity when voting.

      With respect to the general election, however, the district court reversed

course. At 9:23 p.m. on Friday, October 2, the district court granted the respondent’s request for a preliminary injunction in part with respect to the general election. App. 15a–65a. While the district court did not enjoin enforcement of HB 323

App. 67a. On October 7, the First Circuit denied the applicants’ motion without

Rule of Appellate Procedure 8” for the applicants to seek “a stay pending appeal in

the district court in the first instance.” App. 67a. The applicants promptly did so, D.

On October 8, the applicants filed a renewed emergency motion for a stay pending

      The district court’s order cannot be squared with Purcell. The district court

      The district court’s order requires the Secretary to reverse all these efforts in

the immediate lead up to the general election. It does so based on its own interpretation of state law, while generating more questions than it answers. These are the

precise circumstances in which the considerations animating Purcell are at their

apex. And this is especially true given that the respondent waited more than four

months after HB 323 was enacted to initiate this lawsuit.

      This Court should therefore issue a stay pending further proceedings in the

court of appeals and this Court. Given the uncertainty the district court’s order has

injected into New Hampshire’s election procedures, and the need for clarity and

stability with less than three weeks to go before the election, this Court should also

                                    STATEMENT

      Governor Ayotte signed HB 323 into law on April 3, 2026. App. 3a. HB 323

amended RSA 659:13, removing public and private school student IDs from the list

of valid identifications to prove identification when obtaining a ballot. D. Ct. Doc.

45, at ¶2. After HB 323, only government-issued ID could serve as automatic identity verifications. D. Ct. Doc. 45, at ¶2. This change went into effect on June 2. D. Ct.

Doc. 45, at ¶5. New Hampshire’s primary election was held on September 8. D. Ct.

Doc. 45, at ¶8 Its statewide general election will be held on November 3. D. Ct. Doc.

45, at ¶31

      On August 11, the respondent filed a four-count complaint and motion for a

preliminary injunction. D. Ct. Doc. 1, 2. The complaint alleges that RSA 659:13, as

amended by HB 323, violates the First, Fourteenth, and Twenty-Sixth Amendments

to the United States Constitution, and Part I, Article 37 of the New Hampshire

Constitution. D. Ct. Doc. 1, ¶¶ 232–271. The respondent asserts facial constitutional

challenges, seeking declaratory and injunctive relief, pursuant to 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983

and 1988. D. Ct. Doc. 1, ¶¶2–3. Defendants objected to the motion. D. Ct. Doc. 25.

      As directed by the district court, the parties filed a joint timeline setting forth

all pertinent dates, times, and events, and a joint statement of agreed and disputed

facts in advance of an August 31 evidentiary hearing. D. Ct. Doc. 45. After receiving

that the “Purcell doctrine dictates that the court refrain from interfering in the

      On September 2, the respondent asked the district court to consider its motion with respect to the general election on the existing record. D. Ct. Doc. 44, at 1.

                                    ARGUMENT

      An applicant seeking a stay typically must show “(1) a reasonable probability

that four Justices will consider the issue sufficiently meritorious to grant certiorari;

(2) a fair prospect that a majority of the Court will vote to reverse the judgment below; and (3) a likelihood that irreparable harm will result from the denial of a stay.”

Nat’l Republican Cong. Comm. v. Brown, __ U.S. __, No. 26A274, slip op. 4 (Sept. 4,

2026) (per curiam). “In close cases,” this Court “balance[s] the equities and weigh[s]

the relative harms to the applicant and to the respondent.” Id.

      But when “[f]aced with [an order] enjoin[ing] operation of voter identification

procedures just weeks before an election,” this Court will “weigh, in addition to the



                                           7

---

<!-- page 11 -->
harms attendant upon issuance or nonissuance of an injunction, considerations specific to election cases and its own institutional procedures.” Purcell, 549 U.S. at 4.

Even when “the merits are not entirely clearcut,” these election-specific considerations can “dictate” that this Court stay a lower-court injunction. People Not Politicians v. Onder, __ U.S. __, No. 26A274, slip op. 3 (Sept. 25, 2026).

         Here, as explained below, the district court premised relief on an incorrect

interpretation of New Hampshire law. But even if the merits were “not entirely

clearcut,” the Purcell principle overwhelmingly supports a stay of the district court’s

order.

         A.    A stay is warranted under Purcell.

         The court denied injunctive relief prior to the September primaries because

the Purcell principle required it to do so. App. 1a–14a. It should have reached the

same conclusion with respect to the general election. Instead, it issued relief just 32

days before the election. Its order announces a new interpretation of state law that

state election officials have not been trained to administer. And the district court’s

order raises more questions than it answers. Under Purcell, it should be stayed.

         This Court has warned that “[c]ourt orders affecting elections, especially conflicting orders, can themselves result in voter confusion and consequent incentive to

remain away from the polls. As an election draws closer, that risk will increase.”

Purcell, 549 U.S. at 4-5. This Court has also consistently held that each State “has a

compelling interest in protecting voters from confusion” and “preserving the integrity of its election process.” See, e.g., Burson v. Freeman, 504 U.S. 191, 199 (1992). Ac8

---

<!-- page 12 -->
cordingly, this Court has “repeatedly emphasized that lower federal courts should

ordinarily not alter the election rules on the eve of an election.” Republican Nat’l

Comm., 589 U.S. at 424 (collecting cases). The district court’s order here falls

squarely within that admonition.

      “[E]lections are complex and election calendars are finely calibrated processes, and significant upheaval and voter confusion can result if changes are made late

in the process.” Alpha Phi Alpha Fraternity v. Raffensperger, 587 F. Supp. 3d 1222,

1324 (N.D. Ga. 2022). “[A]t every step, state and local officials must communicate to

voters how, when, and where they may cast their ballots through in-person voting

on election day, absentee voting, or early voting.” Democratic Nat’l Comm. v. Wis.

State Legis., 592 U.S. 1039, 1043 (2020) (Kavanaugh, J., concurring). So, courts

should be circumspect and exercise judicial restraint when an election is near. See

id. at 1044.

      That important principle of judicial restraint not only prevents voter
      confusion but also prevents election administrator confusion—and
      thereby protects the State’s interest in running an orderly, efficient
      election and in giving citizens (including the losing candidates and
      their supporters) confidence in the fairness of the election.

Id. (collecting cases). “When an election is close at hand, the rules of the

road should be clear and settled.” Id. at 1043.

      The Purcell principle operates any time a material change to election procedures happens close to an election—voting need not have already begun.

      Late judicial tinkering with election laws can lead to disruption and to
      unanticipated and unfair consequences for candidates, political parties,
      and voters, among others. It is one thing for a State on its own to toy
      with its election laws close to a State’s elections. But it is quite another



                                           9

---

<!-- page 13 -->
thing for a federal court to swoop in and re-do a State’s election laws in
      the period close to an election.

Merrill v. Milligan, 142 S. Ct. 879, 880–81 (2022) (Kavanaugh, J., concurring). The

principle operates as “a sensible refinement of ordinary stay principles for the election context[.]” Id. at 881. The State has an “extraordinarily strong interest in

avoiding late, judicially imposed changes to its election laws and procedures.” Id.

      Over the last five months, local election officials in New Hampshire have

been trained on the new identity-verification rules. D. Ct. Doc. 63, at 10–11. Among

other things, the Secretary’s office updated its Election Procedure Manual, ballot

clerk manuals, and the statewide voter registration system, to reflect these changes.

D. Ct. Doc. 63 at 10. The district court’s injunction requires the Secretary to update

guidance to local election officials “clarifying that, although student identification

cards are no longer expressly valid to establish voter identity under RSA 659:13,

election officials may consider them, along with other available evidence, when determining voter identity under RSA 654:12(d)(4) and RSA 659:13(c)(1) and (3).” App.

66a. This clarification would be a complete reversal of prior guidance and training.

The new guidance would be inconsistent with the Election Procedure Manual, the

statutorily required polling place posters, webpages, and other manuals such as the

ballot clerk procedure manual. Changing all these materials is impracticable just

weeks from the election, and these are the documents used by election officials for

reference on election day, though they will be out-of-date. Notably, “officials” are not

limited to clerks who understand elections—they include ballot clerks and others

with whom the Secretary does not have direct contact. If the injunction is not



                                          10

---

<!-- page 14 -->
stayed, there is a substantial risk of inconsistent application of identity-verification

procedures.

      This is all the more true given that the Secretary’s office has been communicating with the public about the change regarding student IDs since at least April.

D. Ct. Doc. 63, at 10–11. Among other things, the Secretary’s office published student voting guides, updated polling place posters, updated its website, conducted

social media outreach, produced and ran radio ads, and spoke at public events, to

explain how identity verification will work in both the primary and general elections. D. Ct. Doc. 63, at 11–12. Reversing course between the primary and general

elections, and just one month from the general election, creates an unacceptable

risk that the contradiction disincentivizes voting. See Purcell, 549 U.S. at 4–5.

Moreover, the district court’s disagreement with the Secretary’s interpretation of

state law is nuanced, and the press and public could very easily misunderstand

what the district court actually said. To that end, there is substantial risk that voters could show up at the polls with only their student IDs in-hand. But even under

the district court’s interpretation, that is not the law.

      Re-educating local election officials and the public in a short time is practically impossible and risks voter confusion and inconsistent application of law, if not

voter disenfranchisement. These are the very harms against which Purcell is designed to protect.

      While these considerations, standing alone, are sufficient to grant a stay, the

respondent’s unreasonable delay in filing their lawsuit further compels that relief.




                                           11

---

<!-- page 15 -->
This Court has recognized that, in the election context, an “unnecessary” delay in

seeking “preliminary injunctive relief weigh[s] against” such a request. Benisek v.

Lamone, 585 U.S. 155, 160 (2018). “Call it what you will—laches, the Purcell principle, or common sense—the idea is that courts will not disrupt imminent elections

absent a powerful reason for doing so.” Crookston v. Johnson, 841 F.3d 396, 398 (6th

Cir. 2016) (Sutton, J.).

      The respondent filed this lawsuit and sought preliminary relief 130 days after Governor Ayotte signed HB 323 into law. During that time, the Secretary’s office

was actively implementing HB 323. Among other things, the Election Procedure

Manual, Department of State’s website, social media outreach, radio ads, professionally printed polling place posters, and ballot clerk procedure manuals, were

published and distributed reflecting the Secretary’s interpretation of how HB 323

changed identity verification. The respondents have never justified their delay in

seeking relief. And that delay contributed to the short timeframe between the district court’s order and the general election and exacerbated the order’s disruptive

effect. This further supports the issuance of a stay.

      B.     A stay is also warranted under the traditional factors.

      While a stay should be issued based on Purcell alone, it is also warranted under the traditional stay factors. The district court issued relief based on its interpretation of New Hampshire law. App. 15a–65a. The applicants maintain, as they did

below, that the district court’s interpretation is wrong. See, e.g., D. Ct. Doc. 70, at 3–

7. But even if state law is ambiguous—a point the applicants do not concede—it is




                                           12

---

<!-- page 16 -->
hard to conceive why the district court’s late-breaking interpretation should trump

the interpretation published and disseminated by the Secretary’s office for more

than five months. This is especially true given that state, not federal, courts “are

final arbiters of state law in our federal system[.]” United States v. Taylor, 596 U.S.

845, 859 (2022). If the First Circuit does not reverse the district court’s order, then

there is reason to believe this Court will intervene under these circumstances. See,

e.g., Mckesson v. Doe, 592 U.S. 1, 3–4 (2020) (per curiam) (granting certiorari, vacating the court of appeals’ judgment, and remanding, while recognizing that the constitutional question presented would be “greatly simplified by guidance from the

Louisiana Supreme Court on the meaning of Louisiana law”); Railroad Com. Of Tex.

v. Pullman Co., 312 U.S. 496, 500–02 (1941) (recognizing that abstention can be appropriate when a federal constitutional question turns on an antecedent question of

state law).

      The balance of the harms and the public interest also support a stay. The district court has enjoined the Secretary from effectuating state law in the manner the

Secretary believes is required. “Any time a state is enjoined by a court from effectuating statutes enacted by representatives of its people, it suffers a form of irreparable injury.” Maryland v. King, 567 U.S. 1301, 133 S. Ct. 1, 3 (2012) (Roberts, C.J., in

chambers). Moreover, as previously discussed, the district court’s order threatens to

cause voter confusion and potential disenfranchisement, which are manifestly

against the public interest. See, e.g., Purcell, 549 U.S. at 4–5; Burson, 504 U.S. at

199. In contrast, the respondent’s unreasonable delay in seeking relief undermines




                                          13

---

<!-- page 17 -->
its assertions of irreparable harm. See Benisek, 585 U.S. at 160; see also Fishman v.

Schaffer, 429 U.S. 1325, 1330 (1976) (Marshall, J., in chambers) (denying relief in

part because the plaintiffs “delayed unnecessarily in commencing [their] suit”).1

These factors also weigh in favor of granting a stay.

      C.     This Court should issue an immediate administrative stay
             pending resolution of this application.

      The district court’s order has already created a substantial risk of “voter confusion and consequent incentive to remain away from the polls” that the Purcell

principle seeks to avoid. 549 U.S. at 4–5. “As the election draws closer, that risk

will increase.” Id. at 5. The general election is now less than three weeks away. To

mitigate the ongoing risk of harm caused by the district court’s order, this Court

should issue an immediate administrative stay of that order pending resolution of

this application.




1
  That the Secretary made immediate, good-faith attempts to comply with the district court’s order does not change this analysis. The ability to blast-email revised
instructions does not establish that those instructions have displaced prior training
and materials, reached all polling personnel, or been consistently understood by officials and voters. As discussed, the Secretary’s implementation of HB 323 has
spanned months and has including trainings, publications, and press and public engagement. The risk of voter confusion and disenfranchisement caused by the district
court’s order cannot be addressed through a single communication. At this stage,
only a stay of the district court’s order promises to bring clarity and certainty to the
