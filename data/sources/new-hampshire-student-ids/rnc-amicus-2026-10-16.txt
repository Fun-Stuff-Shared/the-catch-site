INTEREST OF AMICUS CURIAE1
      The Republican National Committee is a national committee under 52 U.S.C.

§ 30101. It manages the Republican Party’s business, coordinates election strategy,

and supports Republican candidates nationwide. The RNC coordinates fundraising

and election strategy and develops and promotes the national Republican platform.

The RNC’s membership consists of the party chair, national committeeman, and

committeewoman for each State and territory, including multiple representatives

from New Hampshire who are registered New Hampshire voters. The RNC has

interests in the rules and procedures governing New Hampshire’s elections. These

interests are heightened given the imminent elections for federal and state office.

      The RNC has extensive expertise in election law, election administration, and

voting rights. And its members, candidates, and voters are among those most affected

by lawsuits challenging election rules in New Hampshire and nationwide.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

      Election day is imminent. But less than a month before the election, the district

court issued an injunction that threatens to disrupt identity verification in New

Hampshire’s looming elections. That injunction, so close to an election, violates this

Court’s guidance from Purcell. The Court should stay the injunction.

      I. The district court partially enjoined the State’s voter ID law less than 30

days before election day in violation of the Purcell principle. Purcell applies to voter

identification laws. And courts at all levels, including this Court, have concluded

across election cases that it is now too close to the election to issue injunctions which

could disrupt elections and confuse voters and officials. Worse still, the New

Hampshire district court previously declined to enter injunctive relief ahead of the

primary, so the injunction now will cause confusion by subjecting voters and voting

officials to different sets of rules between the primary and the general election.

      II. The Coalition for Open Democracy unreasonably delayed in its request to

enjoin the State. It waited 130 days after the law was enacted to even file a complaint,

and only then requested urgent preliminary relief. A party seeking preliminary relief

must show reasonable diligence even in the ordinary course. In election cases that

implicate Purcell, undue delay is fatal. Here, where the Coalition provided no excuse

for its delay, the district court issued an injunction to remedy an emergency of the

Coalition’s own making.

                                     ARGUMENT

      The First Circuit recognizes that this Court “has offered a special caution about

the perils of federal courts changing the rules on the eve of an election.” Common

Cause R.I. v. Gorbea, 970 F.3d 11, 16 (1st Cir. 2020) (citing Purcell v. Gonzalez, 549

U.S. 1, 4-5 (2006)). But the district court and the First Circuit did not heed Purcell’s

“special caution.” Id. “[W]hen a lower court intervenes and alters the election rules

so close to the election date,” a reviewing court “should correct that error.” Republican

Nat’l Comm. v. Democratic Nat’l Comm., 589 U.S. 423, 425 (2020). The “election is

close at hand,” so “the rules of the road must be clear and settled.” Merrill v. Milligan,

142 S. Ct. 879, 880-81 (2022) (Kavanaugh, J., concurring in grant of stay

applications).

 I.   Purcell precludes enjoining state voter-ID rules so close to an
      election.

      The Purcell principle is a “bedrock tenet of election law.” Id. It instructs that

“lower federal courts should ordinarily not alter the election rules on the eve of an

election.” RNC, 589 U.S. at 424. To “overcome” Purcell, plaintiffs must show “at least”

that “the underlying merits are entirely clearcut” in their favor, they “would suffer

irreparable harm absent the injunction,” they have “not unduly delayed,” and “the

changes in question are at least feasible before the election without significant cost,

confusion, or hardship.” Milligan, 142 S. Ct. at 881 (Kavanaugh, J., concurring). The

Coalition has not met those stringent requirements.

      Purcell applies to voter-verification laws of the sort the Coalition challenges

here. Count US IN v. Morales, 172 F.4th 974, 976 (7th Cir. 2026) (per curiam); ColonMarrero v. Conty-Perez, 703 F.3d 134, 139 (1st Cir. 2012). That’s because “[c]ourt

orders affecting elections … can themselves result in voter confusion and consequent

incentive to remain away from the polls.” Purcell, 549 U.S. at 4-5. Indeed, those

principles apply with special force to “an injunction suspending the voter

identification rules,” because “[v]oters who fear their legitimate votes will be

outweighed by fraudulent ones will feel disenfranchised.” Id. at 4, 6. So “[t]he

concerns that underlie Purcell—the risk of voter confusion and the unfairness of

unexpected administrative burdens” apply at least as much here as they do to “other

types of belated voting changes.” Tenn. Conf. of the NAACP v. Lee, 105 F.4th 888, 897

(6th Cir. 2024) (per curiam).

      Election season is underway. This Court applied the Purcell principle just

three weeks ago, reminding courts yet again that they “‘should not alter the election

rules of a State on the eve of an election.’” People Not Politicians v. Onder, 609 U.S.

___, slip op. at 3 (2026) (per curiam) (quoting Allen v. Milligan, 608 U.S. 511, 514

(2026) (per curiam)). And this summer, this Court reversed a district court for

violating Purcell by enjoining “Alabama’s ongoing efforts to conduct its imminent

2026 congressional elections under maps that its elected representatives selected.”

Allen, 608 U.S. at 514. In fact, the first instance of this Court applying Purcell in this

election cycle goes as far back as December 2025 when a district court “improperly

inserted itself into an active primary campaign.” Abbott v. League of United Latin

Am. Citizens, 146 S. Ct. 418, 419 (2025). When “a State’s election machinery is

already in progress, equitable considerations might justify a court in withholding the

granting of immediately effective relief,” even if that relief would ordinarily be

justified. Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533, 585 (1964). Already, “candidates, election

officials, and voters have relied on the rules in place.” Malliotakis v. Williams, 146 S.

Ct. 809, 811 (2026) (Alito, J., concurring in grant of stay) (mem.). “By changing the

election rules so close to the election date, … the District Court contravened this

Court’s precedents and erred by ordering such relief.” RNC, 589 U.S. at 424.

      For many months now, courts across the country have concluded that Purcell

“counsels strongly against the issuance of an injunction” this late in the election cycle.

E.g., Foster v. Mississippi, 2026 WL 2352910, at *7-15 (N.D. Miss. Aug. 13, 2026);

Carpenter v. Landry, 2026 WL 2473631, at *5 (M.D. La. Aug. 4, 2026); Sherman v.

Hargett, 2026 WL 1471870, at *7 (M.D. Tenn. May 26, 2026); Ams. for Citizen Voting

Pac v. Wolfe, 2026 WL 1329715, at *3 (E.D. Wis. May 12, 2026); Shafii v. City of

Charleston, 2026 WL 1244855, at *5 (S.D. W. Va. May 6, 2026); Colo. Republican

Party v. Griswold, 2026 WL 1145559, at *3 (D. Colo. Apr. 28, 2026); Gardner v.

Henderson, 2026 WL 496448, at *7 (D. Utah Feb. 23, 2026) (three-judge court); Coll.

Democrats of N.C. v. N.C. State Bd. of Elections, 2026 WL 327927, at *3 (M.D.N.C.

Feb. 8, 2026). In the few recent cases where district courts granted relief, circuit

courts quickly stayed those orders because they were “a clear violation of Purcell.”

E.g., Count US IN, 172 F.4th at 976 (staying “preliminary injunction prohibiting the

application of a statutory provision impacting voter eligibility”); Red Wine & Blue v.

LaRose, 2026 WL 2922945, at *6-7 (6th Cir. Sep. 23, 2026) (injunction issued “about

a month before a looming registration deadline” “appears to run afoul of Purcell.”

(citation modified)). The district court’s order here splits with the many courts

faithfully applying Purcell this election cycle.

Indeed, the district court split with its own prior order. App. 1a–14a; Coal. for

Open Democracy v. Scanlan, 2026 WL 2583044, at *6 (D.N.H. Sept. 1). In September,

the district court held that “the operational window to redistribute guidance, retain

poll workers, and re-educate voters before more ballots are cast is close to closing.

This is the type of compressed timeline that Purcell was designed to guard against.”

App. 13a. The general election is now less than a month away. Purcell itself stayed

“an injunction issued about a month before an election.” Tenn. Conf. of the NAACP,

105 F.4th at 897. And in the last federal election cycle, this Court partially stayed a

much earlier “district court’s May 2, 2024 judgment” affecting voter-registration

rules. RNC v. Mi Familia Vota, 145 S. Ct. 108 (2024) (mem.).

      The Purcell principle “not only prevents voter confusion but also prevents

election administrator confusion—and thereby protects the [local] interest in running

an orderly, efficient election and in giving citizens (including the losing candidates

and their supporters) confidence in the fairness of the election.” Democratic Nat’l

Comm. v. Wis. State Legislature, 592 U.S. 1039, 1044 (2020) (Kavanaugh, J.,

concurring). The risk of confusion is heightened here because the primary election

operated under the very rules that the district court has now enjoined. New

Hampshire held its primary elections on September 8, 2026. Forcing the State and

voters to follow new rules for the general election than applied in the primary election

is exactly the sort of “conflicting guidance” that “could create an ‘incentive’ for voters

to ‘remain away from the polls.’” Tenn. Conf. of the NAACP, 105 F.4th at 900 (quoting

Purcell, 549 U.S. at 5).

Election season was already imminent when the Coalition filed its complaint.

New Hampshire has been “in the thick of election season” for months now. Democratic

Nat’l Comm. v. Wis. State Legislature, 592 U.S. at 1039 (Roberts, C.J., concurring in

denial of application to vacate stay). The district court remade voter verification

policy without enough time remaining to implement the ordered change. That order

violates Purcell. “Given the imminence of the election and the inadequate time to

resolve the factual disputes,” this Court should “of necessity allow the election to

proceed without an injunction suspending the voter identification rules.” Purcell, 549

U.S. at 5.

II.   The Coalition unduly delayed.

      Even if the injunction wouldn’t impose “significant cost, confusion, or

hardship” on voters and election officials, the Coalition’s months-long delay in

seeking relief is inexcusable. Milligan, 142 S. Ct. at 881 (Kavanaugh, J., concurring).

Although similar to the doctrine of laches, see App. 61a-63a, undue delay has special

consequences in the election context—to “overcome” the “Purcell principle,” one of the

showings the Coalition must make is that it “has not unduly delayed bringing the

complaint to court.” Id. Even outside the election context, “a party requesting a

preliminary injunction must generally show reasonable diligence.” Benisek v.

Lamone, 585 U.S. 155, 159 (2018) (per curiam). The “balance of equities … tilt[s]

against” a party who cannot show reasonable diligence. Id. at 158. “The longer the

delay, the more pervasive the doubt” that urgent, preliminary relief is warranted.

Charlesbank Equity Fund II v. Blinds to Go, 370 F.3d 151, 163 (1st Cir. 2004).

<!-- page 12 -->
That the Coalition never provided a “‘reasonable explanation’” for its delay

should have been reason enough to deny their motion. Tenn. Conf. of the NAACP, 105

F.4th at 898 (quoting Crookston v. Johnson, 841 F.3d 396, 398 (6th Cir. 2016)). Again,

the district court’s own prior order explains why that principle should have precluded

relief here. “The Governor signed HB 323 into law on April 3, 2026, and it took effect

on June 2, 2026. The plaintiff waited 130 days after enactment and 70 days after the

law took effect before filing suit and simultaneously demanding emergency relief.”

App. 12a-13a. During that delay, the Secretary of State “updated the Election

Procedure Manual, ballot clerk manuals, student voting guides, polling place posters,

its website, and the statewide voter registration system.” App. 25a.

      Months passed, and election season began, in the time that the Coalition

waited. That delay is not “reasonable diligence.” Benisek v. Lamone, 585 U.S. at 159.

The First Circuit has previously declined to afford relief to a litigant who, “well aware

of the requirements of the election laws, chose not to bring this suit until August 5,

… shortly before the November 2 elections … and two months after the primary

election.” Respect Me. PAC v. McKee, 622 F.3d 13, 16 (1st Cir. 2010). Yet after the

primary elections, the district court did an about-face on its conclusion of

unreasonable delay, even though the conclusion of the primaries was a factor against

injunctive relieve in McKee. See 622 F.3d at 16. The harms and confusion that Purcell

guards against do not reset after a primary—they are heightened because voters and

officials should be able to reasonably assume that the same rules apply in a primary

and a general election. See Purcell, 549 U.S. at 4–5.

The Coalition’s delay undercuts the “District Court’s late-breaking injunction.”

Milligan, 142 S. Ct. at 881 (Kavanaugh, J., concurring). The principle that “courts

will generally decline to grant an injunction to alter a State’s established election

procedures” is “especially true when a plaintiff has unreasonably delayed bringing

his claim.” Crookston, 841 F.3d at 398.

      The Coalition’s delay is inexcusable. It waited months, then sought an

emergency preliminary injunction rather than litigate the case in a timely fashion

from the beginning. The “‘emergency’ is largely one of their own making.” McKee, 622

F.3d at 16; App. 12a-13a. The district court apportioned blame between the parties

for a poorly developed record and insufficient briefing. App. 61a-62a. But it did not

and could not lay any portion of the blame for the delayed filing and request for relief

at the Sate’s feet. The Coalition’s “cries of urgency are sharply undercut by” their

delay. Charlesbank Equity Fund II, 370 F.3d at 163. And any reason to leave the

injunction in place during this appeal cannot survive their neglect. Failing to stay

this injunction would encourage future litigants to wait until the last few days before

an election to seek relief, so that a district court’s preliminary injunction could

provide all the relief they seek through the general election. Courts should not bless

such gamesmanship.

                                   CONCLUSION
      The Court should stay the district court’s injunction pending proceedings in

the First Circuit and in this Court.
