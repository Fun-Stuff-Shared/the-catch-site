ARGUMENT

      The respondent spends the first two-thirds of its response attempting to

downplay and justify the district court’s late-breaking rewrite of New Hampshire’s

election procedures. In doing so, the respondent acknowledges that the district court

substituted its own view of how HB 323 operates for that of the New Hampshire

Secretary of State. The respondent does not dispute (nor could it) that the district

court issued its order barely a month before the general election and after the Secretary had spent months training election officials and informing the public consistent with his interpretation of New Hampshire law. But the respondent says that

this is all fine, either because it thinks the district court got New Hampshire law

right or because, in its view, this Court is unlikely to grant plenary review. As to

Purcell, the respondent suggests that the Secretary’s good-faith efforts to diminish

the disruption caused by the district’s order mean that no stay should issue, while

also arguing that the applicants somehow have unclean hands because it took them

three-and-a-half days (including a weekend) to seek a stay from the First Circuit.

      The respondent’s arguments are not persuasive. Just last month, this Court

reiterated that the election-specific considerations animating Purcell can “dictate”

staying injunctive relief issued by a lower federal court even when “the merits are

not entirely clearcut.” People Not Politicians v. Onder, __ U.S. __, No. 26A274, slip

op. 3 (Sept. 25, 2026). The applicants maintain, as they did below, that the merits

favor their position. But even if the district court based its order on a plausible construction of state law, Purcell dictates that it should have stayed its hand. The re1

A.     The respondent offers no convincing reason to ignore Purcell.

       The district court issued its order late in the evening on October 2, just 32

calendar days before the general election. App. 15a–65a. 1 Notably, this is one day

closer to the election than the order in Purcell. See 549 U.S. 1, 3 (2006) (noting that

the court of appeals issued its injunction on October 5 and that the general election

was on November 7). There is no dispute that by that time, the Secretary had spent

months training election officials and informing the public on how HB 323 would

operate based on the Secretary’s interpretation of state law. See, e.g., D. Ct. Doc. 63,

at 10–12. The district court nonetheless enjoined the Secretary from enforcing that

guidance and directed the Secretary “to issue, as soon as practicable, guidance for

the November 2026 general election” that comported with the district court’s own

view of state law. App. 66a. As explained in the underlying application, Purcell

squarely forecloses this relief.

       The respondent contends that Purcell should not apply because the district

court’s injunction “didn’t alter New Hampshire’s election rules.” Resp. at 24–26. According to the respondent, the Secretary’s guidance is only a “gloss” on the rules, not

a component of the rules themselves. Id. While the respondent contends that Purcell poses no impediment to a federal court enjoining a “gloss,” id., this Court does

not appear to have ever limited Purcell in this way. But even if it had, the respond1 This is the date the district court issued its narrative order. The district court issued a

separate order, containing the terms of the injunction, on October 5, just 29 days before the
general election. App. 66a.

ent’s argument would still fail because the Secretary’s guidance is plainly part of

New Hampshire’s election rules.

         The Secretary is New Hampshire’s “chief election officer.” N.H. Rev. Stat.

Ann. § 652:23, I. In that capacity, the Secretary is required to “provide information

regarding voter registration and absentee ballot procedures for all voters,” which

can include “[i]nstructional and informational materials published by” the Secretary. Id. The Secretary is also required, “with the advice and approval of the attorney general,” to “prepare or cause to be prepared preceding each state general election an up-to-date manual on the New Hampshire election laws and procedures for

conducting elections.” N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 652:22. Likewise, the Secretary is required to “prepare an explanatory document explaining the proof of identity requirements of RSA 659:13, including all permissible methods for proving identity

and the dates of applicability of these requirements.” N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 652:25.

         The Secretary’s guidance was both authorized by and required under these

provisions. The guidance itself contains the Secretary’s explanations of “voter registration . . . procedures,” N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 652:23, I, and “all permissible methods for proving identity and the dates of applicability of these requirements,” N.H.

Rev. Stat. Ann. § 652:25. In other words, it constitutes the formal interpretation of

New Hampshire election law by a state official authorized and required to provide

that interpretation. It is therefore neither incidental to nor severable from New

Hampshire’s “election rules,” much less some sort of voluntary “gloss” on those

rules.

The respondent is, of course, free to challenge the Secretary’s interpretation.

But the respondent cannot evade Purcell by pretending that challenge is something

other than what it is. The respondent’s attempt to distinguish Purcell therefore

fails. As set forth in the underlying application, Purcell applies.

      The respondent argues that this Court should nonetheless ignore Purcell because the Secretary has sought to comply with the district court’s order while simultaneously seeking a stay. Resp. at 28–31. This argument is based on a false premise. The applicants have not argued, as the respondent suggests, that it is physically

impossible to comply with the district court’s order before the general election. They

have instead argued that the very existence of the district court’s order, whether

complied with or not, creates a substantial risk of voter confusion and inconsistent

application of state law. Appl. at 9–11. This is because the Secretary’s office has

spent the past five months training election officials, issuing guidance, and educating the public about HB 323, consistent with the Secretary’s interpretation of state

law. Id. A single updated guidance document cannot unwind those efforts.

      The record below reflects this. To be sure, the Deputy Secretary of State testified that the Secretary’s office can reach most clerks, supervisors, and moderators

by email. D. Ct. Doc. 47, at 187:3–10. But he also explained that the Secretary’s office does not have contact with ballot clerks and other poll workers. D. Ct. Doc. 61,

at 157:10–15. He further explained that a single blast email could not ensure that

each town or city ward retrieved and destroyed all prior versions of the Secretary’s

guidance. D. Ct. Doc. 61, at 162:1–5. And he explained the complexities involved in

implementing even a seemingly simple message that contradicts earlier guidance

      The respondent is therefore wrong to assert that “the status quo is now the

injunction.” Resp. at 29. The status quo remains, as it has since April, the language

of HB 323 itself and the Secretary’s guidance as to how that language should be implemented. The district court’s order disrupts, rather preserves, that status quo.

Even worse, it injects uncertainty where there had previously been stability.

      The respondent’s argument, if adopted, would also present state election officials with a Hobson’s choice. Under the respondent’s conception of Purcell, an election official would have to defy a late-breaking district court order if he wants to

seek a stay. But this would place the election official in jeopardy of contempt, particularly if a stay did not issue. The respondent cites no authority for this untenable

scenario, and this Court should not indulge it.

      This Court should also not indulge the respondent’s attempt to justify its own

significant delay in bringing this lawsuit by equating it to the three-and-a-half days

between the district court’s order and the applicants’ request for a stay. It is hard to

see how these timeframes are comparable on their face. In any event, the respondent cites no authority for the proposition that this Court should turn a blind eye to

how the respondent’s own actions materially contributed to the timing of the district

court’s order and its disruptive effect. This Court’s precedents make clear that these

considerations are relevant. See, e.g., Benesek v. Lamone, 585 U.S. 155, 160 (2018).

Nor does the respondent cite any authority for the notion that under the present

B.    The respondent’s other arguments are not persuasive.

      As mentioned, the respondent devotes the bulk of its response to merits arguments. In doing so, the respondent all but ignores that this case involves considerations that are not present in a typical application for a stay. Purcell makes clear

that when “[f]aced with [an order] enjoin[ing] operation of voter identification procedures just weeks before an election,” this Court will “weigh, in addition to the

harms attendant upon issuance or nonissuance of an injunction, considerations specific to election cases and its own institutional procedures.” 549 U.S. at 4. Only

weeks ago, this Court observed that these election-specific considerations can “dictate” the issuance of a stay when “the merits are not entirely clearcut.” People Not

Politicians, __ U.S. __, No. 26A274, slip op. 3.

      The respondent’s own filing confirms that the merits do not clearly favor its

position. As noted, the respondent acknowledges that the district court premised relief on its own interpretation of state law. Resp. at 17–23. The respondent further

acknowledges that the applicants have offered a contrary interpretation, which

       The respondent suggests that the applicants have somehow changed their

      The respondent’s merits arguments also discount the federalism implications

of the district court’s order. As the respondent notes, the district court appeared to

agree that the Eleventh Amendment barred any independent state-law challenge to

the Secretary’s guidance. Resp. at 16. The district court nonetheless concluded that

the Secretary’s guidance likely violated federal law under the Anderson-Burdick

framework. App. 47a. It reached this conclusion based on how it resolved an antecedent question of state law. As explained in the underlying application, this analysis presents federalism questions that are not clearcut and may eventually warrant

this Court’s intervention. Appl. at 12–13.

      The respondent’s suggestion that the applicants did nothing to justify the

Secretary’s guidance under Anderson-Burdick is also incorrect. This necessarily assumes that the applicants’ construction of state law is wrong. If that construction is

right, then the Secretary’s guidance and HB 323 are coextensive. The same justifications would therefore apply to each. The suggestion that the district court’s “constitutional holding doesn’t turn on whose interpretation is right,” Resp. at 18, ignores this reality.

      The respondent’s reliance on their Twenty Sixth Amendment claim, Resp. at

23–24, is also misplaced. The district court noted that the First Circuit “has not articulated a precise standard for Twenty-Sixth Amendment claims.” App. 50a. Nor

has this Court. The district court further found that neither HB 323 nor the Secre8

---

<!-- page 12 -->
tary’s guidance was intentionally discriminatory. App. 53a–55a. The district court

therefore applied its Anderson-Burdick analysis to the respondent’s Twenty Sixth

Amendment claim. App. 49a–57a. Without any controlling authority on point, it is

certainly not clearcut that this was the correct analysis. And even if it was, it is not

clearcut that the district court’s conclusions were correct for the reasons already

stated. The respondent’s contention that their Twenty Sixth Amendment claim “independently supports the injunction,” Resp. at 24, is therefore not persuasive.

      Finally, there is no merit to the respondent’s contention that a stay should

not issue because “[t]his is an interlocutory appeal from a preliminary injunction.”

Resp. at 15. Many of this Court’s recent decisions on emergency applications involved requests to stay preliminary injunctive relief. See, e.g., Trump v. Cook, 146 S.

Ct. 2234, 2245–46 (2026); Trump v. CASA, Inc., 606 U.S. 831, 837–38 (2025). Purcell did too. 549 U.S. at 3. The fact that this application arises out of a preliminary

posture is hardly a reason to deny a stay.
