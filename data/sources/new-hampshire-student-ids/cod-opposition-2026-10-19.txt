To the Honorable Ketanji Brown Jackson,
Circuit Justice for the First Circuit:

      The Coalition for Open Democracy respectfully opposes the State’s emergency

application for a stay of the preliminary injunction that the United States District

Court for the District of New Hampshire granted on October 2, 2026, and entered as

a separate order on October 5, 2026. (App. 15a–66a.) The district court and the court

of appeals have each declined to stay that injunction. (App. 69a–74a.)

                                    Introduction

      This application asks the Court to stay a narrow injunction against a Secretary

of State directive. No statute is enjoined. The district court declined to enjoin HB 323,

the 2026 New Hampshire law that removed student IDs from the list of photo

identification that a ballot clerk must accept. (App. 45a–47a.) It enjoined only the

April Directive, a Secretary of State press release and email announcing that student

IDs could no longer be used “for any purpose at all,” including under the two statutory

safety valves authorizing election officials to verify a voter’s identity by “any other

evidence that reasonably establishes” who the voter is. (App. 15a, 64a.) After three

days of evidentiary hearings, the court found that the State had offered no interest

specific to the Directive, that the Directive imposes a burden that is “minimal, but

not de minimis,” with direct evidence that it had already disenfranchised at least one

voter, and that the Directive likely violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments

and, independently, the Twenty-Sixth Amendment. (App. 44a, 47a, 56a–57a.) The

Secretary issued revised guidance within days. (Appl. 14 n.1.)

                                      Statement

A.    New Hampshire’s Identity Rules and Their Safety Valves

      New Hampshire law separates registering to vote from obtaining a ballot. Both

require proof of identity. The registration statute lists three kinds of government

photo ID to establish identity, and then a fourth option: “[a]ny other evidence that

reasonably establishes that it is more likely than not that the person is who they

claim to be.” N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 654:12, I(d)(4). (App. 19a.) To obtain a ballot, a

voter presents a “valid photo identification” to a ballot clerk. N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. §

659:13, I(b), II(a). A voter lacking “a valid photo identification” is sent to the

supervisor of the checklist, who “shall review the voter’s qualifications and determine

if the voter’s identity can be verified.” N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 659:13, I(c). (App. 26a–

27a.) The State acknowledged in the district court that the two statutes “do not

materially differ,” that the Secretary’s office “has consistently interpreted” them “in

harmony,” and that the registration statute’s “any other evidence” standard “is the

same process set forth in RSA 659:13.” (App. 21a n.14, 28a.) Supervisors apply that

standard case by case. The Secretary’s Election Procedure Manual tells them that

“[a]ny other evidence that reasonably establishes that it is more likely than not that

the person is who they claim to be” may be “used to prove identity.” (App. 71a.) The

State’s own example is a marriage certificate offered to explain a name that doesn’t

match. (Supp. App. 12a (Dkt. No. 60 ¶ 6).)

      Before 2026, students used their student IDs under both statutes. A student

ID was on the list of expressly valid photo identification for obtaining a ballot, and

the parties stipulated that student IDs “have historically been accepted for the

purposes of voter registration” under the registration statute’s safety valve. (App.

21a–22a.)

B.    HB 323, the April Directive, SB 223, and HB 317

      The Governor signed HB 323 on April 3, 2026, and it took effect June 2. 2026

N.H. Laws ch. 20. It removed student IDs from the list of expressly valid photo

identification in New Hampshire. N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 659:13, II(a). It did that “not

by affirmatively prohibiting acceptance of student IDs at polling stations, but by

removing student ID cards as a form of expressly ‘valid identification’ for obtaining a

ballot.” (App. 23a.) HB 323 “did not change the separate registration statute, RSA

654:12,” and it left the supervisor’s safety valve in N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 659:13, I(c)

in place. (App. 23a, 27a–28a.)

      On April 23, the Secretary issued a press release and an email to local election

officials. The press release, titled “Secretary of State Shares Election Resources for

New Hampshire Students,” announced that “student IDs cannot be used as proof of

ID when registering to vote, requesting an absentee ballot, or checking in to vote in

an election.” (App. 25a.) The email told local election officials that “student IDs can

no longer be used to register to vote or obtain a ballot on election day.” (Supp. App.

2a (Dkt. No. 46 ¶ 3).) The district court called these the “April Directive,” finding that

the Directive barred officials from considering student IDs “for any purpose at all,”

including under the safety valves. (App. 15a–16a.) The Secretary’s Election Procedure

Manual carried the same instruction. After telling supervisors that “any other

evidence” may prove identity, it added a single exception: “a student identification

card cannot be used to prove identity.” (App. 26a, 71a.) The Directive cited HB 323 as

its authority. (App. 25a.)

July 2, added the word “only” to the introductory clause of N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. §

659:13, II(a), which lists the expressly valid forms of identification when obtaining a

ballot. 2026 N.H. Laws ch. 243. HB 317, signed July 10, amended both the

registration and ballot statutes. 2026 N.H. Laws ch. 284. It moved the clause allowing

identity to be verified “by the moderator or another election official” from the

registration statute’s safety valve to the ballot statute’s safety valve, and it otherwise

left N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 654:12, I(d)(4)’s “any other evidence” standard

“unchanged.” (App. 20a, 23a n.24.) As the district court found, the legislature

“specifically amended the statute’s catch-all provision” in HB 317 “but did not address

student IDs at all.” (App. 27a.) At the same time, the State’s Division of Motor

Vehicles accepts a “current student photo/digital image ID card” as proof of identity

C.      The Litigation and the District Court’s Findings

        The Coalition, a nonpartisan voting-rights organization, sued on August 11,

2026, moving for a preliminary injunction against both HB 323 and the Directive.

(App. 16a.) The district court held an evidentiary hearing on August 31 and denied

relief for the September 8 primary under Purcell, because absentee voting for the

primary was “already in progress.” (App. 1a–14a, 59a.) It then ordered the parties to

supplement the record, held two more days of hearings, and directed the State to

“clarify its position” on what a supervisor of the checklist does when a voter arrives

without a listed ID. (App. 18a, 70a.) The State answered that supervisors apply the

registration statute’s “any other evidence” standard. (App. 70a.)

        On October 2, the court issued a fifty-page opinion, which it amended on

October 6 to expand two footnotes. The appendix reproduces the amended opinion.

(App. 15a–65a.) It denied relief as to HB 323, finding that the interests articulated

by the legislature for the statute, stale cards and the lack of REAL ID security

features, were the kind of “generalized, forward-looking concerns” that a State may

invoke under Crawford v. Marion County Election Board, 553 U.S. 181 (2008),

justifying removal of student IDs from the list a ballot clerk must accept. (App. 45a–

47a.)

        The Directive was different. The court found that “the State has articulated no

interest specific to that rule at all.” (App. 47a.) “Neither the State’s briefing nor its

oral argument defends the April Directive on its own terms.” (App. 47a.) The State

“offered no explanation—legislative, administrative, or otherwise—for why the

interests that justify tightening ballot-issuance documentation under RSA 659:13

should justify excluding student identification from RSA 654:12’s safety valve

provision that apparently extends to other forms of non-governmental identification,”

and it “has not identified any security or integrity concern specific to student ID cards

E.    The Stay Proceedings

      The State appealed on October 5 and, without asking the district court for a

stay, moved for one in the First Circuit on October 6. (App. 69a.) The court of appeals

denied that motion without prejudice so that the State could seek a stay “in the

district court in the first instance.” (App. 67a–68a.) The district court denied the

State’s motion the same evening. (App. 69a–72a.) It explained that the State “was on

express notice” of when the ruling would issue, that the court had offered at argument

to grant a stay if it enjoined HB 323, which it did not, and that the State “took four

days to seek a stay from the Court of Appeals.” (App. 69a.) On the merits, it explained

that the injunction “is based on the State’s own articulation of state law throughout

this litigation” and holds “the State to the interpretation it supplied.” (App. 70a.) The

State’s new argument that the word “only” in N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 659:13, II(a)

forecloses the safety valve was “raised for the first time in the motion to stay” and “is

inconsistent with the interpretation actually advanced by the State” when the court

“probed this specific question.” (App. 70a–71a n.15.) And because the Manual already

told supervisors that “any other evidence” may prove identity, the injunction amounts

to “ordering the Secretary of State to remove the single line” that excepted student

                      Reasons for Denying the Application

      A stay pending appeal “is not a matter of right,” and “[t]he party requesting a

stay bears the burden of showing that the circumstances justify an exercise of that

discretion.” Nken, 556 U.S. at 433–34. When the request comes to this Court, the

applicant must show “(1) a reasonable probability that four Justices will consider the

issue sufficiently meritorious to grant certiorari; (2) a fair prospect that a majority of

the Court will vote to reverse the judgment below; and (3) a likelihood that

irreparable harm will result from the denial of a stay.” Nat’l Republican Cong. Comm.

v. Brown, No. 26A274, slip op. 2 (U.S. Sept. 4, 2026) (per curiam) (quoting

Hollingsworth v. Perry, 558 U.S. 183, 190 (2010) (per curiam)). The same test governs

a State’s request to stay a district court’s injunction while its appeal is pending in the

court of appeals. Little v. Reclaim Idaho, 591 U.S. 1060, 1060–61 (2020) (Roberts,

                                           11

---

<!-- page 20 -->
C.J., concurring in grant of stay). And in that posture the applicant “bears an

‘especially heavy burden.’” Id. at 1062 (quoting Packwood v. Senate Select Comm. on

Ethics, 510 U.S. 1319, 1320 (1994) (Rehnquist, C.J., in chambers)).

I.   The State hasn’t shown a reasonable probability that this Court would
     grant certiorari.

A.    An applicant must show that this Court would likely grant certiorari
      and reverse.

      The first element is a threshold, not a formality. It asks whether the case is

one this Court would take on the merits docket. “Were the standard otherwise,

applicants could use the emergency docket to force the Court to give a merits preview

in cases that it would be unlikely to take—and to do so on a short fuse without benefit

of full briefing and oral argument.” Does 1-3 v. Mills, 142 S. Ct. 17, 18 (2021) (Barrett,

J., concurring in denial of application for injunctive relief). “If the underlying merits

issue would not warrant this Court’s review when the case returned to the Court on

the merits docket, then we should deny the application and leave the question of

interim relief to the court of appeals.” Labrador v. Poe, 144 S. Ct. 921, 931 (2024)

(Kavanaugh, J., concurring in grant of stay). Assessing certworthiness “is standard

practice for this Court” on an emergency application. Id. at 936 n.2 (Jackson, J.,

dissenting from grant of stay).

      Purcell doesn’t lower that threshold. The State reads People Not Politicians to

mean that election-specific considerations can “dictate” a stay “even when the merits

are not entirely clearcut.” (Appl. 8.) But that case involved a federal court ordering a

State to hold its congressional elections under a map that the State’s own supreme

                                           12

---

<!-- page 21 -->
court had rejected. Slip op. 1. And the State’s own authority describes Purcell as “a

sensible refinement of ordinary stay principles for the election context,” not a

replacement for them. Merrill v. Milligan, 142 S. Ct. 879, 881 (2022) (Kavanaugh, J.,

concurring). (Appl. 9.) An applicant still has to show that the case is one this Court

would hear.

      The State’s application recites the test and then never applies it. (Appl. 7.) It

doesn’t say what question it would ask this Court to decide, why that question is one

the Court would grant certiorari to answer, or why a majority would reverse. It offers

one sentence: “If the First Circuit does not reverse the district court’s order, then

there is reason to believe this Court will intervene under these circumstances.” (Appl.

13.) The two authorities it cites for that sentence, McKesson v. Doe, 592 U.S. 1 (2020)

(per curiam), and Railroad Commission of Texas v. Pullman Co., 312 U.S. 496 (1941),

are not about certiorari at all. They are about sending questions of state law to state

courts. That’s the State’s theory of certworthiness, and it defeats itself.

B.    The only error the State asserts is a federal court’s reading of a New
      Hampshire statute.

      The application identifies one error on the merits: “the district court premised

relief on an incorrect interpretation of New Hampshire law.” (Appl. 8; see id. at 12–

district court’s interpretation is wrong,” and cites a district-court filing. (Appl. 12

(citing D. Ct. Doc. 70, at 3–7).) It doesn’t contend that the district court misstated the

standard of Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), and Burdick v. Takushi, 504

U.S. 428 (1992), misread Crawford, or erred on the Twenty-Sixth Amendment. It

doesn’t challenge the finding that the State articulated no interest in the Directive,

disenfranchised voter. (App. 44a, 47a.) The federal law here is undisputed. What the

State disputes is what N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 654:12 and N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. §

      That isn’t a question that this Court grants certiorari to decide. “[W]e do not

normally grant petitions for certiorari solely to review what purports to be an

application of state law.” Leavitt v. Jane L., 518 U.S. 137, 144 (1996) (per curiam). A

petition “is rarely granted when the asserted error consists of erroneous factual

findings or the misapplication of a properly stated rule of law.” Sup. Ct. R. 10. Justice

Harlan denied a stay on exactly this ground where the applicant’s remaining

contention was that the court of appeals had “misinterpreted the relevant New York

will undertake to reassess the Court of Appeals’ view of the local law,” so “the

likelihood of certiorari being granted is thus too remote to justify” a stay. Birtcher

Corp. v. Diapulse Corp. of Am., 87 S. Ct. 6, 8 (1966) (Harlan, J., in chambers).

      Leavitt recognized an exception for “blatant federal-court nullification of state

at this juncture to enjoin HB 323, and it held the State to the reading of the safety

II.   The State hasn’t shown a fair prospect of reversal.

A.    The State doesn’t challenge the constitutional holdings or the
      findings that support them.

      The district court enjoined the Directive on two independent federal grounds,

doing so on findings made after three days of evidentiary hearings. Under Anderson

and Burdick, a court weighs the burden on the right to vote against “the precise

interests put forward by the State as justifications for the burden imposed by its rule.”

Burdick, 504 U.S. at 434 (quoting Anderson, 460 U.S. at 789). The court found a

burden that is “minimal, but not de minimis,” falling “exclusively on the population

most likely to hold student ID as their only form of identification,” and supported by

“direct evidence of at least one actual instance of disenfranchisement.” (App. 44a.) On

the other side of the scale, it found nothing. The State “has articulated no interest

specific to that rule at all.” (App. 47a.) It “has not identified any security or integrity

concern specific to student ID cards and why they are unique among other nongovernmental forms of identification such that they cannot satisfy a standard the

legislature left untouched.” (App. 47a.) Its own Deputy Secretary “did not see any

factual reason why a student ID could not be reasonable evidence” of identity. (App.

47a.) And when the Coalition argued that the Directive went beyond HB 323, the

obligated to construct a justification the State has not offered,” the Directive failed.

(App. 49a.)

      The application doesn’t engage with any of this. It doesn’t mention Anderson,

showing the district court’s findings to be wrong, a point taken up below. 549 U.S. at

5. A movant that leaves the grounds for an injunction unchallenged can’t make a

“strong showing” that the injunction was error, Rhode Island v. Trump, 155 F.4th at

47, let alone show a fair prospect that this Court would reverse it.

III.   Purcell cuts against a stay.

A.     The injunction didn’t alter New Hampshire’s election rules.

       The Purcell principle is that “lower federal courts should ordinarily not alter

the election rules on the eve of an election.” Republican Nat’l Comm. v. Democratic

Nat’l Comm., 589 U.S. 423, 424 (2020) (per curiam). Every case the State cites applied

that principle to a federal court that displaced a rule the State’s lawmakers had

enacted. In Purcell, the Ninth Circuit enjoined Arizona’s voter-identification statute.

549 U.S. at 2–3. In Republican National Committee, a district court extended a

statutory absentee-ballot deadline. 589 U.S. at 424. In Merrill, a court ordered

Alabama to redraw its congressional map. And in People Not Politicians, the Eighth

Circuit ordered Missouri to use a map that the Missouri Supreme Court had held “is

                                          24

---

<!-- page 33 -->
not the law and has never been the law.” Slip op. 1. This Court’s point in each case

was the same: “States are free to decide for themselves whether last-minute changes

to an election are in their best interests.” Allen v. Milligan, 608 U.S. 511, 514 (2026)

(per curiam) (quoted in People Not Politicians, slip op. 4). A “State legislature’s

decision either to keep or to make changes to election rules” is not for federal courts

to second-guess in the weeks before voting. Andino v. Middleton, 141 S. Ct. 9, 10

(2020) (Kavanaugh, J., concurring in grant of application for stay).

       Here the legislature decided, and the injunction honors its decision. HB 323

removed student IDs from the ballot clerk’s list. The legislature left both safety valves

in place, and when it returned to the statutes in July it amended the safety valves

with HB 317 and “did not address student IDs at all.” (App. 27a.) The Directive is

what changed the rules. It took a case-by-case standard that the legislature chose to

keep and carved out one document, on the Secretary’s say-so, without offering any

explanation. (App. 47a.) The State itself concedes that “the district court did not

enjoin enforcement of HB 323 itself.” (Appl. 3.) What the court enjoined was “a related

directive from the Secretary of State that reaches further than the statute itself.”

(App. 15a.) The district court didn’t alter that legislative choice. It enforced it.

       Purcell protects a State’s election laws from late judicial change. It doesn’t

protect an executive gloss on those laws from a ruling that the gloss lacks any

justification. When the Ninth Circuit affirmed an injunction against a provision of

Arizona’s Election Procedures Manual, it rejected the Secretary’s Purcell argument

because it was “unclear how enjoining the [provision] would disturb the status quo or

change. Carson v. Simon, 978 F.3d 1051, 1062 (8th Cir. 2020). That’s this case. The

October 6 municipal election. The State did in one business day what its application

says is “practically impossible” in two weeks.

      That changes the Purcell analysis in two ways. First, the status quo is now the

injunction. A stay “simply suspend[s] judicial alteration of the status quo.” Nken, 556

U.S. at 429. When the order being stayed has already been carried out, a stay doesn’t

preserve anything. It reverses what voters and officials have been told. This Court

recognized as much when it refused to stay a consent decree that Rhode Island had

already implemented: “The status quo is one in which the challenged requirement

has not been in effect,” and “many Rhode Island voters may well hold that belief.”

Republican Nat’l Comm. v. Common Cause R.I., 141 S. Ct. 206, 206 (2020). The First

Circuit applied the same reasoning five weeks ago, in a case against this Secretary,

when it denied a stay because voters “who have read and relied upon the Secretary

of State’s representation may arrive to register and vote on election day without the

newly required documentation and be turned away without recourse.” N.H. Youth

Movement v. Scanlan, No. 26-1750, 2026 WL 2620381, at *2 (1st Cir. Sept. 4, 2026).

And the Sixth Circuit denied Tennessee a stay where “the defendants themselves

prominently state” on the State’s website that the injunction governs, voters “relied

on communications from defendants and local election officials,” and “there is no

substantial harm to defendants in continuing to comply with rules they are currently

following.” Memphis A. Philip Randolph Inst. v. Hargett, 977 F.3d 566, 568–69 (6th

Cir. 2020).

Second, it identifies who is asking for a late change. People Not Politicians

turned on that question. The Eighth Circuit had blamed the Missouri Supreme Court

for destabilizing the election, and this Court answered that the state court “was not

responsible for the late-breaking changes”; the Secretary of State, “well aware” of the

risk, “nonetheless chose to proceed.” Slip op. 4. The same is true here, and the

Secretary is on the other side of it. He issued a Directive that went beyond the statute.

He declined to defend it when the court “probed this specific question.” (App. 70a–71a

n.15.) He bypassed the district court and “took four days to seek a stay from the Court

of Appeals.” (App. 69a.) He issued guidance to voters and local election officials and

then announced on the same website that he would ask to have it undone. Two weeks

before the election, with the guidance in voters’ hands, the only late change on the

table is the one the State asks this Court to order.

      The State’s answer is that “[t]he ability to blast-email revised instructions does

not establish that those instructions have displaced prior training and materials.”

(Appl. 14 n.1.) But the Directive itself was an email to local election officials and a

press release. (Supp. App. 2a (Dkt. No. 46 ¶ 3); App. 25a.) The revised guidance went

out the same way, through the same office, to the same people. And if one email isn’t

enough, the remedy is in the Secretary’s hands. The injunction orders guidance “as

soon as practicable.” (App. 66a.) Nothing stops him from further updating the

Manual, the posters, or the website. He has instead spent the two weeks since October

2 asking three courts to withdraw the guidance he issued. The “nuance” that the State

says officials and voters can’t be trusted to grasp is one that the State stated in a

---

<!-- page 39 -->
sentence to the court of appeals: the injunction “preserves the exclusion of student

IDs as valid identification while permitting their consideration alongside other

evidence.” (Supp. App. 16a (C.A. Reply 11).)

      The State is right that Purcell doesn’t wait for voting to begin. (Appl. 9.) But

what has happened in the election so far still matters, because it determines whether

anyone has acted in reliance on the old rule. Here no one has. Absentee voters verify

their identity under a separate statute, N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 657:17-c, by a copy of

a listed ID, a notarized signature, or presenting a listed ID to the clerk. (Supp. App.

4a (Dkt. No. 46 ¶ 113).) The injunction doesn’t touch that statute, and neither would

a stay. When the district court ruled, “no absentee ballots have been returned,” and

the only ballots distributed had gone to military and overseas voters who hold

passports or military IDs. (App. 59a–60a.) New Hampshire has no early in-person

voting. The injunction affects one thing: what a supervisor of the checklist may

consider on November 3 when a voter arrives without a listed ID. A stay would change

nothing for the 97% of voters who hold a listed ID, nothing for absentee voters, and

everything for those who have been told, on the Secretary’s own website, that they

may bring what they have. (App. 71a.)

D.    The district court weighed the Coalition’s delay, and the State’s own
      delay counts against it.

      The State says the Coalition “waited more than four months” to sue. (Appl. 4,

12.) The district court agreed that the lawsuit’s timing was a problem, and it held the

Coalition to it by denying relief for the primary. (App. 1a–14a, 61a.) It then found

that “[b]oth parties” had “contributed to the difficulty of this case” and declined to
                                          31

---

<!-- page 40 -->
“tip the equities toward defendants based on a delay whose consequences both sides

share.” (App. 61a–63a.) That is a discretionary judgment, made on a record that the

State doesn’t contest. Benisek v. Lamone, 585 U.S. 155 (2018) (per curiam), on which

the State relies, affirmed a district court’s discretion to weigh a plaintiff’s delay in

denying relief. Id. at 159–60. It didn’t hold that a reviewing court may reweigh delay

to grant relief that the district court denied. And the State’s own delay counts against

it. The State measures from April 3, when HB 323 was signed. But the Directive it

defends issued April 23, the “only” language it relies on was enacted July 2, and the

statutes it says the court misread were amended July 10. The State had the district

court’s ruling date in advance, didn’t ask that court for a stay, and waited four days

to ask the wrong court. (App. 69a.) An applicant whose timing problem is “[p]artly

from defendants’ own doing” can’t invoke the calendar against the other side.

Memphis A. Philip Randolph Inst., 977 F.3d at 568.

IV.   The remaining factors favor the Coalition.

A.    The State hasn’t shown irreparable harm.

      A stay applicant must show that irreparable injury is likely, not that there is

“some possibility” of it. Nken, 556 U.S. at 434–35. The State’s showing rests on

Maryland v. King: “[a]ny time a State is enjoined by a court from effectuating statutes

enacted by representatives of its people, it suffers a form of irreparable injury.” 567

U.S. 1301, 1303 (2012) (Roberts, C.J., in chambers). (Appl. 13.) But no statute has

been enjoined. The statutes enacted by New Hampshire’s representatives are in force,

and the injunction holds the Secretary to them as the State itself described them.

                                          32

---

<!-- page 41 -->
(App. 63a, 70a.) The interest that King protects is the legislature’s, and the

legislature’s choice is the one that the injunction enforces. What the injunction

displaces is a Directive that the district court found the State had never justified.

(App. 47a.) A State’s sovereign interest lies in enforcing requirements that are “likely

consistent with” the Constitution, Little, 591 U.S. at 1062 (Roberts, C.J., concurring),

not in enforcing a rule that two federal grounds say it may not enforce.

      The State’s conduct confirms the point. It issued guidance to local election

officials the next business day, and it concedes as much. (Appl. 14 n.1.) When this

Secretary acted on an injunction in New Hampshire Youth Movement and

“immediately reinstated” the affected procedures, the First Circuit held that the

State’s own conduct undermined its claim of irreparable harm. 2026 WL 2620381, at

*1. “[T]here is no substantial harm to defendants in continuing to comply with rules

they are currently following.” Memphis A. Philip Randolph Inst., 977 F.3d at 568.

The only harms the State describes are the confusion and administrative burden that

the district court found “negligible” and “modest and surmountable” on the evidence,

and those are the harms a stay would cause. (App. 60a–61a.)

B.    A stay would harm voters and the public interest.

      The balance of harms and the public interest “merge when the Government is

the opposing party.” Nken, 556 U.S. at 435. Both favor the Coalition. The district

court found direct evidence that the Directive had already disenfranchised at least

one voter, and it found that denying relief “potentially leaves young and often firsttime voters without government-issued forms of ID to get through DMV

                                          33

---

<!-- page 42 -->
appointments, fees or vouchers, and trips to multiple offices before election day,”

through a voter ID program that the record suggests “is simply not operational.” (App.

44a, 60a, 72a.) A restriction on the right to vote is an irreparable harm, and “[o]nce

the election occurs, there can be no do-over and no redress.” (App. 58a–59a (quoting

League of Women Voters of N.C. v. North Carolina, 769 F.3d 224, 247 (4th Cir. 2014)).)

New Hampshire lets voters register on election day, but the free voter ID that the

State points to is printed overnight and mailed, so it can’t be had the same day. For

a student with no other ID, the Directive takes election-day registration off the table,

and the court found that “at least one voter experienced exactly this sequence and

was disenfranchised as a result.” (App. 42a–43a.) A stay would withdraw guidance

that is already in voters’ hands and tell them, for the second time in a month, that

the evidence they have will not be considered. The State says the Coalition’s delay

“undermines its assertions of irreparable harm.” (Appl. 13–14.) But the irreparable

harm here is a voter’s, not a litigant’s. A ballot that isn’t cast on November 3 is lost

whether the Coalition sued in April or in August, and the district court has already

discounted the Coalition’s delay once, by denying relief for the primary. (App. 1a–14a,

61a.)

        The State’s public-interest argument assumes that a statute has been

enjoined. (Appl. 13.) None has. The legislature’s choices are in effect under the

injunction. The Secretary’s gloss on them is not, and “[t]he public is not served by a

court enforcing a rule that may exceed an agency’s statutory authority.” (App. 57a

(quoting R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Food & Drug Admin., No. 25-40137, 2026 WL

                                          34

---

<!-- page 43 -->
2409778, at *10 (5th Cir. Aug. 18, 2026)).) Five weeks ago, the First Circuit held that

“the public interest weighs against granting a stay” where voters had relied on this

Secretary’s own representations about what they would need at the polls. N.H. Youth

Movement, 2026 WL 2620381, at *2. That’s this case. A stay would protect no one. It

would turn away the voters who arrive on November 3 with the evidence the

Secretary’s website told them to bring. The public interest lies in letting eligible

voters prove who they are on election day, without a second reversal in the final

weeks.

                                     Conclusion

      The State asks this Court to reinstate a Directive that it has never defended,

on findings that it has never challenged, under a theory that it never presented to

either court below. The injunction enforces New Hampshire’s statutes as the State

itself described them, on two independent federal grounds. The Secretary has already

issued guidance to local election officials and voters. A stay wouldn’t preserve the

status quo. It would change the rules for the second time in a month, two weeks before

the election, in the direction that keeps eligible voters from proving who they are, and

it would make this Court the author of the only late change in this election. The Court

should deny the application and the request for an administrative stay.
