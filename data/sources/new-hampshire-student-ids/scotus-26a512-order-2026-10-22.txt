  “This Court has repeatedly emphasized that lower federal courts should ordinarily not alter the election rules on
the eve of an election.” Republican National Committee v.
Democratic National Committee, 589 U. S. 423, 424 (2020)
(per curiam). Here, the District Court did not point to anything that would justify deviating from that principle. See
People Not Politicians v. Onder, 609 U. S. ___ (Sept. 25,
2026). Accordingly, the application for stay presented to
JUSTICE JACKSON and by her referred to the Court is
granted. The October 2, 2026, and October 5, 2026, orders
granting preliminary injunctive relief by the United States
District Court for the District of New Hampshire, case No.
1:26–cv–647, are stayed pending the disposition of the appeal in the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit and disposition of a petition for a writ of certiorari, if
such a writ is timely sought. Should certiorari be denied,
this stay shall terminate automatically. In the event certiorari is granted, the stay shall terminate upon the sending
down of the judgment of this Court.

2        SCANLAN v. COALITION FOR OPEN DEMOCRACY

                          JACKSON, J., dissenting

   JUSTICE JACKSON, dissenting.
   The Purcell case involved a federal court order that had
completely enjoined enforcement of a state statute requiring voters to provide proof of identity when attempting to
vote. Purcell v. Gonzalez, 549 U. S. 1 (2006) (per curiam).
Given the timing and the potential impact of that disruptive ruling, this Court said that lower courts deciding
whether to enjoin an election rule in the lead-up to an election must consider the potential that the order itself might
be harmful, insofar as it could cause “voter confusion” or
create “incentive[s] to remain away from the polls.” Id., at
4–5.
   Importantly, this so-called “Purcell principle” arose in a
particular context, and this Court has yet to fully define its
limits. It seems to me that a case involving a court order
like the one at issue here—which left the state election rule
untouched—does not necessarily fall within the ambit of
Purcell and its concern about federal courts “changing the
election rules” at the last minute. Republican National
Committee v. Democratic National Committee, 589 U. S.
423, 424 (2020) (per curiam).
   Here, the District Court made a (certainly not clearly erroneous) determination that correcting a publicized misstatement about what New Hampshire’s election rules require was necessary to ensure that certain citizens were not
unduly burdened in their exercise of the fundamental right
to vote.* So it issued a targeted order, requiring only that

   Consequently, here, the District Court’s injunction was
reasonably designed to address alleged misinformation, not
to change the law. Under such circumstances, the relief the
District Court provided actually promoted the interests
Purcell sought to protect. When a federal court determines
that material misrepresentations are being made about a
State’s election rules, an injunction to prohibit that conduct
does not contribute to public confusion, it prevents that very
problem. Cf. Purcell, 549 U. S., at 4–5 (highlighting the risk
of confusion “result[ing]” from “[c]ourt orders”).
   I respectfully dissent because—even, and perhaps especially, when an election is looming—I would not disturb a
clearly justifiable federal court order issued to ensure that
the public receives accurate information about a State’s
electoral processes.
