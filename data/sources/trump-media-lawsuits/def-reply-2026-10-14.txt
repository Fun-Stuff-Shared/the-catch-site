        In a last-ditch effort to save his claims, Plaintiff pivots to a new theory of actual

  malice in his opposition (“Opp.”) (Doc. 104). All but gone are his arguments that

  relying on tax returns from Charles Littlejohn or the failure to obtain an interview with

  Mark Burnett somehow supports actual malice. Instead, Plaintiff argues for the first

  time that “Defendants’ own materials contradicted what they published.” Opp. at 2.

  These purported “contradictions” consist of nothing more than general denials from

  Plaintiff’s own lawyer and out-of-context statements that do noy actually contradict

  the Challenged Statements. Thus, even if this Court were to consider Plaintiff’s

  improper effort to amend his complaint through his opposition, the purported

  “contradictions” with prior publications do not save his claim.

                                     BACKGROUND

        Craig and Buettner published the 2018 Article based largely on documents from

  Plaintiff’s niece, Mary Trump. Doc. 44-4. The Article describes, in meticulous detail

  across 38 pages, the various methods the Trump family used to reduce gift and estate

  taxes, including undervaluing real estate, disguising gifts as loans, and funneling

  money through sham companies like All County. Id. at 6. At the outset, the 2018

  Article quotes Plaintiff’s then-lawyer Charles Harder, stating the “allegations of fraud

  and tax evasion are 100 percent false” and “[t]here was no fraud or tax evasion by

  anyone” (the “General Denial”). Id. at 3. Towards the end of the Article, it reports on

  an IRS audit of Fred and Mary Trump’s combined estates completed in 2000, which

  concluded they were worth “more than Donald Trump and his siblings had claimed,”

  resulting in an “additional $5.2 million in estate taxes,” but still far less than if the

                                      ARGUMENT

  I.    The Denials in the 2018 Article Do Not Show Actual Malice

        Plaintiff’s opposition focuses on three of the Inheritance Statements, 1AC

  ¶¶ 20(a), 20(f), and 20(k), which he now calls the Tax Fraud Statements, and argues

  that the General Denial and Estate Tax Comment provided a “verifiable” reason to

  doubt those Challenged Statements. Opp. at 7–8. The argument fails. First, the General

  Denial does not show Defendants subjectively doubted the truth of any Challenged

  Statement. “Denials are so commonplace” that “they hardly alert the conscientious

  reporter to the likelihood of error.” Harte-Hanks Commc’ns, Inc. v. Connaughton, 491

  U.S. 657, 691 n.37 (1989) (cleaned up); see Trump v. Dow Jones & Co., Inc., 833 F. Supp.

  3d 1359, 1373 (S.D. Fla. 2026); Brimelow v. N.Y. Times Co., 2021 WL 4901969, at *2-3

  (2d Cir. Oct. 21, 2021). Here, there is nothing “specific and concrete” “in the content

  of the denial” nor any “supporting evidence produced in conjunction with the denial

  that carries a doubt-inducing quality.” Montgomery v. Risen, 197 F. Supp. 3d 219, 263

  (D.D.C. 2016), aff’d, 875 F.3d 709 (D.C. Cir. 2017). This conclusion is particularly

  sound here given Plaintiff’s failure to challenge the accuracy of the 2018 Article,

  including the Tax Fraud Statements, in the intervening six years before publication of

  the Book. Crucially, from Buettner and Craig’s perspective, Plaintiff tacitly admitted

  the truth of their detailed reporting. As reported in the 2018 Article, Plaintiff’s lawyer

  suggested The Times would face “substantial liability” for defamation if it reported

  Plaintiff participated in any fraud or tax evasion. Doc. 44-4 at 25. Yet, in 2021, when

  Plaintiff actually sued over the 2018 Article, he did not challenge “the truth of any

        Second, Harder’s Estate Tax Comment should be disregarded as Plaintiff once

  again mischaracterizes what the Book says. Plaintiff’s opposition suggests that

  Defendants had a “specific reason to doubt” the Tax Fraud Statements—particularly

  that Fred Trump’s loans to his son were “a likely tax fraud that went unnoticed”—

  because in the 2018 Article Plaintiff’s counsel said the relevant taxing authorities had

  “examined the challenged positions in ‘real time’ and ‘fully approved all of the tax

  filings.’” Opp. at 3, 8 (citing Doc. 44-4 at 3, 35). But nothing in the 2018 Article or

  Book supports the conclusion that the taxing authorities that reviewed the Trumps’

  estate taxes ever examined “challenged positions” in “real time” concerning Fred

  Trump’s forgiven loans over decades or the All County “invoice-padding” that began

  in 1992, the events at issue in the Tax Fraud Statements. To the contrary, the 2018

  Article expressly reports that such transfers—which occurred years earlier—were not

  included in the estate tax audit that taxing authorities completed after Mary’s death in

  August 2000, but should have been. Doc. 44-4 at 34–35. Critically, the Book also

  reports on the audits that Harder’s Estate Tax Comment was responding to, again

  reporting that the IRS found an increase in the estate’s value, but the family still paid

  “hundreds of millions of dollars less than they would have paid in gift or estate taxes”

  II.   Plaintiff’s Other Supposed Contradictions Are Also Non-Existent

        As to the Apprentice Statements, despite Plaintiff’s efforts, it is not

  contradictory to report that Burnett agreed to share profits 50/50 to “lock in someone

  of Trump’s stature,” Book at 345–46; 366, and also to conclude that “other people

  would do all the work,” 1AC ¶ 20(q), with respect to product placement opportunities.

        And the Survey Article’s hyperlinks provide supporting context for the article’s

  discussion, not contradiction. “The hyperlink is the twenty-first century equivalent of

  the footnote for purposes of attribution in defamation law.” Adelson v. Harris, 973 F.

  Supp. 2d 467, 484 (S.D.N.Y. 2013), aff’d, 876 F.3d 413 (2d Cir. 2017). For example,

  the article includes both Plaintiff’s denial of his niece’s SAT cheating allegation and

  the statement that “the widow” of the alleged “test-taking friend said that she was

  confident her husband did no such thing.” Doc. 44-3 at 7. It links to an ABC article

  reporting that the widow said that “if the book [wa]s referencing her late husband,”

  then the allegation was false because her recollection was that her husband did not

  meet Plaintiff until they were students at Wharton. https://tinyurl.com/mu7s3end.

  Project Veritas v. Cable News Network, Inc., 121 F.4th 1267, 1283–84 (11th Cir. 2024),

  thus does not help Plaintiff. There, the CNN employee first accurately reported that

  Twitter suspended Project Veritas for publishing private information and then, four

  days later, made a contradictory statement that the suspension was due to it

  “promoting misinformation.” Id. at 1272. Here, by contrast, the linked-to material

  hyperlink to a 1998 U.S. Treasury Department press release provides the supporting

  penalty for “BSA [Bank Secrecy Act] violations” based on failing “to comply with

  Treasury’s anti-money laundering requirements.” https://tinyurl.com/3axx3h96.

  III.   Plaintiff’s Theory Is Untethered to the 2AC and Should Not Be Considered

         Plaintiff’s new theory of actual malice suffers from another defect: it is not

  properly before the Court. The 2AC does not cite Harder or his 2018 comments or

  invoke any denials. Plaintiff “cannot now use his briefing to add new [factual]

  allegations and argue that those new assertions support his cause of action.” Michel v.

  NYP Holdings, Inc., 816 F.3d 686, 705–06 (11th Cir. 2016); see also, e.g., Orta v. City of

  Orlando, 2015 WL 2365834, at *5 n.3 (M.D. Fla. May 18, 2015).
