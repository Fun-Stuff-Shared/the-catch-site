            The defendants move (Docs. 44, 103, and 107) to dismiss the plaintiff ’s second

    amended complaint (the complaint, Doc. 99). The plaintiff responds (Docs. 81 and

            No party disagrees that New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), is

    the source of the law that governs the pleading standard a plaintiff must overcome to

    state a claim for defamation against a public official, such as the plaintiff. Times v.

    Sullivan states that the First Amendment “prohibits a public official’s recovering

    damages for a defamatory falsehood relating to his official conduct unless he proves

    that the statement was made with ‘actual malice’ — that is, with knowledge that it

    was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not.” 84 S. Ct. at 726.

    Twenty-five years later, Justice Stevens in Harte-Hanks Communications v. Connaughton, 401 U.S. 657, 686 (1989), states, “The meaning of terms such as ‘actual malice’

    — and, more particularly ‘reckless disregard’ — however, is not readily captured in

    one infallible definition” but, rather, “only through the course of case-by-case

    adjudication can we give content to these otherwise elusive constitutional standards.” (The quotations in this order, all of which are familiar to the parties, are — in

    the current phrase — “cleaned up” for ease of reading.)

          Times v. Sullivan has germinated in the courts and over the years decisional

    precedent in the Supreme Court and elsewhere — about which judges, lawyers, and

    others have composed countless pages of commentary, some laudatory and some antagonistic — has lent a degree of content and texture to the “actual malice” requirement but more often by specifying what “actual malice” or “reckless disregard” is

    not, rather than what it is, and by specifying what facts are insufficient to allege actual malice rather than what facts are sufficient. For example, even in Harte-Hanks,

    Justice Stevens finds that “failure to investigate before publishing, even when a reasonably prudent person would have done so, is not sufficient to establish reckless disregard,” but he immediately adds that “reckless disregard may be found where there

    are obvious reasons to doubt the veracity of the informant or the accuracy of his report.” Perhaps sensing the unsatisfying content of those observations and perhaps

    attempting to clarify, Justice Stevens later in Harte-Hanks states, “Although failure to

    investigate will not alone support a finding of actual malice, the purposeful avoidance of the truth is in a different category.” 401 U.S. at 692. Against this indistinct

    background, the parties’ debate exactly how Times v. Sullivan and intervening precedent apply to the pleadings in this action.

                            The formidable edifice of “actual malice”

           In the motion to dismiss, the defendants begin, of course, with Times v. Sullivan and explore in detail the principles and applications that have descended from

    Times v. Sullivan and how they combine and resolve to secure the protections of the

    “actual malice” standard for a publisher and an author. The defendants note with citations to authority that the challenged statements — about a presidential candidate

    published immediately preceding a presidential election — are “political speech of

    the highest order” and that reporting on that topic and at that moment “occupies the

    highest rung on the hierarchy of First Amendment values.” The defendants insist

    correctly that the standard of pleading required of a public-official plaintiff is “famously daunting,” an “exacting standard,” and the defendants proceed to delineate

    examples of the formidable standard “actual malice” erects.

           For example, in the matter of “actual malice,” the defendants correctly assert

    that “reckless disregard” for truth is a “purely subjective test” and the “beliefs or actions of a reasonable person are irrelevant.” The law, especially the tort law, is intensely concerned elsewhere with reasonableness, but not in the defamation of a public official. In other words, “all that matters are the speakers’ subjective beliefs about

    the truth of their own statements.” To warrant a finding of actual malice, “there

    must be sufficient evidence to permit the conclusion that the defendant in fact entertained serious doubts as to the truth of his publication,” that is, “sufficient evidence

    to permit the conclusion that the defendant actually had a high degree of awareness

    of probable falsity.”

          The defendants, again with citations to authority, note that, notwithstanding

    the ordinary meaning and usage of “actual malice,” “allegations of ill-will, improper

    motive or personal animosity play no role in determining whether a defendant acted

    with actual malice,” which “does not concern ill-will, improper motive, or personal

    animosity,” a conclusion which appears correct, in law if not elsewhere in the English language or in the common understanding. In sum, “actual malice is not about

    whether the speaker had evil intent or a motive arising from ill-will.”

          The defendants, again with citations to authority, remind the reader that “a

    departure from journalistic standards does not constitute actual malice.” In other

    words, no enforceable or other journalistic “standard of care” rises pari passu as the

    “order” of the journalistic speech heightens, as a loftier rung in the hierarchy of First

    Amendment values is achieved, and as coincidentally the potential for public and private injury from defaming a public official and misleading the electorate becomes

    more acute and consequential.

          The defendants add that neither “reliance on unnamed sources” nor choosing

    “not to speak with any witnesses who could have refuted the allegedly defamatory

    statements” nor a “failure to investigate” nor even a “failure to review records that

    are public” will establish actual malice.

          Another defensive principle featured by the defendants is that, although “actual malice” means publication “with knowledge that [the publication] was false or

    with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not,” the “required awareness of falsity is absent when the publisher’s claims are reasonably supported by numerous

    prior reports,” previously published in “reputable sources,” or “supported by corroborating reports in other news media.”

           As well, the defendants feature not only the fact that a writer or publisher has

    a “right to report information they believe to be true,” even if the report is untrue, but

    also that “reliance on a potentially biased source does not by itself establish actual

    malice.” A book publisher, the defendants recall, is “entitled as a matter of law to

    rely on an author’s reputation and experience” and on the author’s “reportorial abilities,” and a public-official plaintiff must show “independent actual malice,” that is,

    the publisher’s actual malice apart from and independent of the author, to establish

    actual malice.

           The defendants assert, again supported with citations to authority, that the

    plaintiff must allege facts, not mere conclusions, “specific to each Defendant and

    each statement in suit that plausibly allege actual malice, i.e., knowledge of falsity or

    probable falsity” and, to that end, that the plaintiff must allege specific facts plausibly

    showing that an author or a publisher had “a specific reason to doubt the truth of

    their reporting”; and that the plaintiff must allege “how each statement in suit is false

    or what [the plaintiff] contends the truth to be . . . .” The defendants note with citations that evidence of “extensive research” and “considerable corroboration” tends to

    weaken an inference of actual malice as to any statement and, similarly, that an inference of actual malice is weakened, “undercut” as the defendants write, by evidence of the solicitation of comment from the plaintiff and from others who might

          The defendants further note with citations to authority that “subjective interpretations of undisputed facts, which are not capable of being proven false” are

    support a defamation claim the statement “must be more than negative”; must

    “subject the plaintiff to ‘hatred, distrust, ridicule, contempt or disgrace’”; and must

               Pleading a plausible and reasonable inference of actual malice

          The plaintiff begins the defense of the complaint by repair to the familiar

    pleading standard of Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544 (2007), and Ashcroft

    v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662 (2009), summarized succinctly but precisely in Iqbal:

                 Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8(a)(2), a pleading must
                 contain a “short and plain statement of the claim showing that
                 the pleader is entitled to relief.” As the Court held in Twombly,
                 the pleading standard Rule 8 announces does not require “detailed factual allegations,” but it demands more than an unadorned, the-defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation. A
                 pleading that offers “labels and conclusions” or “a formulaic
                 recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do.” Nor
                 does a complaint suffice if it tenders “naked assertion[s]” devoid of “further factual enhancement.”

                 To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to “state a claim to relief
                 that is plausible on its face.” A claim has facial plausibility
                 when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to
                 draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the
                 misconduct alleged. The plausibility standard is not akin to a
                 “probability requirement,” but it asks for more than a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully. Where a complaint pleads facts that are “merely consistent with” a defendant's liability, it “stops short of the line between possibility and
                 plausibility of ‘entitlement to relief.’”

          The plaintiff, citing authority, states that “a public-figure plaintiff need only allege facts sufficient to give rise to a reasonable inference of actual malice — not direct proof.” Of course, the plaintiff necessarily agrees that, based on Times v. Sullivan, the reasonable and plausible inference that the facts alleged in the complaint

    must create is that the author or the publisher uttered defamatory statements “with

    knowledge they [they were] false or with reckless disregard of whether [they were]

    false or not.” The plaintiff says reckless disregard is revealed by “the circumstances

    surrounding the publication,” by allegations of “purposeful avoidance of the truth,”

    by facts showing “the statement was inherently implausible or that there were obvious reasons to doubt the veracity of the informant,” by facts showing “adherence to a

    pre-conceived narrative,” and by facts showing “reliance on anonymous or unreliable sources without further investigation.”

          In sum, the parties agree both that the standard of Times v. Sullivan controls

    and that the standard of Iqbal and Twombly controls. The parties have examined at

    length perhaps every angle on every application of these important standards to this

    action.

            Obviously, even a qualified expert’s offering a superficial characterization

     such as a transaction is “suspicious” or “could” violate the law or attributing to a

     transaction the (unspecified and undefined) “smell” of crime or noting that a gift tax

     return can evade IRS scrutiny or generously giving his immensely valuable time to

     aid laypersons “lost in the weeds” is far from a formal legal opinion sufficient to remove serious doubt about the truthfulness and accuracy of a statement accusing a

     specific person of fraud or tax evasion or some related crime arising from a specific

     transaction, including a crime allegedly effected through “ties to the Mafia.” The

     confident detection of tax and other crimes, including money laundering, of the kind

     that some of the challenged statements attribute to the plaintiff, that is, tax crimes including estate and gift tax crimes and the like, is a task that is intensely fact-dependent, and the law that governs is singularly complex and within the immediate competence of only a relatively small cohort of specialized practitioners. Anyone who publishes the claim that someone else has committed a crime of the complex nature described in some of the challenged statements and who has not received an explicit

     opinion from a qualified source after a thorough examination of pertinent facts necessarily proceeds with particularized reason to doubt the truth of their statements and

     with knowledge of a high degree of probable falsity of the statements; in other words,

     they know that they don’t know, but they are undeterred. Knowing of the necessity

     for that opinion but failing to procure that opinion is a fine example of the purposeful

     avoidance of the truth, a knowing failure that remains actionable, even after Times v.

     Sullivan.

                                         CONCLUSION

            For these reasons, including the reasons detailed by the defendants in the motion to dismiss and the supplement, the motion to dismiss Counts III and IV is

     GRANTED, but the motion to dismiss Counts I, II, V, and VI is DENIED. The

     twenty-seven challenged statements listed on page eight of this order (paragraphs 39,

     43, 47, 51, 59, 63, 67, 71, 88, 92, 96, 100, 104, 112, 116, 120, 125, 129, 133, 137,

     141, 145, 149, 153, 157, 162, and 166) are not actionable as, and will not proceed further in this action as claims of, defamation published with “actual malice.” The

     plaintiff ’s request to again amend the complaint is DENIED because no reason appears, even after initial discovery, to conclude that an additional amendment is not

     futile. The defendants must answer within twenty-eight days. Nothing in this order

     constitutes a ruling as to the admissibility at trial of evidence of the content and circumstances of any of the twenty-seven challenged statements listed above but not actionable as defamation.
