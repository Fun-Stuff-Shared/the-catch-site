                                        INTRODUCTION

        After three attempts to submit a viable complaint—the last with the benefit of

 extensive briefing, the Court’s questioning at oral argument, and the depositions of

 five witnesses involved in Defendants’ publications—Plaintiff still fails to plead a

 plausible defamation claim as to the Challenged Statements.1

        As to actual malice, the additions to Plaintiff’s Second Amended Complaint

 (“2AC”), Doc. 99, taken alone or together with the entire 2AC, still fail to plausibly

 allege that Defendants were “highly aware” that any of the Challenged Statements

 was false or “entertained serious doubts” as to their truth. Jacoby v. Cable News Network,

 Inc., 2021 WL 5858569, at *5 (11th Cir. Dec. 10, 2021). For most of the Challenged

 Statements, Plaintiff offers no new allegations of actual malice and instead reasserts

 allegations he no longer has a good faith basis to allege. For example, Plaintiff alleges

 again that, in the 2016 Jim Rutenberg article, The Times “admit[s] that [it] view[s]

 President Trump as a threat who does not deserve fair treatment,” 2AC ¶ 218, even

 after this Court disputed that conclusion, observing that the article is instead “a

 rumination about the quandary of a journalist,” Doc. 95 (“Hearing Tr.”) at 60:7–22.

        For the remaining Inheritance Statements and the Survey Statements, Plaintiff

 mainly alleges that Defendants did not reconfirm previously reported facts that had

 not been challenged. But there is nothing improper about Susanne Craig and Russ

       A. Failure to Plead Defamatory Meaning

       Whether a statement is reasonably capable of a defamatory interpretation is a

 question of law for the Court. See, e.g., Keller v. Miami Herald Pub. Co., 778 F.2d 711,

 714–15 (11th Cir. 1985). Plaintiff “cherry picks statements in the [Book and Articles]

 out of context.” Turner, 879 F.3d at 1267. But in determining the gist of a challenged

 statement, the Court must look to “the entire context” of the publication. Rubin v. U.S.

 News & World Report, Inc., 271 F.3d 1305, 1306 (11th Cir. 2001). As one example, what

 Plaintiff identifies as the sting of the challenged Business and Apprentice Statements—

       As to the statements of opinion based on disclosed facts, 1AC ¶¶ 20(b)–20(m),

 20(q), 20(u), 21(d), 21(i), 21(e)–21(f), Plaintiff’s conclusory allegation that they are

 “not an expression of an opinion” is entitled to no weight. See Turner, 879 F.3d at 126263 (“Whether the statement is one of fact or opinion…[is] question[] of law for the

 court.”). Plaintiff does not dispute the facts upon which the opinions are based—even

 after Defendants set them forth in detail in their motion. For example, the 2AC leaves

 unchallenged the bases for the subjective characterization that he was “drawn to men

 who displayed a penchant for violence,” 1AC ¶ 20(d), including his choosing to hire a

 former classmate who brutally beat another child and a security guard who “knocked

 … around and shoved” two young men. Book at 63, 69, 174. While Plaintiff may

 disagree with Defendants’ opinions, opinions based on unchallenged disclosed facts

 are not actionable as a matter of law.

       C. Damages

       Plaintiff no longer specifies which statements are defamation per se and which

 are defamation per quod, but the latter—at minimum, 1AC ¶¶ 20(d), 20(g), 20(h),

 20(n), 20(q), and 20(s)—must be dismissed for failure to plead special damages.

                                    CONCLUSION

       For the foregoing reasons, and those set forth in Defendants’ motion, Doc. 44,

 the 2AC should be dismissed. And, because Plaintiff has now had three opportunities

 to try to state a claim, including with the benefit of five depositions, dismissal should

 be with prejudice.
