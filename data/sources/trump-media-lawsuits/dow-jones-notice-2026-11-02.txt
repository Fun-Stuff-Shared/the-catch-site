        Pursuant to Local Rule 3.01(j), Defendants submit notice of the following

  supplemental authority in support of their pending motion to dismiss the Second

  Amended Complaint: Trump v. Dow Jones & Co., No. 1:25-cv-23232-DPG, Doc. 80

  (S.D. Fla. Oct. 31, 2026) (“Dow Jones Order”). Because the Dow Jones Order is not yet

  readily available, a copy is attached as Exhibit A.

        The decision in Dow Jones dismissing with prejudice plaintiff President Donald

  J. Trump’s claims for defamation per se and defamation per quod supplements the

  following arguments raised in Defendants’ motion to dismiss papers.

  1.    Pleading actual malice requires more than conclusory or irrelevant

        allegations. See Doc. 44 at 10–14; Doc. 103 at 2–3, 11–12.

               “[T]he allegations regarding malice are conclusory, vague,
               sometimes inconsistent with the Article, and often irrelevant….”
               Dow Jones Order at 14.

  2.    Pre-publication denials do not establish actual malice. See Doc. 44 at 28; Doc.

        103 at 10, 12; Doc. 107 at 2–4.

               “Though vague, the Amended Complaint appears to allege that by
               choosing to publish the Article despite President Trump’s and his
               team’s denials, Defendants acted with actual malice…. But that is
               not what the actual malice standard requires and runs afoul of the
               First Amendment. Indeed, were that the law, any time a public
               figure or his team denies an accusation, journalists would be
               immediately blocked from publishing negative or contradictory
               press—a result that flies in the face of the ‘American privilege to
               speak one’s mind, although not always with perfect good taste, on
               all public institutions.’” Dow Jones Order at 15–16 (citation
               omitted).

  3.    An alleged failure to investigate does not establish actual malice, and

        contacting people for comment, even if they do not respond, does not show

        a failure to investigate. See Doc. 44 at 13–18, 20–21, 25–26, 27–28; Doc. 103

        at 2–3, 5, 8–9, 10–12.

              “[A] failure to investigate, without more, does not establish actual
              malice. Rather, ‘a plaintiff must show the defendant deliberately
              avoided investigating the veracity of the statement in order to
              evade learning the truth.’ . . . The Amended Complaint comes
              nowhere close to this standard. Indeed, the Article details a
              thoughtful and detailed investigation. Before running the story,
              Defendants contacted President Trump and his team, Justice
              Department officials, and the FBI for comment . . . Defendants
              also attempted to speak with Maxwell; she did not respond to their
              request . . . . In addition, Defendants reached out to other
              individuals . . . . In short, President Trump’s allegation that
              Defendants failed to conduct an adequate investigation is rebutted
              by the Article and is insufficient to establish actual malice.” Dow
              Jones Order at 15 (citations omitted).

  4.    The defamation per quod claims must be dismissed for failure to plead special

        damages. See Doc. 44 at 44–45; Doc. 103 at 15.

              “Reputational harm, however, is not a special damage. . . .
              Moreover, this allegation [that special damages include lost
              profits, out of pocket losses, and realized losses in the form of
              reputational harm], without more, is too conclusory to plead
              special damages.” Dow Jones Order at 19 n.21 (citation omitted).

  5.    Dismissal should be with prejudice. See Doc. 103 at 15.

              “[B]ecause this is President Trump’s second attempt to allege his
              claims, the Court finds that dismissal with prejudice is
              appropriate.” Dow Jones Order at 19.
