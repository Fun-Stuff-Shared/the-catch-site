                                      ARGUMENT

    I.      The SAC plausibly alleges actual malice.

         A complaint pleads actual malice when it alleges “facts sufficient to give rise to

 a reasonable inference” that the defendant knew its statement was false or recklessly

 disregarded its truth. Michel v. NYP Holdings, Inc., 816 F.3d 686, 702 (11th Cir. 2016).

 In Project Veritas, the Eleventh Circuit applied that standard to reverse the dismissal of

 a complaint alleging that the defendant’s reporter had stated the true facts, days before

 the network broadcast a false account. 121 F.4th at 1283–84. The inference may rest

 on “any competent evidence, either direct or circumstantial.” Herbert v. Lando, 441

 U.S. 153, 164 n.12 (1979). Recklessness may be found when a publisher has “obvious

 reasons to doubt” the source’s veracity or the accuracy of the reports. St. Amant v.

 Thompson, 390 U.S. 727, 732 (1968). “[P]urposeful avoidance of the truth,” including

 a “deliberate decision not to acquire knowledge of facts that might confirm the

 probable falsity” of the charge, supports a finding of actual malice. Harte-Hanks

 Commc’ns, Inc. v. Connaughton, 491 U.S. 657, 692 (1989). So does evidence of the

 defendant’s motive and the care it took. Id. at 668. The SAC pleads these facts.

    II.        The Supplement’s remaining arguments fail.

          A.     Defamatory meaning.

          Section II.A of the Supplement addresses the Business, Penchant, and

 Apprentice Statements on allegations already before the Court. ECF No. 103 at 8.

 Those allegations were sufficient when first pleaded, for the reasons in ECF No. 81,

 and President Trump stands on that briefing as to the Business and Penchant

 Statements. ECF No. 81. Additionally, Section II.A fails on its own terms. A

 publication is defamatory per se if it “charges that a person has committed an infamous

 crime” or “tends to injure one in his trade or profession.” Loomer v. Maher, 2025 WL

 756549, at *3 (M.D. Fla. Jan. 16, 2025). The Tax Fraud Statements charge crimes

 outright. ECF No. 99 ¶¶ 32, 55, 75. Other false, malicious, and defamatory Statements

 improperly strike at a sterling professional identity built over four decades: that

 President Trump’s fortune came “mostly outside of his ability to run a business,” and

 that “the less he has been involved in decision-making, the better his chances of

 financial success.” Id. ¶ 43.

          Defendants respond that the Book elsewhere documents President Trump’s

 successes, so the “entire context” defeats the sting. ECF No. 103 at 12–13. But a

 publication does not neutralize a defamatory gist by including favorable details

 alongside it; were it otherwise, no book could ever defame. Context is assessed “in its

 totality,” Hay v. Indep. Newspapers, Inc., 450 So. 2d 293, 295 (Fla. 2d DCA 1984), and,

 here, the totality begins on the cover, which tells the reader that this is the story of How

        B.     Falsity and opinion.

        Defendants’ falsity arguments ask the Court to resolve disputes that the SAC

 raises. The false claim that President Trump “had sold the bulk of his father’s empire

 for almost $250 million less than it was worth,” ECF No. 99 ¶ 96, is a specific

 numerical claim, and the SAC alleges that assets were not valued at the falsely stated

 figures, id. ¶¶ 97, 154. Rule 8 does not require a plaintiff to plead a competing appraisal,

 in order to deny a false one.

        Separately, Defendants argue that borrowing the jacket with permission

 “effectively confirms” the medals statement. ECF No. 103 at 13. Not true. Permission

 confirms only that President Trump wore the jacket. The Statement charges more: that

 he “knowingly” wore it, “in effect appropriating medals that he did not win himself.”

 ECF No. 99 ¶ 162. The sting is deceit—passing off another’s honors as one’s own—

 and the SAC states that he never claimed the medals were his, and wore the jacket

 “with the full consent and endorsement of his classmate, Michael Scadron.” Id. ¶ 163.

 Whether a photograph taken with permission, and without any claim of entitlement,

 amounts to “appropriating” honors is not for the Court to resolve against the pleading.

        The SAT and money laundering statements are addressed in Part I.A: a

 widow’s denial and an agency release naming only an entity establish falsity, not

 substantial truth. ECF No. 99 ¶¶ 167, 171, 173. That President Trump “does not

 report privilege, which protects only a substantially accurate account of the proceeding

 reported. Woodard v. Sunbeam Television Corp., 616 So. 2d 501, 502 (Fla. 3d DCA 1993).

       Defendants’ final, wrong position is that the Challenged Statements are

 opinions resting on disclosed facts that President Trump “does not dispute.” ECF No.

 103 at 15. He disputes them. ECF No. 99 ¶¶ 48, 59–60, 67–68, 105, 232. In addition,

 Michel, 816 F.3d at 699). When a media outlet publishes accusations under these

 circumstances, it is encouraging the “reasonable reader to be less skeptical and more

 Co., 82 N.Y.2d 146, 156 (1993). Thus, Defendants’ opinion arguments fail.
