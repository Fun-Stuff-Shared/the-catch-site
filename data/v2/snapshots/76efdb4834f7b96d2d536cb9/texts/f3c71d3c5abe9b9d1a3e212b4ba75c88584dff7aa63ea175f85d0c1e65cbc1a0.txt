INTRODUCTION
The Constitution commits the power to regulate elections exclusively to the
States and Congress. Both California and Congress have exercised that authority to
develop rules for election administration, and California elections are administered
in accordance with those rules. Now, in the middle of an election, the federal
Executive Branch demands that California officials cast those indisputably lawful
rules aside. Specifically, Plaintiff demands that Defendants refrain from counting
the votes of individuals on a list of over 20,000 allegedly registered voters compiled
by the federal government, and who Plaintiff “believes” to be “likely noncitizens.”
Plaintiff purports to locate authority for this dramatic assertion of Executive power
in a federal misdemeanor prohibition on individual noncitizen voting. This tortured
legal theory offends the letter and spirit of the Constitution.
Plaintiff’s lawsuit asks the Court to credit a few remarkable propositions: that
the federal government has standing any time it believes that state law does not
adequately prevent a federal crime; that the government can override state law
whenever it imagines that state policies might allow a third party to break the law,
and even compel States to cooperate in law enforcement efforts; and that relief
sought pursuant to this dubious theory need not bother with constitutional or
statutory limitations. Each one of these arguments fail, foreclosing Plaintiff’s claim
at the threshold and on the merits. The Court should grant State Defendants’ motion
to dismiss.
BACKGROUND
I. LEGAL BACKGROUND
California Secretary of State Dr. Shirley N. Weber is the State’s Chief Officer
of Elections. See Cal. Gov’t Code § 12172.5(a); Cal. Elec. Code § 10. California’s
58 counties are primarily responsible for administering elections in the State.
States hold primary responsibility for federal elections under the United States
Constitution, including their “Times, Places and Manner,” subject to laws enacted

by Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1; see also id. art. II, § 1, cl. 2. Unless
Congress provides otherwise, States therefore have the authority “to provide a
complete code for [federal] elections.” Smiley v. Holm, 285 U.S. 355, 366 (1932);
see also Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council of Ariz., Inc., 570 U.S. 1, 8–9 (2013)
(“ITCA”). States also have sole authority to set voter qualifications, or “who may
vote.” ITCA, 570 U.S. at 16; see also U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 1; id. amend. XVII.
United States citizenship is one of several requirements to vote in California
elections, and the State enforces this obligation in several ways. See Cal. Const. art.
2, § 2(a); Cal. Elec. Code §§ 2100, 2101. California requires all voter registration
applicants to attest to citizenship under oath, under penalty of perjury and by
signature. Cal. Elec. Code §§ 2101, 2102(c), 2111, 2150(a)(1), 2264(a)(3), (b).
Elections officials must cancel the registration of any voter “[u]pon proof that the
person is . . . ineligible to vote,” subject to procedural safeguards. Cal. Elec. Code
§§ 2201(a)(8), (c), 2213 (emphasis added). State law permits challenges to mail
ballots related to “the voter’s qualifications to vote,” including citizenship. Id.
§§ 14240, 15105, 15106. Finally, state law also penalizes numerous related
offenses, including fraud in voter registration and voting, id. §§ 18100, 18500,
18560, and assisting ineligible voters to vote, id. § 18561. See also id. § 18543
(criminalizing fraudulent or spurious mass voter challenges for improper purposes).
For those who are eligible to vote, California’s Constitution guarantees a right
to vote and “have that vote counted.” Cal. Const. art. 2, §§ 2, 2.5. State law further
affirms that Californians “have the right to cast a ballot if [they] are a valid
registered voter.” Cal. Elec. Code, § 2300(a)(1)(A); see also id. § 2000(a).
California sends each active registered voter a mail ballot. Cal. Elec. Code
§§ 3000.5, 3003, 3010; see also id. § 3102. After voting their ballot, a voter places
it in an envelope that is signed under penalty of perjury to establish the validity of
the vote. Id. §§ 3010(a)(2), 3011(a)(1)–(2). Once elections officials receive a mail
ballot, they compare the signature on the ballot envelope to the signature on file for

Congress exercised its Elections Clause authority to establish limited baseline
requirements for federal elections, with which California law conforms. These
requirements touch on the ability to cast a ballot, voter registration, voter roll
maintenance, and certain mail voting procedures.
Congress enacted the Voting Rights Act of 1965 to safeguard the right to vote.
In Section 11(a) of the Act, Congress provided that “[n]o person acting under color
of law shall fail or refuse to permit any person to vote who is entitled to vote under
[the Act] or is otherwise qualified to vote, or willfully fail or refuse to tabulate,
count, and report such person’s vote.” 52 U.S.C. § 10307(a). This provision
prohibits public officials from denying any qualified person the right to vote.
United States v. Post, 297 F. Supp. 46, 50 (W.D. La. 1969).
Congress enacted the National Voter Registration Act (“NVRA”) in 1993 to
reduce barriers to voter registration, protect the integrity of federal elections, and
improve the accuracy of voter registration rolls. The NVRA expressly recognizes
“method[s] of voter registration provided for under State law,” and builds atop that
foundation by requiring certain additional methods to be made available to those

seeking “to register to vote in elections for Federal office.” 52 U.S.C. § 20503(a).
Under the NVRA, States must inform applicants of the applicable voter eligibility
requirements and penalties associated with submitting false information on an
application. Id. § 20507(a)(5). The NVRA also requires States to accept and use

The NVRA provides a framework for the removal of voters from voter rolls,
ensuring that the process is fair and protects eligible voters. That framework
addresses removals at the voter’s request, or due to death, change of residence, or
the voter becoming ineligible under state law “by reason of criminal conviction or
mental incapacity.” 52 U.S.C. §§ 20507(a)(3)–(4), (d)(1). The NVRA requires
States to complete systematic removals from the official list of eligible voters no
later than 90 days before a federal primary or general election. Id.
§ 20507(c)(2)(A). This window of time is known as the “quiet period.” Overall, the
NVRA requires only that States “‘conduct a general program that makes a
reasonable effort to remove the names of ineligible voters from the official lists of
eligible voters by reason of’ a death or change in residence of the registrant.” Pub.
Int. Legal. Found., Inc. v. Nago, 174 F.4th 664, 667 (9th Cir. 2026), cert. petition
filed on other grounds No. 26-98 (quoting 52 U.S.C. § 20507(a)(4)). California has
enacted a comprehensive list maintenance program to implement the NVRA’s
requirements. See, e.g., Cal. Elec. Code §§ 2155, 2155.3, 2201, 2220, 2221–2227.
Congress enacted the Help America Vote Act (“HAVA”) in 2002 to help
upgrade the States’ voting infrastructure. Among other things, HAVA set standards
for applicants to verify their identity upon registration. See 52 U.S.C. § 21083.

II. FACTUAL BACKGROUND AND ALLEGATIONS
On July 16, 2026, Department of Homeland Security (“DHS”) Secretary
Markwayne Mullin sent a letter to Secretary Weber alleging that approximately
190,000 voters registered in California “may be” noncitizens, with almost no
further detail. Exhibit (“Exh.”) 1 to the Complaint (“Compl.”). Secretary Weber
responded expressing “serious doubts about the reliability of [DHS’s] data and
accuracy of [DHS’s] claims,” and asking for a detailed description of the
methodology utilized to identify the allegedly ineligible voters. Compl. Exh. 2.
On the afternoon of October 13, the U.S. Department of Justice (“DOJ”) sent a
letter to Secretary Weber informing her that DOJ provided a thumb drive to the Los
Angeles County Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk containing “a list of names the
Federal Government believes are likely noncitizens who were sent a . . . ballot” for
the 2026 General Election. Compl. Exh. 3. DOJ demanded that Secretary Weber
“review the list within 48 hours to determine whether any citizens are listed.” Id. If
her office could not “independently confirm citizenship” for any listed individuals,
the letter demanded that she instruct elections officials to (1) segregate and not
process or tabulate that individual’s mail ballot “until their citizenship status can be
determined” and (2) take immediate steps to prevent sending mail ballots to any
listed individuals who have not already received them “until [the federal
government] can confirm citizenship.” Id.
Plaintiff’s Complaint does not allege that Plaintiff provided the list directly to
State Defendants, the specific underlying methodology used to compile it, or proof
of noncitizenship for any individuals on the list. Instead, the Complaint vaguely
describes, for the first time, the “investigative process” by which DHS allegedly
compares information about voter rolls with “internal data to identify potential
matches between registered voters and possible noncitizens.” Compl. ¶¶ 33–46.
DHS allegedly utilizes unspecified “third-party software” to identify potential
matches. Id. ¶ 35. It also allegedly uses a “customary analytical platform” to

LEGAL STANDARD
A complaint must be dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction if a
plaintiff lacks standing to sue. Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1). The plaintiff bears the
burden of demonstrating standing “for each claim” and “each form of relief” that it
seeks. See DaimlerChrysler Corp. v. Cuno, 547 U.S. 332, 352 (2006) (internal
citations and quotations omitted). A complaint must also “state a claim to relief that
is plausible on its face,” or otherwise face dismissal under Federal Rule of Civil

Procedure 12(b)(6). Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly¸ 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007). A
court may consider the complaint, documents attached to and incorporated by

to dismiss into a motion for summary judgment.” United States v. Ritchie, 342 F.3d
903, 908 (9th Cir. 2003).
ARGUMENT
I. PLAINTIFF LACKS STANDING
“To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must show (i) that he suffered an
injury in fact that is concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent; (ii) that the
injury was likely caused by the defendant; and (iii) that the injury would likely be
redressed by judicial relief.” Pinkert v. Schwab Charitable Fund, 48 F.4th 1051,
1054 (9th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). At the pleading stage, a plaintiff must
“allege facts that, when accepted as true, show that [these elements] are satisfied.”
Id. Moreover, when a plaintiff claims standing based on “the threat of future injury”
it must allege sufficient facts to establish that “the threatened injury is certainly
impending.” Wright v. Serv. Emps. Int’l Union Loc. 503, 48 F.4th 1112, 1118 (9th
Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). Here, Plaintiff fails to establish injury in fact based
either on past or threatened future injury.
First, Plaintiff asserts that it has standing “to vindicate its sovereign interests,”
including its interest in “enforcing federal law.” Compl. ¶ 9; see also ¶¶ 55–56. In
United States v. California, however, a Central District court recently rejected the
theory that “the mere existence of a preempted state law creates a sovereign injury
to the federal government.” 824 F. Supp. 3d 1079, 1083 (C.D. Cal. 2026) (emphasis
in original). Here, Plaintiff’s claim to standing is even weaker because Plaintiff

Elections Clause Preemption. Plaintiff’s preemption claim must be
adjudicated under the Elections Clause. Where Congress has not exercised its
Elections Clause authority to displace state election law, the States retain the
“responsibility for the mechanics of congressional elections,” ITCA, 570 U.S. at 9
(quotations omitted), including as to “registration, supervision of voting, protection
of voters, prevention of fraud and corrupt practices, [and] counting of votes,”
Moore v. Harper (2023) 600 U.S. 1, 29 (quotation omitted). Both the Ninth Circuit
and the Supreme Court have recognized that it is the Elections Clause preemption
framework, not that of the Supremacy Clause, that governs in cases concerning the
rules for election administration. See ITCA, 570 U.S. at 13–15; Gonzalez v.
Arizona, 677 F.3d 383, 392–393 (9th Cir. 2012), aff’d sub nom. ITCA, 570 U.S. 1.
This is one such case, as Plaintiff contests and seeks relief from California’s rules
and conduct with respect to election administration. See, e.g., Compl. ¶¶ 5 (alleging
that “actions,” including sending mail ballots and tabulating them in accordance
with state and federal law, “are preempted”), 62–63 (seeking relief from same).
Under the Elections Clause preemption framework, congressional enactments
displace state election law only “so far as [Congress] . . . exercise[s]” its authority

“and no farther.” ITCA, 570 U.S. at 9 (citation omitted and emphasis added); see
also Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 384 (1879) (federal law preempts state
election law only “so far as the conflict extends”). “Because the power the
Elections Clause confers is none other than the power to pre-empt, the reasonable
assumption is that the statutory text accurately communicates the scope of
Congress’s pre-emptive intent.” ITCA, 570 U.S. at 14. The inquiry is
“straightforward”—as to the mechanics of elections, either Congress has exercised
its Elections Clause power to displace States’ rules, or it has not. Id. at 9; see, e.g.,
Watson v. Republican Nat’l Comm., 609 U.S. 672, 681–685 (2026) (finding that
federal statutes setting the day for federal elections do not preempt state laws
providing for counting ballots postmarked by election day but received later).
Here, Congress obviously has not. 18 U.S.C. § 611 criminalizes individual
noncitizen voting and does not purport to set any rules governing election
administration. Under these circumstances, it is the State’s constitutional
prerogative to enact and implement the rules about which Plaintiff complains,
including citizenship verification requirements set by Congress itself. See infra,
Sec. II.B. If Plaintiff would like to change California’s rules or “actions” pertaining
to election administration, the proper means of doing so is via congressional
enactment—not Executive initiative. See generally Georgia v. Meadows, 88 F.4th
1331, 1347 (11th Cir. 2023) (recognizing that the federal Executive has no
authority to “interfere[] with state election procedures based solely on [its] own
initiative”). Plaintiff ignores this outcome-determinative constitutional framework
entirely.

asserted injury” to voting rights against “the precise interests put forward by the
[government] as justifications for the burden imposed,” considering “the extent to
which those interests make it necessary to burden the [voters’] rights.” Anderson v.
Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, 789 (1983). A “regulation that imposes a severe burden
is subject to strict scrutiny.” Nader v. Brewer, 531 F.3d 1028, 1035 (9th Cir. 2008).
This balancing test confirms the unconstitutional nature of DOJ’s demands of
Defendants. The burden Plaintiff seeks to impose on duly registered voters is
severe. Unlike cases where courts have upheld routine, procedural rules designed to
manage registration or balloting, see, e.g., Crawford v. Marion Cnty. Elec. Bd., 553
U.S. 181, 202–203 (2008) (requirement to present photo ID when voting in person);
Ariz. Democratic Party, 18 F.4th at 1181 (election day deadline for curing
signatures), Plaintiff demanded that California sequester and refuse to tabulate
ballots cast by duly registered voters outside of any statutory procedure and in the
absence of any individualized evidence. These steps would unquestionably have a
devastating disenfranchising effect. Plaintiff has not articulated a standard by which
citizenship can be “determined” or “confirm[ed],” see Compl. Exh. C at 2–3, and it
outright concedes that it “is not practicable or feasible for the United States” to
carry out that confirmation process “before the 2026 election concludes,” id. ¶ 60.
Nor does the Complaint offer any factual basis to support a conclusion, or even an
inference, that Defendants could feasibly do so. Even construing all allegations in

C. 18 U.S.C. § 611 Does Not Authorize the Relief Plaintiff Seeks
Finally, Plaintiff’s claim fails for the independent reason that Plaintiff asks this
Court to issue a civil injunction based on alleged violations of 18 U.S.C. § 611, a
criminal statute. “As a general rule,” however, courts “are reluctant to issue
injunctions against the commission of a crime.” 11A Fed. Prac. & Proc. Civ. § 2942
& n.73 (3d ed.). This rule rests upon “the adequacy of the criminal remedy to
protect the plaintiff’s interests.” Id. The government may seek an injunction against
potential crime when a specific statute authorizes such relief. See, e.g., Beck v.
Prupis, 529 U.S. 494, 496 n.1 (2000). But Congress has not authorized injunctive
relief for 18 U.S.C. § 611. Congress has expressly authorized the Attorney General
to seek injunctive relief to prevent certain alleged criminal violations of other
election laws, see 52 U.S.C. §§ 10308(a)–(d), 20510(a), 20511, but 18 U.S.C. § 611
is not among them—an “omission[ ] [that] should be understood as exclusion[ ].”
Silvers v. Sony Pictures Ent., Inc., 402 F.3d 881, 885 (9th Cir. 2005) (en banc)
(citation omitted).

grant the motion to dismiss this case.
