TEMPORARY RESTRAINING ORDER
A. Reasons Why This Order Is Issued
1. Standard for Temporary Restraining Order
A party seeking preliminary relief must show that (1) it is likely to succeed on the
merits, (2) it is likely to suffer irreparable harm, and an injunction is both (3) equitable
and (4) in the public interest. NetChoice, LLC v. Bonta, 152 F.4th 1002, 1012 (9th Cir.
2025) (citing Winter v. Nat. Res. Def. Council, Inc., 555 U.S. 7 (2008)).
2. Plaintiff Is Likely to Prevail on the Merits
Under the Supremacy Clause, the “Constitution, and the Laws of the United States
which shall be made in Pursuance thereof . . . shall be the supreme Law of the Land.”
U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2; Arizona v. United States, 567 U.S. 387, 399 (2012) (“The
Supremacy Clause provides a clear rule that federal law” is supreme over state law).
Federal preemption principles ensure the constitutional supremacy of federal law. Under
those principles, contrary “state law must yield to the regulation of Congress within the
sphere of its delegated power.” Savage v. Jones, 225 U.S. 501, 533 (1912). And federal
law can preempt state law “[e]ven without an express provision for preemption.” Crosby
v. Nat’l Foreign Trade Council, 530 U.S. 363, 373 (2000). That includes where “state
law stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and
objectives of Congress,” id. (citations omitted) (cleaned up), or otherwise “directly
conflict[s]” with federal law, Kansas v. Garcia, 140 S. Ct. 791, 808 (2020) (Thomas, J.,
concurring). State action must “give way” when it conflicts with “a considered
judgment” embodied in federal law. Arizona, 567 U.S. at 399, 405.
Such conflicts can arise in different ways and through different forms. One
throughline, though, is that States cannot avoid preemption by facilitating a private party
to violate federal law. “It is . . . axiomatic that a state may not induce, encourage, or

promote private persons to accomplish what it is constitutionally forbidden to
accomplish.” Norwood v. Harrison, 413 U.S. 455, 465 (1973). For example, if a state
purported to subsidize federal crimes, that would conflict with the considered judgment
of Congress. See, e.g., Musta v. Mendota Heights Dental Center, 965 N.W.2d 312, 321-
27 & n.11 (Minn. 2021) (federal law preempted a requirement that an employer
subsidize the purchase of a federally prohibited drug), cert. denied, 142 S. Ct. 2834
(Mem.) (2022).
Preemption principles apply when state officials act in a manner that obstructs or
conflicts with federal law. “It has long been a settled principle that federal courts may
enjoin unconstitutional action by state officials.” Puerto Rico v. Branstad, 483 U.S. 219,
228 (1987) (emphasis added) (citing Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908)).
Accordingly, whether state and local officials “act[] through . . . procedures rather than a
court order or a general law . . . is irrelevant to [the] analysis.” Golden State Transit
Corp. v. City of Los Angeles, 475 U.S. 608, 614 n.5 (1986). Preemption does not turn on
the form of state action. It “turns on the actual content of [a state’s] policy and its real
effect on federal rights.” Livadas v. Bradshaw, 512 U.S. 107, 119 (1994) (citing and
adopting Livadas v. Aubry, 987 F.2d 552, 561 (9th Cir. 1991) (Kozinski, J., dissenting)
(“The point is, the Commissioner is applying state law and his own policy in a way that
impinges on protected federal rights.”).
Here, federal law makes it “unlawful for any alien to vote” in federal elections. 18
U.S.C. § 611(a). Accordingly, Defendants cannot “induce, encourage, or promote”—let
alone knowingly facilitate—noncitizen voting for a candidate who is running for a seat
in the U.S. Senate or the U.S. House of Representatives when state election officials
have notice of specific individuals who are likely to be noncitizens. Norwood, 413 U.S.
at 465; cf. City of Richmond v. J.A. Croson Co., 488 U.S. 469, 492 (1989) (applying
Norwood’s “induce, encourage, or promote” principle to scenario where a local
government “had essentially become a ‘passive participant’ in a system of racial
exclusion” (emphasis added)); Mut. Pharm. Co. v. Bartlett, 570 U.S. 472, 475 (2013)

(explaining that state actions “that require a private party to violate federal law are pre-

Yet that is precisely what Defendants appear to be doing—and their actions
conflict with federal law. Federal law preempts Defendants’ facilitation of unlawful
voting by likely noncitizens in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 611. By facilitating violations of
the statute, Defendants’ actions conflict with the “considered judgment” the statute
expresses. Arizona, 567 U.S. at 399, 405. If Defendants were just seeking to “induce,
encourage, or promote” violations of the statute, that would be enough to trigger federal
preemption. Norwood, 413 U.S. at 465. But Defendants are not just supporting conduct
that federal law prohibits (such as when a state purports to subsidize illegal activity);
they appear to be directly facilitating violations of that federal law by feigning ignorance
of noncitizen votes. It “is simply implausible that Congress would have” criminalized
voting by noncitizens “if it had been willing to” let States “compromise [the]
effectiveness” of that law by participating in its violation. Crosby, 530 U.S. at 376. And
Defendants’ systematic defiance of federal law is all the more egregious because it
undermines the federal rights of United States citizens to vote in an election in which
only eligible voters participate. “Voters who fear their legitimate votes will be
outweighed by fraudulent ones will feel disenfranchised.” Purcell v. Gonzalez, 549 U.S.
1, 4 (2006).
The United States is likely to prevail on the merits of its claim that Defendants’
facilitation of unlawful voting by sending vote-by-mail ballots to likely noncitizens and
counting ballots that are returned—after knowing that noncitizens are likely registered to
vote—is preempted as applied to the individuals the Federal Government has positively
identified for Defendants.
3. Plaintiff Prevails on Factors Two, Three, and Four
a. The United States Will Suffer Irreparable Harm Absent a TRO
For three reasons, the United States will suffer irreparable harm absent injunctive
relief. First, when state action is “preempted, a finding with regard to likelihood of

success fulfills the remaining [preliminary injunction] requirements.” Tex. Midstream
Gas Servs., LLC v. City of Grand Prairie, 608 F.3d 200, 206 (5th Cir. 2010). This is
because irreparable harm necessarily results from the operation of preempted state
action. See California, 173 F.4th at 1069 (granting injunction pending appeal because
violation of Supremacy Clause was irreparable); Valle del Sol v. Whiting, 732 F.3d at
1029 (finding irreparable harm based on a Supremacy Clause violation); United States
v. Arizona, 641 F.3d 339, 366 (9th Cir. 2012) (same), aff’d in part, rev’d on other
grounds, 567 U.S. 387 (2012). Put differently, the United States suffers a “per se
irreparable harm” when state actions violate the Supremacy Clause. United States v.
Texas, 557 F.Supp.3d 810, 821 (W.D. Tex. 2021) (collecting cases); Daimler, 2025 WL
3049944, at *17 (same).
Second, the “inability to enforce its duly enacted [law] clearly inflicts irreparable
harm on” the United States. Abbott v. Perez, 585 U.S. 579, 602 n.17 (2018); see also
Maryland v. King, 567 U.S. 1301, 1303 (2012) (Roberts, C.J., in chambers) (same). The
Supreme Court has recently and repeatedly found that the Federal Government would be
irreparably harmed if it could not enforce federal law. See, e.g., Dep’t of Homeland Sec.
v. D.V.D., 145 S. Ct. 2153 (Mem.) (2025); Noem v. Doe, 145 S. Ct. 1524 (Mem.) (2025);
Noem v. Nat’l TPS All., 145 S. Ct. 2728 (Mem.) (2025); see also Hollingsworth v. Perry,
558 U.S. 183, 190 (2010) (per curiam) (must show irreparable harm to obtain a stay).
Because Defendants are frustrating the United States’ ability to effectively enforce 18
U.S.C. § 611—which is a priority—the United States would suffer irreparably absent an
injunction. Cf. Trump v. CASA, Inc., 606 U.S. 831, 859–60 (2025) (recognizing that the
Federal Government “will suffer irreparable harm” if it is prevented from “enforcing its
policies”).
Finally, our constitutional system is irreparably injured when unlawful votes cancel
out lawful votes. Unlawful voting undermines public faith in elections and the legitimacy
of those who represent the public. And eroded trust in our democracy is irreparable.

b. The Balance of Equities and the Public Interest Weigh in the
United States’ Favor
This action seeks to protect the interests of the United States, so the burdens that
will result without injunctive relief are directly tied to the public benefits that will be
protected. See Nken, 556 U.S. 435. That is all the more true given the longstanding
“recognition that preventing a violation of the Supremacy Clause serves the public
interest.” California, 921 F.3d at 893–94. Indeed, the Ninth Circuit has long held that
“plaintiffs who “establish[] a likelihood that [a] policy violates the U.S.
Constitution . . . have also established that both the public interest and the balance of the
equities favor a preliminary injunction.” California, 173 F.4th at 1069 (quoting Ariz.
Dream Act Coal. v. Brewer, 757 F.3d 1053, 1069 (9th Cir. 2014)).
Moreover, injunctive relief will preserve the United States’ sovereign authority.
“Frustration of federal statutes and prerogatives are not in the public interest[.]” Alabama,
691 F.3d at 1301; United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Coop., 532 U.S. 483, 496–
98 (2001) (courts of equity must respect Congress’s policy choices as set forth in a statute);
Am. Trucking Associations, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles, 559 F.3d 1046, 1060 (9th Cir.
2009) (similar). Because the challenged conduct here frustrates the purpose of 18 U.S.C.
§ 611 to prevent noncitizen voting, allowing Defendants to proceed would not be in the
public interest. By contrast, an injunction will not burden Defendants, as it does not have
the authority to permit noncitizen voting. So an injunction would maintain that status quo.
On the other side, Defendants have expressed concern about administering the
United States’ request to set aside vote-by-mail ballots cast by likely noncitizens. Those
concerns cannot stand as an impediment to administering an election free from unlawful
voting. And California, having routinely administered elections since its founding in 1850,
is well-equipped to implement the United States’ requested relief. In fact, the very relief
sought here—ballot segregation—is provided for in California’s elections regulations.
“Resolution of challenged ballots,” California regulations state, “shall take place in a
segregated area within the recount location[.]” Cal. Code Regs. tit. 2, § 20363. California

has also known about noncitizen registration since July. See Compl. Ex. A.
The United States has offered to assist California to confirm the citizenship of the

election. And the United States protects its sovereignty by enforcing Congress’ prohibition
against noncitizens voting.
B. Order
TO DEFENDANTS STATE OF CALIFORNIA, SHIRLEY WEBER in her
official capacity as California Secretary of State, and DEAN C. LOGAN, in his official
capacity as the County of Los Angeles Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk:
PENDING HEARING on the below Order to Show Cause, you and all those in
active concert or participation with you ARE HEREBY ENJOINED to segregate all
vote-by-mail ballots received from individuals on the list provided to DEAN C.
LOGAN. Next, you are ENJOINED to confirm the citizenship of the person who sent a
segregated vote-by-mail ballot. When confirming a person’s citizenship, you shall not
rely solely on the person’s registration-related attestation, and you may collaborate with
Plaintiff UNITED STATES. If DEFENDANTS determine that the individual is not a
U.S. citizen, DEFENDANTS are HEREBY RESTRAINED from tabulating that
person’s vote and ENJOINED to take all steps necessary to ensure the vote-by-mail
ballot is not tabulated, counted, and certified as valid. If the DEFENDANTS determine
