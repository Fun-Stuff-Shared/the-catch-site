MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES
I. INTRODUCTION
Los Angeles County is administering the November 3, 2026 General
Election the same way it administers every election: according to applicable
laws, regulations, and practices – that includes applicable federal law California
law, California Code of Regulations and a clear aim—to ensure that every
eligible voter has access to voting, that the election is conducted securely with
integrity, and that every valid ballot submitted is counted an included in the
certified returns. The County has not changed. The Federal Government’s
posture has. Weeks before Election Day, the United States asks this Court to
intrude on the administration of an election. But the Constitution assigns the
administration of elections to the States and their localities, and the federal
government may enter that domain only where Congress has authorized it and
only where it has suffered a cognizable injury. Neither is true here. The
Complaint should be dismissed.
A. The County’s Election Procedures
Defendant Dean C. Logan is sued in his official capacity. (Compl. ¶ 16.)
The County’s administration of the November 3, 2026 General Election is the
largest and most complex local election operation in the nation, serving 88 cities,
55 general and special districts, 140 unincorporated areas, and 19 languages.
(Declaration of Monica Flores in Support of the County’s Opposition, ECF No.
32-4 ¶ 5.) The County serves approximately 5.9 million registered voters, with
5,000 voting precincts for countywide elections. (Id.)
To register to vote, under state law, a person “may prove he or she is a
citizen by his or her certification under penalty of perjury on the affidavit of
registration.” (Cal. Elec. Code § 2111; see also 52 U.S.C. § 20508(b)(2) (federal
mail voter registration form must include “an attestation that the applicant meets
each such requirement,” including citizenship, and “require[] the signature of the
applicant, under penalty of perjury”).) Consistent with California and federal law

B. DHS’s July 2026 Letter to the Secretary of State
On July 16, 2026, Department of Homeland Security (“DHS”) Secretary
Markwayne Mullin wrote to Secretary of State Shirley N. Weber asserting that a
“[p]reliminary review” of California’s publicly available voter data revealed
“there may be as many as 190,832 non-citizens registered to vote in California.”
(Compl. Ex. A.) On July 23, 2026, Secretary Weber responded that she had
“serious doubts about the reliability of [DHS’s] data,” noting that her office had
never shared the statewide voter registration list with DHS, and requested a
“detailed description of the methodology utilized by DHS to identify these
allegedly ineligible voters, including . . . all data sources, software, and matching
criteria,” and whether artificial intelligence was used. (Compl. Ex. B.) Secretary
Weber also advised that her office would comply with the NVRA’s prohibition
on systematic cancellation of registrations within 90 days of a federal election—a
period that began on August 5, 2026. (Id.) DHS did not provide the requested
methodology, any list of such alleged records, or otherwise respond to Secretary
Weber.

II. LEGAL STANDARDS
Rule 12(b)(1). Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction. Kokkonen
v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am., 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994). The plaintiff bears
the burden of establishing standing. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 561
(1992). A Rule 12(b)(1) challenge may be facial or factual. Safe Air for
Everyone v. Meyer, 373 F.3d 1035, 1039 (9th Cir. 2004). In a facial attack, the
movant asserts that the allegations of the complaint are insufficient on their face
to invoke federal jurisdiction. Id. In a factual attack, the court may review
evidence beyond the complaint and need not presume the truthfulness of the
plaintiff’s allegations. Id.; see also Leite v. Crane Co., 749 F.3d 1117, 1121–22
(9th Cir. 2014). The County’s motion presents both.
Rule 12(b)(6). To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain
sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to “state a claim to relief that is
plausible on its face.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell
Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). “Threadbare recitals of the
elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory statements, do not
suffice,” and the Court is “not bound to accept as true a legal conclusion
couched as a factual allegation.” Id. The Court may take judicial notice of
matters of public record and may consider documents incorporated by reference
into the complaint without converting the motion into one for summary
judgment. Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc., 899 F.3d 988, 998 (9th Cir.
2018). When a complaint lacks a cognizable legal theory or sufficient facts to
support a cognizable legal theory, dismissal is appropriate. Mendiondo v.
Centinela Hosp. Med. Ctr., 521 F.3d 1097, 1104 (9th Cir. 2008); see also
Balistreri v. Pacifica Police Dep’t, 901 F.2d 696, 699 (9th Cir. 1988). Where
amendment would be futile, dismissal without leave to amend is proper. See
Watison v. Carter, 668 F.3d 1108, 1117 (9th Cir. 2012).

The Executive invokes a federal interest that no one disputes: noncitizens
may not vote in federal elections. California law says the same thing. But an
interest in enforcing the law is not an injury, and a federal prohibition does not
dispense with Article III. The Executive, like any other plaintiff, must identify a
concrete injury, actual or imminent, fairly traceable to the County’s conduct and
likely to be redressed by the requested relief. Lujan, 504 U.S. at 560–61. The
Complaint identifies none.
The Executive’s theory rests on a chain of possibilities: that registered
voters identified through federal database matching are noncitizens; that some
will cast ballots; that the County will count those ballots; and that doing so will
impair a federal interest. (Compl. ¶¶ 58–60.) But the Complaint does not allege
that any identified voter has cast an unlawful ballot, much less that the County
has counted one. (See Compl. ¶¶ 62–63.) Nor does it identify any federal
investigation or prosecution that the County has obstructed; what the Executive
principally alleges is that federal authorities cannot complete their own
investigations before the election concludes. (Compl. ¶ 60.) That is not an injury
attributable to the County.
A. The Complaint Alleges No Actual or Certainly Impending Injury
Article III requires an injury that is concrete and particularized and actual
or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical. Lujan, 504 U.S. at 560. When a
plaintiff seeks prospective relief against anticipated conduct, the threatened
injury must be “certainly impending,” or there must be a substantial risk that it
will occur. Clapper v. Amnesty International USA, 568 U.S. 398, 409, 414 n.5
(2013). A speculative chain of future events will not suffice. Id. at 410–14.
The Complaint identifies no concrete, particularized injury to the
Executive. It offers four theories: an abstract sovereign interest in preemption

B. The Claim Is Not Ripe, and the Alleged Injury Rests on a
Speculative Chain of Contingencies
The Complaint asks the Court to act now against an injury that depends on
events that have not occurred and that the Executive itself cannot say will occur.
Ripeness, which in its constitutional dimension “coincides squarely with
standing’s injury in fact prong,” bars claims that rest “upon contingent future
events that may not occur as anticipated, or indeed may not occur at all.”
Thomas v. Anchorage Equal Rts. Comm’n, 220 F.3d 1134, 1138–39 (9th Cir.
2000) (en banc); Texas v. United States, 523 U.S. 296, 300 (1998). A threatened
injury must be “certainly impending” or present a “substantial risk”;
“[a]llegations of possible future injury are not sufficient.” Clapper, 568 U.S. at
409, 414 n.5; Tex. Voters All., 495 F. Supp. 3d at 451–52.
The Executive’s theory depends on a chain of contingencies as explained
above. The Complaint pleads none of these links. It alleges only that noncitizens
“might be registered” (Compl. ¶ 59), describes the listed persons as “likely”
noncitizens (id. ¶¶ 4, 47) and the list as the input to “further investigation” (id. ¶¶
38–43), and it concedes that investigation cannot be completed before the
election ends (id. ¶ 60).

B. Preemption Requires a Conflict with Federal Law, Not with
Federal Enforcement Preferences, and No Such Conflict Exists
Here
Preemption serves one purpose: it “provides a rule of decision” when state
and federal law conflict. Murphy, 584 U.S. at 477. The necessary predicate is a
conflict between federal law and a state-law requirement. Id.; see Kansas v.
Garcia, 589 U.S. 191, 202 (2020) (“There is no federal preemption in vacuo . . .
.”) (quoting Puerto Rico Dep’t of Consumer Affs. v. Isla Petroleum Corp., 485
U.S. 495, 503 (1988)). No conflict, no preemption.
Nor can preemption rest on some “brooding federal interest” untethered to
a federal legal requirement. Virginia Uranium, Inc. v. Warren, 587 U.S. 761, 767
(2019) (plurality opinion). Federal law may preempt state restrictions imposed
through executive or administrative action, not just legislation. See Arizona
Dream Act Coal. v. Brewer, 855 F.3d 957, 971 (9th Cir. 2017) (holding that a
governor’s executive order and resulting agency policy, not a statute,
“encroache[d] on the exclusive federal authority to create immigration
classifications and so is displaced by the [Immigration and Nationality Act]”).
But that is not this case. The Executive identifies no state-law requirement that
conflicts with Section 611. (Compl. ¶¶ 58–60.) Instead, it asks the Court to treat
the County’s refusal to undertake additional citizenship verification at the
Executive’s request as preempted—even though Section 611 regulates individual
voters, not the election officials who administer elections. (Id. ¶¶ 17–19, 58–60.)
