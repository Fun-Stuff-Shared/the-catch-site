INTRODUCTION
Pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 62(d), the United States moves for an
injunction pending appeal of this Court’s constructive denial of the United States’ emer-
gency application for preliminary injunctive relief. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 62(d); see A.A.R.P.
v. Trump, 605 U.S. 91, 94 (2025) (“A district court's inaction in the face of extreme ur-
gency and a high risk of ‘serious, perhaps irreparable,’ consequences may have the effect
of refusing an injunction” and concluding that inaction for “14 hours and 28
minutes . . . had the practical effect of refusing an injunction.” (citations omitted)).
The United States has appealed this Court’s effective denial of its application for an
injunction requiring Defendants to (1) set aside vote-by-mail ballots from persons identi-
fied by the Federal Government likely to be noncitizens and (2) to independently—or in
collaboration with the Federal Government—verify their citizenship status before tabulat-
ing their vote-by-mail ballot. See ECF Nos. 22, 62. The requested injunction is warranted
because the United States is likely to succeed on appeal, and an injunction would prevent
further irreparable harm to the United States and the general public while the United
States’ appeal proceeds.
The United States brings this application on an ex parte basis because every minute
of processing that occurs without segregating the ballots of suspected non-eligible voters
causes untraceable disenfranchisement and dilution of the votes of United States citizens.
Once illegally cast ballots are counted and commingled with lawful votes, the resulting
harm is impossible to remedy. The urgency is heightened because of the fundamental right
to vote and to have one’s lawful vote counted without dilution.
Defendant Dean Logan admits in his opposition to the United States’ application
for temporary restraining order, “[s]ince the County began its [Vote By Mail (“VBM”)]
ballot processing operations on October 11, 2026, the County has processed more than
331,000 VBM ballots to date and anticipates much higher volumes of VBM ballots to be
received as Election Day approaches (citation omitted). Regardless of voting method,

ARGUMENT
“A plaintiff seeking a preliminary injunction must establish that [it] is likely to suc-
ceed on the merits, that [it] is likely to suffer irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary
relief, that the balance of equities tips in [its] favor, and that an injunction is in the public
interest.” Winter v. NRDC, 555 U.S. 7, 20 (2008). “The standard for evaluating an injunc-
tion pending appeal is similar to that employed by district courts in deciding whether to
grant a preliminary injunction.” Feldman v. Arizona Sec’y of State’s Off., 843 F.3d 366,
367 (9th Cir. 2016). All of those factors support the United States and warrant injunctive
relief here.
A. The Federal Government is likely to prevail on the merits.
1. Under the Supremacy Clause, the “Constitution, and the Laws of the United
States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof . . . shall be the supreme Law of the
Land[.]” U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2; Arizona v. United States, 567 U.S. 387, 399 (2012)
(“The Supremacy Clause provides a clear rule that federal law” is supreme over state law).
Federal preemption principles ensure the constitutional supremacy of federal law. Under
those principles, contrary “state law must yield to the regulation of Congress within the
sphere of its delegated power.” Savage v. Jones, 225 U.S. 501, 533 (1912). And federal
law can preempt state law “[e]ven without an express provision for preemption.” Crosby
v. Nat’l Foreign Trade Council, 530 U.S. 363, 372 (2000). That includes where “state law
stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objec-
tives of Congress,” id. at 373 (citations omitted) (cleaned up), or otherwise “directly con-
flict[s]” with federal law, Kansas v. Garcia, 140 S. Ct. 791, 808 (2020) (Thomas, J.,
9
Case 2:26-cv-12025-DOC-ADS Document 76-1 Filed 10/23/26 Page 14 of 22 Page
ID #:1253
concurring). State action must “give way” when it conflicts with “a considered judgment”
embodied in federal law. Arizona, 567 U.S. at 399, 405.
States cannot avoid preemption by facilitating a private party to violate federal law.
It is “axiomatic that a state may not induce, encourage or promote private persons to ac-
complish what it is constitutionally forbidden to accomplish.” Norwood, 413 U.S. at 465.
For example, if a state purported to subsidize federal crimes, that would conflict with the
considered judgment of Congress. See, e.g., Musta v. Mendota Heights Dental Ctr., 965
N.W.2d 312, 321-27 & n.11 (Minn. 2021) (federal law preempted a requirement that an
employer subsidize the purchase of a federally prohibited drug), cert denied, 142 S. Ct.
2834 (Mem.) (2022).
When evaluating the obstacle that state action poses to federal aims, courts “ex-
amin[e] the federal statute as a whole and identify[] its purpose and intended effects[.]”
Crosby, 530 U.S. at 373. To illustrate, when a State “authorizes [persons] to engage in
conduct that the federal [law] forbids, it ‘stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and
execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.’” Mich. Canners and Freezers
Ass’n, Inc. v. Agricultural Marketing and Bargaining Bd., 467 U.S. 461, 478 (1984) (quot-
ing Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941)). At bottom, obstacle preemption exists
wherever a state “undermines the intended purpose and natural effect” of a federal statute.
Crosby, 530 U.S. at 373 (internal quotation marks omitted).
These preemption principles apply when state officials act in a manner that obstructs
or conflicts with federal law. “It has long been a settled principle that federal courts may
enjoin unconstitutional action by state officials.” Puerto Rico v. Branstad, 483 U.S. 219,
228 (1987) (emphasis added) (citing Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908)). Accordingly,
whether state and local officials “act[] through . . . procedures rather than a court order or
a general law . . . is irrelevant to [the] analysis.” Golden State Transit Corp. v. City of Los
Angeles, 475 U.S. 608, 614 n.5 (1986). Preemption does not turn on the form of state
action. It “turns on the actual content of [a state’s] policy and its real effect on federal
10
Case 2:26-cv-12025-DOC-ADS Document 76-1 Filed 10/23/26 Page 15 of 22 Page
ID #:1254
rights.” Livadas v. Bradshaw, 512 U.S. 107, 119 (1994) (citing and adopting Livadas v.
Aubry, 987 F.2d 552, 561 (9th Cir. 1991) (Kozinski, J., dissenting) (“The point is, the
Commissioner is applying state law and his own policy in a way that impinges on protected
federal rights.”)).
Courts have thus held that federal law preempts a wide range of conflicting state
actions. Where state officials operating a state university had a “policy” of “preferential
treatment” regarding “in-state status” that the Supreme Court deemed inconsistent with
federal immigration law, that “policy” itself “violate[d] the Supremacy Clause.” Moreno,
458 U.S. at 17. Preemption likewise applied when state officials acted to “encourag[e]
racial segregation” in violation of the Equal Protection Clause. United States v. City of
Jackson, 318 F.2d 1, 3–4 (5th Cir. 1963). Or when California itself sought to pursue civil
litigation in state court to set emissions standards in violation of the Clean Air Act. See
Daimler Truck N. Am. LLC v. California Air Res. Bd., 2025 WL 3049944, at *17–*18
(E.D. Cal. Oct. 31, 2025). A “conflict in technique can be fully as disruptive to the system
Congress enacted as [a] conflict in overt policy” written in a statute or regulation. Arizona,
567 U.S. at 406. After all, Ex parte Young, through which litigants there sought to enjoin
unconstitutional state action, does not focus on the validity of state law; it focuses on
whether “a state official who enforces that law ‘comes into conflict with the superior au-
thority of [the] Constitution.’” Virginia Off. for Prot. & Advocacy v. Stewart, 563 U.S.
247, 254 (2011) (quoting Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. at 159-60). That is the case here.
a. Federal law makes it “unlawful for any alien to vote” in federal elections. 18
U.S.C. § 611(a). Accordingly, California cannot “induce, encourage or promote”—let
alone knowingly facilitate—noncitizen voting for a candidate who is running for a seat in
the U.S. Senate or the U.S. House of Representatives when state election officials have
notice of specific individuals who are likely to be noncitizens. Norwood, 413 U.S. at 465;
cf. City of Richmond v. J.A. Croson Co., 488 U.S. 469, 492 (1989) (applying Norwood’s
“induce, encourage or promote” principle to scenario where a local government “had

McLoughlin Decl. ¶ 25, ECF No. 62-3. The officials even have immediate access to the
names of those potential voters. But California is now poised to tabulate the votes of per-

Federal law preempts California’s actions here. By facilitating violations of the stat-
ute, California’s actions conflict with the “considered judgment” the statute expresses.
Arizona, 567 U.S. at 399, 405. If California was just seeking to “induce, encourage or
promote” violations of the statute, that would be enough to trigger federal preemption.
Norwood, 413 U.S. at 465. But California is not just supporting conduct that Federal law
prohibits (such as when a state purports to subsidize illegal activity); it is directly facili-
tating violations of that Federal law by knowingly ignoring likely noncitizen votes. It “is
simply implausible that Congress would have” criminalized voting by noncitizens “if it
had been willing to” let States “compromise [the] effectiveness” of that law by participat-
ing in its violation. Crosby, 530 U.S. at 376. And California’s systematic defiance of Fed-
eral law is all the more egregious because it undermines the Federal rights of United States
citizens to vote in an election in which only eligible voters participate. “Voters who fear
their legitimate votes will be outweighed by fraudulent ones will feel disenfranchised.”
Purcell v. Gonzalez, 549 U.S. 1, 4 (2006).
b. California’s actions also frustrate the objectives and purposes of 18 U.S.C.
§ 611(a). Congress’s objectives and purposes are clear: only citizens vote in federal elec-
tions. Yet California is knowingly facilitating voting by likely noncitizens, and it is set to
tabulate their votes without even attempting to verify their citizenship status when faced
with credible evidence. If merely “authoriz[ing]” conduct that federal law prohibits can
undermine “the full purposes and objectives of Congress[,]” Michigan Canners, 467 U.S.
at 478, it follows that knowingly facilitating such conduct triggers obstacle preemption.
California’s actions pose an obstacle to the United States’ ability to implement Con-
gress’s purposes and objectives by enforcing 18 U.S.C. § 611(a). The President has made
clear that investigating and prosecuting violations of the statute are a major priority—
including by expressly directing the Attorney General to “take all lawful steps to deter and
address noncompliance with . . . 18 U.S.C. 611(a).” Exec. Order, Ensuring Citizenship
Verification and Integrity in Federal Elections § 5 (Mar. 31, 2026). The Department of

By facilitating tens of thousands of votes from likely noncitizens, California is ob-

Federal Government to prosecute thousands, let alone tens of thousands, of individuals
that two federal investigative offices have identified as likely noncitizens. And while a

to the United States’ law enforcement efforts.
Accordingly, California’s facilitation of unlawful voting by sending vote-by-mail
ballots to likely noncitizens and counting ballots that are returned—after knowing that
these noncitizens are likely registered to vote—is preempted as applied to the individuals
the Federal Government has identified for California.
B. The other injunction factors strongly favor the government.
The United States is likely to succeed on the merits and suffer irreparable harm.
Critically, the public interest and balance of the equities overwhelmingly support an in-
junction requiring Defendants to segregate ballots of individuals known to the Federal
Government and Defendants to be highly likely to be ineligible to vote. Such an extraor-
dinary remedy is necessary to preserve the status quo, protect the integrity of our elections,
and prevent potentially tens of thousands of violations of Federal criminal law.
Irreparable harm necessarily results from the enforcement of a preempted state law.
See New Orleans Pub. Serv., Inc. v. Council of the City of New Orleans, 491 U.S. 350,
366–67 (1989) (irreparable injury may be established “by a showing that the challenged

The harm here is also quintessentially irreparable because California’s mishandling
of ballots cannot be undone. Under California law, election officials open ballots and then
separate for all time the ballot from the envelope. Once this occurs, it cannot be undone.
There is no further way for officials to track or trace ballots cast by individuals later de-
termined to be ineligible to vote. Nor is there any way for Federal law enforcement to trace
these as part of criminal investigations. As we write, California election officials are al-
ready tabulating votes. The only way to preserve the eroding status quo is for an injunction
pending appeal to require segregating the ballots Federal officials have identified as asso-
ciated with ineligible voters. Otherwise, what is done can never be undone.
The balance of equities and the public interest likewise favor the United States. Be-
cause the challenged laws are invalid under the Supremacy Clause, they are not in the
public interest. Am. Trucking Ass’ns, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles, 559 F.3d 1046, 1060 (9th
