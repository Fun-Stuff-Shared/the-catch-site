I.     BACKGROUND

       North Carolina permits voting in myriad ways, including by

absentee ballot upon request.                N.C. Gen. Stat. § 163-226.                  A

qualified voter may request an absentee ballot for any reason or

no reason at all.         N.C. Gen. Stat. § 163-230.1.              The county board

must then mail the voter certain items, including an absentee ballot

and container-return envelope, which contain specific instructions

for    the   voter      and   for    which       there   are     specific    statutory

requirements.      N.C. Gen. Stat. § 163-230.1(a)(1)-(4).

       The county board is required to print an application on the

outside      of   the    container-return           envelope      that    includes       a

certification by the absentee voter – by signature – that he or she

voted the ballot enclosed in the container-return envelope, as well

as a space for the names, signatures, and addresses of two witnesses

or one notary public who witnessed the voter casting the ballot

enclosed in the container-return envelope.                     Id. § 163-229(b)(1),

(b)(3); 163-231(a)(6).              After marking the absentee ballot, the

voter must submit it to the county board in the container-return

envelope.     Id. § 163-231(b).

       North Carolina statutory law sets out specific requirements

as to the absentee ballot, including the following:

       •   The voter must place his or her “folded ballots in the

           container-return envelope and securely seal it, or have

           this done in the voter’s presence.”                 Id. § 163-231(a)(3).

II.    ANALYSIS

       A.   Standing

       The State Board argues that Plaintiffs lack standing.             “No

lawsuit may proceed in federal court unless the party seeking relief

has Article III standing.” Carolina Youth Action Project v. Wilson,

60 F.4th 770, 778 (4th Cir. 2023).      “This showing is particularly

important when political organizations try to vindicate the rights

of individual voters mere weeks before a national election[.]”

Republican Nat’l Comm. v. N.C. State Bd. of Elections, 120 F.4th

390, 408 (Diaz, C.J., concurring).      Because standing questions the

court’s authority to act, the court addresses it first.

       To have standing, a plaintiff must show: (1) he has suffered

an “injury in fact,” (2) the injury is “fairly . . . trace[able]

to the challenged action of the defendant,” and (3) it is “likely”

that “the injury will be ‘redressed by a favorable decision.’”

Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992) (alteration

and omission in original) (citations and internal quotation marks

omitted) (quoting Simon v. E. Ky. Welfare Rights Org., 426 U.S. 26,

27, 38 (1976)). In multi-plaintiff cases, “[a]t least one plaintiff

must have standing to seek each form of relief requested in the

complaint.”    Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates, Inc., 581 U.S. 433,

                                   14

consider whether other plaintiffs have standing to seek that same

form of relief.      Carolina Youth Action, 60 F.4th at 778 (citing

Town of Chester, 581 U.S. at 439.

       The three elements of standing will be addressed in turn.

            1.    Injury-in-Fact

     The injury in fact “requirement ensures that plaintiffs have

a ‘personal stake in the outcome of the controversy.’”                   Kenny v.

Wilson, 885 F.3d 280, 287 (4th Cir. 2018) (quoting Warth v. Seldin,

422 U.S. 490, 498 (1975)).       An injury in fact is “‘an invasion of

a legally protected interest’ that is ‘concrete and particularized’

and ‘actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.’” Spokeo,

Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330, 339, (2016) (quoting Lujan, 504 U.S.

at 560).      “An allegation of future injury may suffice if the

threatened injury is certainly impending, or there is a substantial

risk that the harm will occur.”      Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus,

573 U.S. 149, 158 (2014) (citation and internal quotation marks

omitted).     When plaintiffs seek prospective relief, they must

establish an ongoing or imminent injury in fact.                        O’Shea v.

Littleton, 414 U.S. 488, 495–96 (1974).

     An     organizational     plaintiff   can      satisfy       the    standing

requirements in two ways: either injury in its own right, or injury

        The court must determine whether the burdens on voters’ rights

are severe, minimal, or “in between these two extremes.”                     Obama for

Am. v. Husted, 697 F.3d 423, 429 (6th Cir. 2012).                             Election

regulations that impose a “severe” burden are subject to strict

scrutiny.         Pisano v. Strach, 743 F.3d 927, 933 (4th Cir. 2014).

Such severe burdens are generally “invidiously” discriminatory by

virtue       of   being     “irrelevant    to     the   voter’s     qualifications.”

Crawford, 553 U.S. at 189 (Stevens, J., announcing the judgment of

the court) (citing Harper v. Va. Bd. of Elections, 383 U.S. 663,

666-67 (1966)).           Generally, strict scrutiny “is the standard for

cases where ‘the State totally denied the electoral franchise to a

particular class of residents, and there was no way in which the

members of that class could have made themselves eligible to vote.’”

Mays v. LaRose, 951 F.3d 775, 786 (6th Cir. 2020) (quoting Rosario,

410 U.S. at 758).

        By    contrast,      “‘evenhanded       restrictions      that   protect    the

integrity and reliability of the electoral process itself’ are not

invidious and satisfy the standard set forth in Harper.”                    Crawford,

553 U.S. at 189-90 (Stevens, J.) (quoting Anderson, 460 U.S. at 788

n.9).        In such cases where the burden is “modest,” “election

laws . . . will        be    upheld   if    the    state    can    ‘articulate’     its

‘important regulatory interests.’”                Buscemi v. Bell, 964 F.3d 252,

263 (4th Cir. 2020) (quoting Libertarian Party of Va. v. Alcorn,

        There is no “litmus test for measuring the severity of a burden

that state law imposes on . . . an individual voter, or a discrete

court must consider both the “character and magnitude” of the

Anderson, 460 U.S. at 789), and then “make the ‘hard judgment’ that

our adversary system demands,” Crawford, 553 U.S. at 190 (Stevens,

all affected voters, “within the landscape of all opportunities

that [the State] provides to vote.”                Mays, 951 F.3d at 784-85; see

also Crawford, 553 U.S. at 198-99 (Stevens, J.) (reviewing burden

on “most voters” where photo ID law applied to in-person voters);

Tully v. Okeson, 977 F.3d 608, 617 (7th Cir. 2020) (reviewing burden

Burdick, 504 U.S. at 434 (quoting Norman v. Reed, 502 U.S. 279, 289

(1992)).      Non-severe burdens, by contrast, are subject to Anderson–

Burdick’s more flexible balancing standard, under which this court

must weigh “the character and magnitude of the asserted injury to

the rights protected by the First and Fourteenth Amendments that

III. CONCLUSION

     For   the    reasons   set     forth    above,   the   court    finds    that

Plaintiffs’ motion for preliminary injunction should be denied.

     The Supreme Court has repeatedly admonished lower courts that

they “should ordinarily not alter the election rules on the eve of

an election.”       Republican Nat’l Comm., 589 U.S. at 424.                 Here,

“[t]he election is not merely upcoming; it has already started.”

People Not Politicians, 2026 WL 2883222, at *2.              “Absentee voting

has already begun.”         Id.       Indeed, early voting is underway.

Accordingly, “[t]he Purcell principle applies with even greater

force here.”     Id.

     Plaintiffs have also delayed. They waited eighteen days after

the 2026 Numbered Memo was issued to bring this action, and they

did not file their motion for preliminary injunction until the day

before the counting of absentee ballots would begin.

     Apart from these deficiencies, Plaintiffs have not made the

“clear showing” the law requires that they are likely to succeed
