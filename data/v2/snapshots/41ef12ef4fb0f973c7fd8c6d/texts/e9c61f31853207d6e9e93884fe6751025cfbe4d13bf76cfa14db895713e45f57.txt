INTRODUCTION
Plaintiff asks this Court to interfere with state and local elections procedures
when an election has “already started.” People Not Politicians v. Onder, 609 U.S.
___, 2026 WL 2883222, *2 (Sept. 25, 2026). Mail ballots have been cast and
processed in California since October 5, and Election Day is just one week from
Tuesday. With no noted dissents, the Supreme Court recently—and repeatedly—
stayed orders arising from the Eighth Circuit that interfered with an ongoing
election. E.g., id. This Court should not go down that path. The basis for
plaintiff’s request is as unprecedented as it is meritless. It identifies no source of
appellate jurisdiction in the absence of any order from the district court; no injury
for purposes of standing; no recognized cause of action; no theory on the merits
resembling anything previously endorsed by the judiciary; no irreparable harm for
purposes of injunctive relief; and no exception to the settled rule that lower federal
courts should not alter the rules of approaching—or ongoing—elections. Purcell v.
Gonzalez, 549 U.S. 1, 4-6 (2006).
On the merits, plaintiff’s theory is that California officials’ actions to
administer state elections laws are “preempted” because those officials are
purportedly “obstructing” investigation of a federal criminal statute barring
noncitizens from voting. But California has long had laws in place to forbid
noncitizen registration and voting, remove noncitizens from the rolls, verify

1

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (4 of 27)

signatures, tabulate ballots, and adjudicate challenges to the validity of ballots
through legally specified procedures. Plaintiff does not suggest California is
failing to adhere to those laws or that the state laws violate federal laws. It instead
argues that California is “facilitating” violations of federal law by refusing to
deviate from longstanding state elections laws. Mot. 24.
That theory makes no sense, factually or legally. On the facts, plaintiff
ignores the State’s many laws to enforce voter-eligibility rules. It also glosses over
its own slipshod, unreliable process. Plaintiff alleged last July that California
“may” have some 190,000 noncitizens on its voter rolls. When state officials
requested more information in writing, plaintiff did not respond. California did not
hear anything further until October 13—just three weeks before Election Day—
when plaintiff sent California a letter alleging that it “believes” 20,000 voters in
L.A. County are “likely” noncitizens. California is understandably skeptical given
plaintiff’s refusal to share critical details about the investigation that produced
these allegations, as well as the federal government’s utter failure to substantiate
similar allegations recently made in other States, such as Nevada.
Beyond these factual flaws with plaintiff’s preemption theory, the theory
would also turn the Constitution on its head. When it comes to rules of elections
administration, the federal government has no power to interfere with the States
unless Congress passes a law governing the time, place, or manner of elections.

2

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (5 of 27)

The only statute identified by the application here—18 U.S.C. § 611(a)—restricts
who may vote, but it does not specify how States should go about regulating voter
eligibility. And it says nothing about the time, place, or manner of federal
elections. To the extent plaintiff’s theory is that California is not doing precisely
what the federal administration desires to assist in enforcing federal laws, that
theory is contrary to the Tenth Amendment and foreclosed by precedent.
“[R]efusing to help is not the same as impeding,” and provides no basis for
preemption. United States v. California, 921 F.3d 865, 888 (9th Cir. 2019).1
STATEMENT
1. The Constitution charges States with primary responsibility for
administering federal elections. U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1; art. II, § 1, cl. 2.
Unless Congress provides otherwise, States have the authority “to provide a
complete code for [federal] elections.” Smiley v. Holm, 285 U.S. 355, 366 (1932).
States also have sole authority to set voter qualifications, or “who may vote.”
Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council of Ariz., Inc., 570 U.S. 1, 16 (2013) (ITCA).
This case involves one such qualification: California’s requirement that only
citizens may vote. See Cal. Const. art. II, § 2(a); Cal. Elec. Code §§ 2100, 2101.

If the Court disagrees, and decides to issue an injunction, it would be
imperative for the Court to preserve the status quo by issuing an administrative
stay, see Doe #1 v. Trump, 944 F.3d 1222, 1223 (9th Cir. 2019), to allow time for
the State to seek emergency en banc review.

California enforces that requirement in several ways, including by requiring
attestations of citizenship under penalty of perjury to register and by criminalizing
voting by noncitizens. Infra pp. 12-13. California law also “sets forth a
comprehensive statutory framework for the care and handling of ballots.”
Cervantes v. Bianco, 596 P.3d 602, 608 (Cal. 2026). Among other things,
California requires elections officials to mail registered voters a ballot at least 29
days before an election. Cal. Elec. Code §§ 3001, 4005(a)(8)(A). After filling out
the ballot, a voter places it in an envelope, signs it, and returns it. Id.
§§ 3010(a)(2), 3011(a)(1)-(2).
Once a vote-by-mail ballot is received, the Registrar verifies the signature,
and, if the signature is valid, separates the ballot from the identification envelope
and tabulates the vote. Cal. Elec. Code §§ 3019(a)(1), 3019(b), 15101, 15109.
Because California law also protects ballot secrecy, see Cal. Const., art. 2, § 7,
once a signature is verified and a ballot is separated from its envelope, it is not
possible to identify who cast it, Dkt. 32-4 at 8. The only circumstance in which the
Registrar may not tabulate a vote (once the signature is verified) is if certain
specified individuals contest a voter’s eligibility at the place the mail ballot is
processed. That process is governed by a carefully reticulated set of state and local
laws. See Cal. Elec. Code §§ 14240, 15104, 15105; Cal. Code Regs. tit. 2,
§ 20879. Any person who challenges a mail voter’s qualifications has the “burden

of establishing extraordinary proof” that the voter is ineligible to vote “at the time
the challenge is made.” Cal. Elec. Code § 15106; Cal. Code Regs. tit. 2, § 20879.
Federal law also touches upon several aspects of voting. The National Voter
Registration Act of 1993 (NVRA) and the Help America Vote Act (HAVA)
mandate that state voter-registration forms include certain information. 52 U.S.C.
§§ 20508(b)(2), 21083(b)(4)(A)(i). The NVRA also requires States to make
“reasonable effort[s] to remove” specified ineligible voters from its voter rolls.
Id. § 20507(a)(4). While States set voter-eligibility requirements, federal law
makes it a misdemeanor for noncitizens to “vote in any election held solely or in
part for the purpose of electing” federal officials, with certain exceptions. 18
U.S.C. § 611(a). No federal statute prescribes the manner in which States must
process ballots once received.

ARGUMENT
For an injunction pending appeal, plaintiff must show that it is likely to
succeed on the merits, it will be irreparably harmed, the balance of equities tips in
its favor, and an injunction would be in the public interest. Brown as next friend of
K.M.K. v. Wash. Interscholastic Activities Ass’n, 2026 WL 2891827, *1 (9th Cir.
Sept. 25, 2026). Where, as here, a plaintiff seeks a mandatory injunction, an
injunction pending appeal will not be granted “unless extreme or very serious
damage will result.” Id.
I.

THIS COURT LACKS JURISDICTION
28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1) grants this Court jurisdiction over appeals from

“orders” either “granting” or “refusing” injunctions. Because no “order” was
entered here, this Court lacks appellate jurisdiction. See Fed. R. App. P. 4(a). A
district court’s denial of a temporary restraining order would ordinarily not be
appealable, let alone a district court’s failure to rule on a TRO request on plaintiff’s

7

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (10 of 27)

preferred schedule. See, e.g., Off. of Pers. Mgmt. v. Am. Fed’n of Gov’t Emps.,
AFL-CIO, 473 U.S. 1301, 1303-1305 (1985) (Burger, C.J., in chambers).
Nor is this the rare case where the Court has “jurisdiction to review
interlocutory orders that have the practical effect of refusing an injunction.”
A.A.R.P v. Trump, 605 U.S. 91, 94 (2025). “A district court’s inaction in the face
of extreme urgency and a high risk of ‘serious, perhaps irreparable,’ consequences
may have the effect of refusing an injunction.” Id. Plaintiff comes nowhere close
to meeting that extraordinary bar. It asserts that urgent action is needed to prevent
tabulation of votes under standard state election practices based on an unfounded
claim that noncitizens have received vote-by-mail ballots and may return them.
Mot. 10. That ignores the state rules governing tabulation of ballots, as well as
existing state and federal safeguards against noncitizen voting, supra pp. 3-5, and
rests on rank speculation, infra pp. 10-11, 21-23. And far from preventing
irreparable harm, compare A.A.R.P., 605 U.S. at 93, plaintiff’s requested relief
would cause extraordinary harm by altering the ordinary rules for tabulating ballots
in the middle of an election, see Purcell, 549 U.S. at 4-6.
II.

PLAINTIFF IS UNLIKELY TO SUCCEED ON THE MERITS
A.

Plaintiff Lacks Standing and a Cause of Action

1. To obtain preliminary relief, a plaintiff “must make a clear showing of
each element of standing.” Townley v. Miller, 722 F.3d 1128, 1133 (9th Cir.

8

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (11 of 27)

2013). Here, plaintiff cannot establish that California’s elections laws cause it
“injury in fact.” Id. Plaintiff asserts that it suffers “per se” harm whenever it
alleges that a federal law preempts state law. Mot. 34. But as several district
courts have recently concluded, “the mere existence of an [allegedly] preempted
state law” does not “create[] a sovereign injury to the federal government.” United
States v. California, 824 F. Supp. 3d 1079, 1083 (C.D. Cal. 2026); accord United
States v. Michigan, No. 26-cv-00246, Dkt. 44 at 7 (W.D. Mich. Oct. 7, 2026).
Members of the Supreme Court have also raised doubts about that expansive
“sovereign” theory of standing. See, e.g., Tr. of Oral Argument at 5:7-9:8, 29:2230:4, 44:4-45:8, 45:14-46:7, United States v. Texas, 142 S. Ct. 416 (2021) (No. 21588); see also Br. for Respondents at 34-38, United States v. Texas, 142 S. Ct. 416
(Mem.) (2021) (No. 21-588) (“[p]urported ‘sovereign interests’” do not alone
suffice to “give the United States standing”).
In arguing otherwise below, plaintiff relied on several cases, none of which
squarely addresses whether the federal government has standing to sue States
based solely on the existence of an allegedly preempted state law. Dkt. 22-2 at 1516. For example, in Arizona v. United States, 567 U.S. 387 (2012), the federal
government “asserted concrete, particularized injuries resulting from Arizona’s
immigration laws,” including that Arizona’s laws would “impose significant and
counterproductive burdens on federal agencies charged with enforcing the national

9

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (12 of 27)

immigration scheme.” Michigan, No. 26-cv-00246, Dkt. 44 at 9; see also id. at 911 & n.3 (distinguishing other authorities). The are no similar allegations here.

in the enforcement of federal law. Dkt. 22-2 at 16-17. But it cited no authority for
the proposition that spending money to enforce a criminal prohibition provides
standing to challenge state policies.2 Such a theory lacks a limiting principle; it
would allow the federal government to “initiate a campaign of preemption suits
under the aegis of its sovereignty to bring state law in line with its own political
agenda.” California, 824 F. Supp. 3d at 1085. Standing doctrine is intended to
prevent exactly that sort of abuse; it “implements the Framers’ concept of the
proper—and properly limited—role of the courts in a democratic society.” FDA v.
All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 380 (2024).

dubious. See infra pp. 21-23. And the only way that voting by noncitizens could

B.

18 U.S.C. § 611’s Criminal Prohibition Does Not Preempt
California’s Election Laws

1. The Elections Clause vests States “with responsibility for the mechanics of
congressional elections.” ITCA, 570 U.S. at 9. Although Congress has power to
“pre-empt state legislative choices” as to “how federal elections are held,”
Congress may not regulate “who may vote in them.” Id. at 9, 16. “Prescribing
voting qualifications, therefore, ‘forms no part of the power to be conferred upon
the national government’ by the Elections Clause, which is ‘expressly restricted to
the regulation of the times, the places, and the manner of elections.’” Id. at 12.
California has exercised its broad authority over elections by specifying who
may vote in congressional elections and regulating the times, places, and manner
of holding them. Under California law, only citizens may vote. Supra p. 3.
California enforces that requirement by, for instance, requiring an attestation of
citizenship under oath to register to vote; by matching signatures on mail ballots
12

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (15 of 27)

and registration records; and by criminalizing fraudulent voting by ineligible
voters. E.g., Cal. Elec. Code §§ 2101, 2102(c), 2111, 2150(a)(1), 2264(a)(3), (b),
3019, 18560. Elections officials must also cancel the registration of any voter
upon proof that the person is ineligible to vote. Id. §§ 2201(a)(8), (c), 2213.
Under state law, specified individuals may challenge mail ballots based on the
voter’s qualifications to vote, including citizenship. Supra pp. 4-5. Once a ballot
is cast, detailed provisions govern how state officials must handle a ballot,
including by signature matching and counting a ballot. Cervantes, 596 P.3d at 608.
Detailed provisions also specify the exclusive circumstances in which cast ballots
may not be counted. Supra pp. 4-5.
Dissatisfied with California’s manner of conducting elections and verifying
citizenship of its voters, plaintiff asserts that section 611 preempts California’s
detailed process for qualifying voters and counting ballots. Mot. 27. But
section 611 is a misdemeanor criminal statute that provides only that it “shall be
unlawful for any alien to vote in any” federal election, except in certain
circumstances. 18 U.S.C. § 611(a)(1)-(a)(3). It governs an individual’s conduct
and does not specify how a State must verify the qualifications of voters, what a
State must do when presented with an allegation that a voter is unqualified, or how
a State must handle voted ballots from allegedly unqualified voters. Nor does it
authorize the federal government to superintend or interfere with a State’s

13

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (16 of 27)

administration of its elections. California’s election regime thus does not conflict
with any aspect of section 611—and “state law is preempted by the federal
[election] statutes only ‘so far as the conflict extends.’” Watson v. Republican
Nat’l Comm., 609 U.S. 672, 697 (2026).
In fact, section 611’s plain terms explicitly honor state laws concerning voter
qualification requirements. The statute exempts from its scope noncitizens who
are “authorized to vote” by state law under specified circumstances. 18 U.S.C.
§ 611(a)(1)-(a)(3). Thus, far from supporting a blanket override of a State’s voter
qualification determinations, the statute expressly incorporates state law in
defining its scope. If the statute had the expansive preemptive scope suggested by
plaintiff, it would plainly exceed Congress’ limited power under the Elections
Clause. Supra p. 12. Indeed, a federal court recently held that section 611 is
unconstitutional because it interferes with States’ “sole authority to set voter
qualifications.” United States v. Cox, 2026 WL 2948729, *10 (S.D. Fla. 2026); see
Mem. of Court-Appointed Amicus Paul Clement, United States v. Cox, No. 25CR-60249, Dkt. 46 (S.D. Fla. July 23, 2026) (arguing section 611 is
unconstitutional).
2. Plaintiff does not seriously contend that California’s system of election
administration conflicts with the terms of section 611. Rather, it argues that
California “knowingly facilitate[s]” violations of the statute by declining to adopt

14

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (17 of 27)

the federal government’s requested approach to voter-eligibility verification.
Mot. 27. That is not a valid preemption argument, but an improper effort to
impose new obligations on elections officials that Congress has never enacted.
Earlier this year, Congress refused to enact voter-verification requirements
“through the democratic process.” Watson, 609 U.S. at 696; see S.B. 1383, 119th
Cong., https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1383 (the SAVE
America Act). Plaintiff should not be allowed to circumvent that process and
achieve through the courts—and an unfounded preemption theory—what could not
be achieved through democratic means. In any event, plaintiff’s factual premise is
flatly wrong: California law prohibits voting by noncitizens; it does not
“knowingly” encourage such voting in any way. Supra pp. 3-4.
Plaintiff’s obstacle preemption theory (Mot. 30-33) also fails under settled
precedent. “‘The Supreme Court has found obstacle preemption in only a small
number of cases’ of two specific types: those where ‘the federal legislation at
issue involved a ‘uniquely federal area[] of regulation’ and state law ‘directly
interfered with the operation of the federal program,’ and those where ‘a federal
enactment clearly struck a particular balance of interests that would be disturbed or
impeded by state regulation.’” ConocoPhillips Alaska, Inc. v. Alaska Oil & Gas
Conservation Comm’n, 177 F.4th 964, 972 (9th Cir. 2026). Neither circumstance
exists here: election administration is traditionally the province of States, supra

15

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (18 of 27)

p. 12, and there is no credible argument that California’s mechanism for verifying
voter eligibility or tabulating votes “disturb[s]” or “impede[s]” section 611, which
says nothing about how to verify eligibility or count votes.
On the contrary, California’s election rules fully comply with Congress’s
mandates governing election administration. For example, the NVRA and HAVA
require voter registration applicants to attest to voter eligibility under oath,
including citizenship. 52 U.S.C. §§ 20508(b)(2), 21083(b)(4)(A)(i). California
does, too. Cal. Elec. Code §§ 2101, 2102(c), 2111, 2150(a)(1), 2264(a)(3), (b).
The NVRA also requires that States “conduct a general program that makes a
reasonable effort to remove” specified ineligible voters. 52 U.S.C. § 20507(a)(4).
California does this as well. See Cal Elec. Code §§ 2200 et seq. State law cannot
impede federal law when it simply “trace[s]” it. Chamber of Commerce of U.S. v.
Whiting, 563 U.S. 582, 602 (2011).
At bottom, plaintiff’s preemption theory amounts to the view that the
Executive Branch may demand that States “work[] with the Federal Government to
prevent” violations of federal criminal laws. Dkt. 1 at 14. But the federal
government cannot “commandeer” States “to address particular problems,” or
“command the States’ officers, or those of their political subdivisions, to
administer or enforce a federal regulatory program.” Printz v. United States, 521
U.S. 898, 929, 935 (1997). As this Court has emphasized, “refusing to help is not

16

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (19 of 27)

the same as impeding,” and is no basis for an obstacle-preemption claim.
California, 921 F.3d at 888.
3. Plaintiff is likely to fail on the merits for a host of other reasons. “As a
general rule,” courts “are reluctant to issue injunctions against the commission of a
crime” in recognition of “the adequacy of the criminal remedy to protect the
plaintiff’s interests.” 11A Wright & Miller’s Fed. Prac. & Proc. § 2942 & n. 73
(3d ed. 2026). While plaintiff may seek an injunction against a potential crime
when a specific statute authorizes such relief, e.g., Beck v. Prupis, 529 U.S. 494,
496 n.1 (2000), Congress has not authorized it under section 611. And while
Congress has expressly authorized USDOJ to seek injunctive relief to prevent
alleged criminal violations of other election laws, see 52 U.S.C. §§ 10308(d),
20510(a), section 611 is not among them—an “omission[] [that] should be
understood as exclusion[],” Silvers v. Sony Pictures Ent., Inc., 402 F.3d 881, 885
(9th Cir. 2005) (en banc).
Granting the requested relief would also violate multiple state and federal
laws. At a minimum, it would violate California’s laws governing its elections.
Supra pp. 3-5, 12-13. It would also contravene: the NVRA’s prohibition on any
state “program the purpose of which is to systematically remove the names of
ineligible voters from the official lists of eligible voters” in the 90 days leading up
to an election, 52 U.S.C. § 20507(c)(2); the Voting Rights Act’s command that

“[n]o person acting under color of law shall fail or refuse to permit any person to
vote who is . . . qualified to vote, or willfully fail or refuse to tabulate, count, and
report such person’s vote,” id. § 10307(a); and the Constitution’s protections of the
right to vote, see Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428, 434 (1992); Dkt. 31-1 at 19-23.
III. EQUITABLE CONSIDERATIONS DECISIVELY WEIGH AGAINST AN
INJUNCTION
1. Relief “may be inequitable if it is issued shortly before an election, when
candidates, election officials, and voters have relied on the rules in place at that
time.” Malliotakis v. Williams, 146 S. Ct. 809, 811 (2026) (Alito, J., concurring).
As the Supreme Court reiterated just two days ago, “lower federal courts should
ordinarily not alter the election rules on the eve of an election.” Scanlan v.
Coalition for Open Democracy, 2026 WL 3167846, *1 (Oct. 22, 2026). This
“Purcell principle” prevents “the uncertainty engendered by judicial disruptions to
the status quo in the midst of elections [that] can and often will cause eligible
voters to remain away from the polls.” Mi Familia Vota v. Fontes, 111 F.4th 976,
985 (9th Cir. 2024).
The “Purcell principle applies with even greater force” in circumstances like
these: where the “election is not merely upcoming; it has already started.” Onder,
2026 WL 2883222, *2. As required by California law, supra p. 4, the County
mailed approximately 5.9 million vote-by-mail ballots to registered voters in its
jurisdiction weeks ago, Dkt. 32-4 at 9, 13. And as detailed above, supra pp. 4-5,
18

Case: 26-7070, 10/24/2026, DktEntry: 30.1, (21 of 27)

once a vote-by-mail ballot is received, the Registrar simply verifies the signature
on its envelope, and, if the signature is valid, removes the ballot and tabulates the
vote. As of four days ago, the Registrar had completed this process for 331,000
voters. Dkt. 32-4 at 10. The only circumstance in which the Registrar may not
tabulate the vote (once the signature is verified) is if a specified person contests a
voter’s eligibility at the place the mail ballot is being processed, supra pp. 4-5,
based on “extraordinary proof” presented “at the time the challenge is made” that
the person who cast the vote is ineligible, Cal. Elec. Code § 15106.
Yet now—with voting already underway—plaintiff seeks a court order
bypassing that state scheme and imposing new elections procedures for up to
20,000 votes. That would plainly “alter the election rules of a state on the eve of
an election”; indeed, it would “usher in electoral chaos.” Onder, 2026 WL
2883222, *2. There could be no clearer case for the application of Purcell.
Plaintiff asks this Court to impose an entirely new set of requirements for
processing and tabulating ballots, in the middle of an election, that are impossible
to satisfy. As the Registrar explains, segregating ballots would be a logistical and
administrative nightmare: plaintiff’s list does not contain the information
necessary to identify voters; the Registrar has no established system for
segregating ballots; and trying to create one now risks shutting down the system
the Registrar needs to run the entire election. Dkt. 32-3 at 6-8, Dkt. 32-4 at 14-17.

It is also not possible to segregate ballots already tabulated: to preserve the
right to a secret ballot, see Cal. Const., art. 2, § 7, once the signature verification
process is complete, the Registrar separates the ballot from its envelope, and there
is no way to identify which voter cast it, see Dkt. 32-4 at 8. As the top official
charged with running the County’s elections has concluded, it “would be virtually
impossible to timely certify the election results in compliance with state law” if the
requested injunction was granted. Id. at 4. Purcell exists to prevent just that result.
See Merrill v. Milligan, 142 S. Ct. 879, 881 (Kavanaugh, J., concurring) (Purcell
ensures that “[w]hen an election is close at hand, the rules of the road [are] clear
and settled”).
Plaintiff’s only response is that “California is well-equipped to provide a
remedy, as it has laws on its books that facilitate the very relief sought here.”
Mot. 10. That grossly misunderstands California’s elections regime. As
explained, California law sets forth a detailed and exclusive process through which
specified individuals may contest vote-by-mail voter eligibility. Supra pp. 4-5. No
aspect of that process contemplates separating ballots upon receipt in response to
third-party demands. And the sole provision of state law that plaintiff cites—
California Code of Regulations, title 2, § 20363—applies only to ballots
challenged during a formal “recount.” See Cal. Code. Regs. tit. 2, div. 7, ch. 4.8.
Plaintiff has not invoked that process, which is governed by a specific set of laws

Plaintiff further asserts that it will be harmed because, absent an injunction,
