MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES
I. INTRODUCTION
Voting is already underway in Los Angeles County. Ballots have been
mailed, returned, and processed, and election officials are working toward fixed
deadlines for counting and certification. Plaintiff United States of America,
through the Department of Justice (together, “the Executive”), now asks this Court
to interrupt that work and enjoin the withholding of 20,000 registered California
voters’ ballots pending investigation based solely on an unreliable voter roll
maintenance list (the “October List”).
The Executive portrays that intervention as modest and its list as the product
of a thorough investigation. Neither characterization survives scrutiny. The
Executive’s own declarations describe a bulk data match that began with a voter
roster more than a year old, obtained from an unidentified source, supplemented
through an unnamed commercial platform, and compared against federal
databases. (McLoughlin Decl. ¶¶ 20–21; Bartell Decl. ¶ 16-17.) The Executive’s
own declarant concedes that the resulting data may be “incomplete, outdated, or
mismatched.” (Bartell Decl. ¶ 16.) The Complaint describes the October List as
the starting point for further work, not a determination of ineligibility. (Compl. ¶¶
38–43.) And that work, the Executive concedes, cannot be finished before the
election ends. (Id. ¶ 60.)
A review of the October List shows the consequences of treating suspicions
as a basis for withholding or segregating ballots. Of the more than 20,000 records
on the Executive’s list, 7,035 bear County addresses. (Declaration of Aman
Bhullar (“Bhullar Dec.”) ¶ 25.) That total includes duplicates, cancelled
registrations, and a high percentage of U.S. born-persons. (Declaration of Monica
Flores (“Flores Dec.”) ¶ 45.) Federal agencies’ database “matching” cannot be
equated with imminent unlawful votes. Other States’ experience confirms why
such matches require investigation and individualized proof. Some States use the
Department of Homeland Security’s (“DHS”) Systematic Alien Verification for

II. RELEVANT BACKGROUND
A. The County’s Election Procedures
Defendant Dean C. Logan is sued in his officially capacity as the Los
Angeles County Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk (the “County”). (Compl. ¶ 16.)
The County’s administration of the November 3, 2026 General Election is the
largest and most complex in the nation, servicing 88 cities, 55 general and special
districts, and 140 unincorporated areas. (Flores Dec. ¶ 5.) The County serves
approximately 5.9 million registered voters, with 5,000 voting precincts for
countywide elections. (Id.)
In California, voter rolls are maintained by the California Secretary of State.
(Id. ¶ 6.) The California voter registration system is called VoteCal. (Id.) The Los
Angeles County Database Voter Information Management System is called
(“DIMS”). (Id.) The DIMS database and VoteCal databases interact with each
other in order to facilitate the maintenance of up-to-date voter rolls for the County.
(Id.) The County is not responsible for the voter rolls outside of the County, nor
does it have access to statewide data. (Id.)
For voter registration, under Elections Code § 2111, a person “may prove
he or she is a citizen by his or her certification under penalty of perjury on the
affidavit of registration.” (Id. ¶ 7; see also 52 U.S.C. § 20508(b)(2) (federal mail
voter registration form must include “an attestation that the applicant meets each
such requirement,” including citizenship, and “require[] the signature of the
applicant, under penalty of perjury”).) Consistent with California and federal law,
for registration, the County relies on the under oath representation for proof of an
individual’s citizenship, no additional verification is required. (Id.)
California law requires the County to mail a vote-by-mail (“VBM”) ballot
to every active registered voter 29 days before the election on October 5, 2026.
(Id. ¶ 18; see also Cal. Elec. Code § 3000.5.) All VBM ballots require voter and
signature verification before the vote can be counted. (Flores Dec. ¶ 20.) This

B. DHS’ July 2026 Letter To The Secretary Of State
On July 16, 2026, DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin wrote to Secretary of
State Shirley N. Weber asserting that a “[p]reliminary review” of California’s
publicly available voter data revealed “there may be as many as 190,832 non-
citizens registered to vote in California.” (Compl. Ex. A.) On July 23, 2026,
Secretary Weber responded that she had “serious doubts about the reliability of
[DHS’s] data,” noting that her office had never shared the statewide voter
registration list with DHS, and requested a “detailed description of the
methodology utilized by DHS to identify these allegedly ineligible voters,
including . . . all data sources, software, and matching criteria,” and whether
artificial intelligence was used. (Compl. Ex. B.) Secretary Weber also advised that
her office would comply with the NVRA’s prohibition on systematic cancellation
of registrations within 90 days of a federal election—a period that began on
August 5, 2026. (Id.) DHS did not provide the requested methodology or
otherwise respond to Secretary Weber.

III. LEGAL STANDARD
A temporary restraining order is “an extraordinary remedy that may only
be awarded upon a clear showing that the plaintiff is entitled to such relief.” Winter
v. Nat. Res. Def. Council, Inc., 555 U.S. 7, 22 (2008). The movant must establish
that (1) it is likely to succeed on the merits, (2) it is likely to suffer irreparable
harm absent preliminary relief, (3) the balance of equities tips in its favor, and (4)
an injunction is in the public interest. Id. at 20. The standard for a temporary
restraining order is “substantially identical” to that for a preliminary injunction.
Stuhlbarg Int’l Sales Co. v. John D. Brush & Co., 240 F.3d 832, 839 n.7 (9th Cir.
2001). When the government is a party, the third and fourth factors merge. Drakes
Bay Oyster, 747 F.3d 1073, 1092 (9th Cir. 2014)
The relief sought here is mandatory: it would compel State and County
election officials to take affirmative steps—segregating ballots and conducting a
new citizenship-verification process—that alter, rather than preserve, the status
quo. Mandatory injunctions are “particularly disfavored” and should not issue
“unless the facts and law clearly favor the moving party.” Garcia v. Google, Inc.,
786 F.3d 733, 740 (9th Cir. 2015) (en banc). The Executive’s contention that its
injunction would “preserve the status quo”, (Appl. at 3, 27), inverts the inquiry.
The status quo is the County’s administration of an election under existing law, in
which registered voters’ ballots are verified and counted. An order requiring the
County to withhold and segregate ballots and create a new citizenship-verification
regime is the opposite.

a voter’s qualifications to use the same standards, practices, and procedures used
for other voters in the same county already found qualified. Id. § 10101(a)(2)(A).
