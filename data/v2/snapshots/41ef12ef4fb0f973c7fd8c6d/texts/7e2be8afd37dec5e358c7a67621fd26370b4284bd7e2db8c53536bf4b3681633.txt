INTRODUCTION
With the November 3, 2026 General Election well underway, the United
States demands that California’s elections officials refuse to count votes cast by as
many as 20,000 voters duly registered under state and federal law. This demand
rests on mere suspicion, aroused by suspect data that the Department of Justice
(“DOJ”) failed to provide state officials. Instead, the California Secretary of State
received a letter from DOJ demanding that she work to identify the citizenship of
an unspecified list of individuals, or else direct elections officials to refuse to
tabulate their ballots. This lawsuit followed less than 48 hours later.
At best, this litigation is a reckless political stunt. At worst, it threatens to
disenfranchise thousands of Californians this election. Plaintiff’s sole claim—that a
federal criminal prohibition on individual noncitizen voting preempts large swaths
of California’s election administration—borders on the absurd. The relief Plaintiff
seeks is as unlawful as it is untenable. And the stakes could not be higher; with
thousands of ballots being cast every day across California, the election must
proceed in accordance with the long-settled laws that govern its administration. The
Court should deny Plaintiff’s application.
BACKGROUND

engaged in a comprehensive effort to interfere in state elections based on
unsupported claims of voter fraud. The government has sued thirty states plus the
District of Columbia to attempt to obtain unredacted voter rolls, and federal courts
have rejected those efforts in all of the 26 cases to have reached a final decision
(including in California).1 The government has also sought to impose onerous new

II. LEGAL BACKGROUND
The California Secretary of State, Dr. Shirley N. Weber, is the state’s Chief
Officer of Elections. Lean Decl. ¶ 5. California’s 58 counties are primarily
responsible for administering elections in the state. Id. ¶ 4. Each county is
responsible for registering voters, maintaining voter rolls, issuing ballots to
registered voters, and tabulating and certifying election results. Id.
While Plaintiff has submitted a declaration asserting that a federal official
“understand[s]” that the Secretary’s Office was “provided that list on October 13,”
ECF No. 22-4 (“Bartell Decl.”) ¶ 22, they are mistaken, Evans Decl. ¶ 8.
3

States hold primary responsibility for federal elections under the United States
Constitution, including their “Times, Places and Manner,” subject to laws enacted
by Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1; see also id. art. II, § 1, cl. 2. Unless
Congress provides otherwise, States therefore have the authority “to provide a
complete code for [federal] elections.” Smiley v. Holm, 285 U.S. 355, 366 (1932);
see also Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council of Ariz., Inc., 570 U.S. 1, 8–9 (2013)
(“ITCA”). States also have sole authority to set voter qualifications, or “who may
vote.” ITCA, 570 U.S. at 16; see also U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 1; id. amend. XVII.
United States citizenship is one of several requirements to vote in California
elections, and the State enforces this obligation in several ways. See Cal. Const. art.
II, § 2(a); Cal. Elec. Code §§ 2100, 2101. California requires all voter registration
applicants to attest to citizenship under oath, under penalty of perjury and by
signature. Cal. Elec. Code §§ 2101, 2102(c), 2111, 2150(a)(1), 2264(a)(3), (b).
Elections officials must cancel the registration of any voter “[u]pon proof that the
person is . . . ineligible to vote,” subject to procedural safeguards. Cal. Elec. Code
§§ 2201(a)(8), (c), 2213. State law permits challenges to mail ballots related to “the
voter’s qualifications to vote,” including citizenship. Id. §§ 14240, 15105, 15106.
Finally, state law also penalizes numerous related offenses, including fraud in voter
registration and voting, id. §§ 18100, 18500, 18560, and assisting ineligible voters
to vote, id. § 18561. See also id. § 18543 (criminalizing fraudulent or spurious mass
voter challenges for improper purposes).
For those who are eligible to vote, California’s Constitution guarantees a right
to vote and “have that vote counted.” Cal. Const. art. II, §§ 2, 2.5. State law further
affirms that Californians “have the right to cast a ballot if [they] are a valid
registered voter.” Cal. Elec. Code, § 2300(a)(1)(A); see also id. § 2000(a).
California sends each active registered voter a mail ballot. Cal. Elec. Code
§§ 3000.5, 3003, 3010; see also id. § 3102. After voting their ballot, a voter places
it in an envelope that is signed under penalty of perjury to establish the validity of

the vote. Id. §§ 3010(a)(2), 3011(a)(1)–(2). Once elections officials receive a mail

Elections Clause Preemption. Plaintiff’s preemption claim must be
adjudicated under the Elections Clause. Where Congress has not exercised its
Elections Clause authority to displace state election law, the States retain the
“responsibility for the mechanics of congressional elections,” ITCA, 570 U.S. at 9
(quotations omitted), including as to “registration, supervision of voting, protection
of voters, [and] prevention of fraud and corrupt practices,” Smiley, 285 U.S. at 366.
Both the Ninth Circuit and the Supreme Court have recognized that it is the
Elections Clause preemption framework, not that of the Supremacy Clause, that
governs in cases concerning the rules for election administration. See ITCA, 570
U.S. at 13–15; Gonzalez v. Arizona, 677 F.3d 383, 392–393 (9th Cir. 2012), aff’d
sub nom. ITCA, 570 U.S. 1. This is one such case, as Plaintiff contests and seeks
relief from California’s rules and conduct with respect to election administration.
See, e.g., ECF No. 1 (“Compl.”), ¶ 62; TRO at 19, 20 n.2.
Under the Elections Clause preemption framework, congressional enactments
displace state election law only “so far as [Congress] . . . exercise[s]” its authority
“and no farther.” ITCA, 570 U.S. at 9 (citation omitted and emphasis added); see
also Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 384 (1879) (federal law preempts state
election law only “so far as the conflict extends”). 18 U.S.C. § 611 plainly has no
such preemptive effect, as it concerns individual noncitizen voters’ criminal
conduct in casting a vote and does not purport to set any rules governing election
administration. Under these circumstances, it is the State’s constitutional
prerogative to maintain the rules about which Plaintiff complains. See generally
Georgia v. Meadows, 88 F.4th 1331, 1347 (11th Cir. 2023) (recognizing that the
federal Executive has no authority to “interfere[] with state election procedures
based solely on [its] own initiative”).

Supremacy Clause Preemption. Even if the standard Supremacy Clause
framework applies, Plaintiff fails to show obstacle preemption. To establish
obstacle preemption, Plaintiff must show that California’s rules for election
administration “stand[] as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the

United States, 567 U.S. 387, 399 (2012) (citations and quotations omitted).
This is a high bar. “The Supreme Court has found obstacle preemption in only
a small number of cases,” and never under circumstances like these. ConocoPhillips
Alaska, Inc. v. Alaska Oil and Gas Conservation Comm’n, 177 F.4th 964, 972 (9th
Cir. 2026). And a “presumption against preemption” raises that bar even higher
where “a statute regulates a field traditionally occupied by states.” Mont. Medical
Ass’n v. Knudsen, 119 F.4th 618, 623 (9th Cir. 2024). Under this presumption,
courts “assume that a federal law does not preempt the state’s police powers absent
a clear and manifest purpose of Congress.” Id.
Plaintiff cannot carry this burden. A presumption against preemption applies
here because 18 U.S.C. § 611 plainly pertains to a field traditionally occupied by
the State, by the Founders’ design—i.e., regulating federal elections and
determining voter qualifications. See ITCA, 570 U.S. at 8. And Plaintiff has not
demonstrated that any aspect of California’s election administration is an obstacle
to enforcing 18 U.S.C. § 611 as Congress intended. Indeed, nothing in 18 U.S.C.

C. Plaintiff’s Requested Relief Is Unlawful
Additionally, Plaintiff has no prospect of success on the merits because the

To determine whether an election procedure unconstitutionally trespasses on

F.4th 1179, 1194–95 (9th Cir. 2021) (due process and other constitutional
challenges to deprivations of the right to vote are analyzed under Anderson-

voting rights against “the precise interests put forward by the [government] as

strict scrutiny.” Nader v. Brewer, 531 F.3d 1028, 1035 (9th Cir. 2008).

III. THE PURCELL PRINCIPLE AND EQUITIES HEAVILY DISFAVOR A
RESTRAINING ORDER
Plaintiff’s relief is barred by the well-established Purcell principle. That
principle provides that “lower federal courts should not ‘alter the election rules’” of
a State “on the eve of an election.” Allen v. Milligan, 608 U.S. 511, 514 (2026); see
also Purcell v. Gonzalez, 549 U.S. 1 (2006).
Plaintiff argues Purcell does not apply because, in Plaintiff’s telling, it only
seeks to maintain existing election rules, and its sought-after relief can cause “no
confusion among voters or otherwise” because “it concerns how to handle certain
ballots once cast.” TRO at 21–22 n.3. Plaintiff’s representations are difficult to
square with this litigation and the law.
First of all, Courts routinely apply Purcell to deny injunctive relief related to
“how to handle ballots once cast,” TRO at 21–22 n.3. See, e.g., Republican Nat’l
Comm. v. Democratic Nat’l Comm. (2020) 589 U.S. 423, 425 (applying Purcell in
overturning a district court order allowing ballots cast by mail and postmarked after
election day to be tabulated); Democratic Nat’l Comm. v. Wis. State Legislature,
592 U.S. 1039, 1043–44 (2020) (discussing Purcell and the impropriety of a district
court injunction changing the treatment of cast ballots under state law six weeks
before election day) (Kavanaugh, J., concurring); New Georgia Project v.
23
Case 2:26-cv-12025 Document 31-1 Filed 10/20/26 Page 36 of 41 Page ID #:373
Raffensperger, 976 F.3d 1278, 1283 (11th Cir. 2020) (applying Purcell in reversing
district court injunction concerning the treatment of cast ballots “in the middle” of
election with “absentee ballots already printed and mailed”); Common Cause
Indiana v. Lawson, 977 F.3d 663, 665 (7th Cir. 2020) (same).
Plaintiff is also wrong to the extent it implies that the requested relief is
consistent with California law. See, e.g., TRO at 3, 14, 21–22 n.3, 28. State law sets
forth a detailed and exclusive process for contesting voter eligibility, no aspect of
which contemplates separating ballots upon receipt in response to third-party
demands. See, e.g., Cal. Elec. Code §§ 14240 (challenges to voter eligibility),
15105 (allowing challenges to mail ballots on the same grounds as challenges at
polling places “prior to the opening of the identification envelope of the challenged
voter”), 15106 (establishing a burden of “extraordinary proof of the validity of the
challenge at the time the challenge is made”); Cal Reg. tit. 2, § 20879 (describing
challenges to mail ballots). Plaintiff cites only Cal. Code Regs. tit. 2, § 20363 to
argue that the relief sought here—widespread, pre-tabulation ballot segregation—
“is provided for in California’s regulations.” See, e.g., TRO at 3, 28. But this
provision applies to ballots challenged during a formal “recount,” governed by Cal.
Elec. Code §§ 15600 et seq. Plaintiff clearly has not invoked this process, which
entails wholly different administrative considerations. The requested relief would
undoubtedly “alter the election rules” while the election is ongoing, contrary to the
Purcell principle. Allen, 608 U.S. at 514.

Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 435 (2009).

no public interest in the perpetuation of unlawful agency action.” Novedades Y

is clear that it would not be equitable or in the public’s interest to allow the [United

Plaintiff argues that the public interest favors an injunction because Plaintiff
