INTRODUCTION
Federal law prohibits noncitizen voting in federal elections. 18 U.S.C. § 611. In July
2026, the U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) alerted the California Secretary
of State (together with other Defendants, California) that a preliminary review of state
records revealed numerous noncitizen registered voters. On October 13, after the Federal
Government thoroughly analyzed that preliminary data, it identified over 20,000 individ-
uals who it reasonably believes are noncitizen voters participating in the federal election.
More precisely, two agencies housed within DHS that are charged with immigration en-
forcement—U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) and Homeland Security
Investigations (HSI)—deployed a longstanding multiagency and multilayered process to
whittle down a preliminary list of over 175,000 potential noncitizen voters to a list of over
20,000 people who are “(1) registered to vote in California, (2) were sent a vote-by-mail
ballot, and (3) are highly likely to be noncitizen aliens.” Decl. of Nicolas Bartell ¶ 25;
Decl. of Eric McLoughlin ¶ 25. The Federal Government gave that list to California to
protect American citizens’ votes while preventing unlawful votes. In response, California
not just refused to act; it made clear that it is committed to treating ballots from those
likely noncitizens equal to ballots from American citizens, prompting this suit and motion
that seeks commonplace election relief when ballots are called into question.
This Court should grant the motion. Otherwise, there is a significant risk that Amer-
ican votes will be diluted. Just this morning, the United States charged dozens of nonciti-
zens for election and immigration related crimes. See Press Release, Department of Justice
28 Foreign Nationals Arrested on Charges that they Illegally Registered and Voted (Oc-
tober 19, 2026) (detailing 18 U.S.C. § 611 and other federal charges). Every single one of
these individuals is on the list the Federal Government provided California. And the State
is no stranger to voting-record irregularities. California has admitted to mishandling tens
of thousands of voter records, including by registering numerous noncitizens to vote. See
Mark Osborne, 1,500 noncitizens may have been registered to vote in California DMV

As an initial matter, this action follows a long line of cases in which the United
States seeks and obtains injunctive relief when state actors violate federal law, especially
in matters involving immigration and elections. See, e.g., Arizona v. United States, 567
U.S. 387 (2012) (affirming order that prevented Arizona from enforcing its immigration

Miss., 318 F.2d 1, 3–4 (5th Cir. 1963) (enjoining state officials from “encourag[ing] racial
segregation” in violation of federal law). Consistent with this precedent, the United States
has standing and may seek equitable relief to enforce the supremacy of federal law, as
California itself has acknowledged, infra pp. 25–26.

BACKGROUND
I. Legal Background
A. Federal Law
Since at least 1996, Congress has made it “unlawful for any alien to vote in any
election held solely or in part for the purpose of electing a candidate for the office of
President, Vice President, Presidential elector, Member of the Senate, Member of the
House of Representatives, Delegate from the District of Columbia, or Resident Commis-
sioner[.]” 18 U.S.C. § 611(a). A noncitizen may, however, vote in an election that other-
wise includes federal candidates if (a) “the election is held partly for some other pur-
pose[,]” (b) a state or local law allows noncitizens to vote, and (c) voting “is conducted
independently of voting for a candidate for such Federal offices, in such a manner that an
alien has the opportunity to vote for such other purpose, but not an opportunity to vote for
a candidate for any one or more of such Federal offices.” Id. Congress passed this section
as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996. See
Pub. L. No. 104-208, div. C, title II, § 216(a), 110 Stat. 3009-572 (Sept. 30, 1996). A
noncitizen who violates the voting ban may be fined or imprisoned. See 18 U.S.C.
§ 611(b); United States v. Knight, 490 F.3d 1268, 1271 (11th Cir. 2007) (finding 18 U.S.C.
§ 611 to be a general intent crime where the government needs only to “prove that the
defendant knowingly engaged in the conduct prohibited” (citation omitted)).
Congress promulgated 18 U.S.C. § 611(a) under its plenary authority over immi-
gration and its power to regulate federal elections under the Elections Clause. The Federal
4

Government “has broad, undoubted power over the subject of immigration and the status
of aliens.” Arizona v. United States, 567 U.S. 387, 394 (2012); see also Chavez-Perez v.
Ashcroft, 386 F.3d 1284, 1292 (9th Cir. 2004) (“[f]ederal authority in the areas of immi-
gration and naturalization is plenary”). This plenary authority “derives from various
sources, including the Federal Government’s power to establish a uniform Rule of Natu-
ralization,” “its power to regulate Commerce with foreign Nations,” “and its broad author-
ity over foreign affairs.” Toll v. Moreno, 458 U.S. 1, 10 (1982). Indeed, “any policy toward
aliens is vitally and intricately interwoven with contemporaneous policies in regard to the
conduct of foreign relations, the war power, and the maintenance of a republican form of
government.” Harisiades v. Shaughnessy, 342 U.S. 580, 588–89 (1952); accord Arizona,
567 U.S. at 395 (recognizing that it is “fundamental that foreign countries” confer with
“one national sovereign” on immigration issues). This naturally includes authority over
the statuses of aliens and “regulation of the conduct of an alien before naturalization.”
Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 66 (1941). Thus, “the regulation of aliens is so inti-
mately blended and intertwined with responsibilities of the national government that
where it acts, and the state also acts on the same subject, ‘the act of congress, or the treaty,
is supreme; and the law of the state, though enacted in the exercise of powers not contro-
verted, must yield to it.’” Id. Section 611(a) thus regulates the conduct of aliens vis-a-vis
the Federal Government under its plenary authority over aliens.
In addition, the Elections Clause in Article I provides that “Congress may at any
time by Law make or alter such Regulations, except as to the Places of chusing Senators.”
Otherwise, “The Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Repre-
sentatives, shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof.” U.S. Const. art. I,
§ 4, cl. 1. Along with the Necessary and Proper Clause, the Elections Clause grants Con-
gress the authority to regulate the manner of conducting federal elections, regardless of
whether it can regulate the qualifications for voting. See Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council
of Arizona, Inc. (ITCA), 570 U.S. 1, 7-9, 16-17 (2013); Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 62
5

(1976) (per curiam). Section 611(a) thus readily falls within those broad grants of author-
ity, as it regulates the conduct of aliens within the sphere of federal elections and allows
for the “maintenance of a republican form of government” by ensuring only citizens can
vote in federal elections. See Smiley v. Holm, 285 U.S. 355, 366–67 (1932) (“In exercising
[its Elections Clause] power, the Congress may supplement the[] state regulations . . . . It
may impose additional penalties for the violations of the state laws.”); Burroughs v. United
States, 290 U.S. 534, 547 (1932) (“The power of Congress to protect the election of Pres-
ident and Vice President from corruption [is] clear.”); Bluman v. FEC, 800 F. Supp. 2d
281, 283 (D.D.C. 2011) (Kavanaugh, J.), aff’d, 565 U.S. 1104 (2012) (upholding federal
statute banning certain aliens from engaging in election-related expenditures, on the
ground that “[t]he Supreme Court has long held that the government (federal, state, and
local) may exclude foreign citizens from activities that are part of democratic self-govern-
ment in the United States”).
Either font of federal authority can independently support the validity and preemp-
tive nature of 18 U.S.C. § 611(a). See NFIB v. Sebelius, 567 U.S. 519, 561 (2012) (up-
holding individual mandate as a tax even though it violated the Commerce Clause); Edye
v. Robertson, 112 U.S. 580, 594-95 (1884) (even if head count payment violated Taxing
Clause, it was a proper exercise of immigration authority). That is true even if there are
some unconstitutional applications of the law. See United States v. Raines, 362 U.S. 17
(1960). But 18 U.S.C. § 611(a) constitutionally applies to situations where aliens are vot-
ing in federal elections even if they are authorized to do so under state law. Thus, 18 U.S.C.
§ 611(a) has preemptive effect like any other federal law, especially in the plenary realm

LEGAL STANDARD
To obtain a preliminary injunction, the moving party must establish that it is “likely
to succeed on the merits,” that it is “likely to suffer irreparable harm in the absence of
preliminary relief,” that “the balance of equities tips in [its] favor,” and that “an injunction
is in the public interest.” CTIA - The Wireless Ass’n v. City of Berkeley, 928 F.3d 832, 841
(9th Cir. 2019) (quoting Winter v. Nat. Res. Def. Council, Inc., 555 U.S. 7, 20 (2008)). In
Secretary Weber expressed “concern[]” that the Federal Government may be violating a court
order preventing it from using data. Ex. B at 3 (citing Common Cause v. DOJ, No. 1:26-cv-01352-SLS
(D.D.C.)). That concern is misplaced. In Common Cause, the court granted summary judgment and in-
validated a purported “voter list maintenance policy” under which the Civil Rights Division requested
States’ unredacted voter rolls, compared those rolls against a federal database called Systematic Alien
Verification for Entitlements, and asked States to remove ineligible voters. Id., Dkt. 51 at 9. The court’s
order, however, “[did] not prevent the federal government from taking lawful steps to support states’
efforts to maintain accurate voter rolls.” This case did not originate under that policy. Nor is this matter
seeking voter removals. The accusation that the Federal Government violated a court order is thus merit-
less. See also id., Dkt. 57 at 13–14 (explaining the material factual and legal differences between the
voter rolls efforts at issue in Common Cause and those at issue in the present litigation).

ARGUMENT
I. The United States Has Standing and May Seek Equitable Relief
A. Article III requires a plaintiff to “have (1) suffered an injury in fact, (2) that is
fairly traceable to the challenged conduct of the defendant, and (3) that is likely to be
redressed by a favorable judicial decision.” Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330, 338
(2016). When considering standing, courts must “accept as valid” the merits of the claim,
Fed. Election Comm’n v. Cruz, 596 U.S. 289, 298 (2022); accord Missouri, 114 F.4th at
985, meaning that this Court must analyze standing against the backdrop of California
facilitating unlawful voting in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 611. For two reasons, “the [United
States] has standing to sue to enjoin enforcement of preempted state” actions. KalshiEX
LLC v. Johnson, 830 F. Supp. 3d 907, 910 (D. Ariz. Apr. 10, 2026) (collecting cases); see
also Safe Sts. All. v. Hickenlooper, 859 F.3d 865, 898 (10th Cir 2017) (“The Supreme
Court has reaffirmed time and again that the United States is empowered to enforce the
supremacy of federal law against preempted State action, and that it may obtain an injunc-
tion to that effect.”) (emphasis added).
First, “[i]t is beyond doubt” that a complaint “asserts an injury to the United States”
if it alleges an “injury to [U.S.] sovereignty arising from violation of its laws.” Vermont
Agency of Nat. Res. v. United States ex rel. Stevens, 529 U.S. 765, 771 (2000); Arizona v.
United States, 567 U.S. 387 (2012) (affirming order granting preliminary injunction be-
cause Arizona violated federal law); Arizona v. Yellen, 34 F.4th 841, 851–53 (9th Cir.
2022) (recognizing a “sovereign injury” to the United States). When state officials act in
a way that “impugn[s] the United States’ sovereignty, a personal injury to the United States
arises.” LUPE, 604 F. Supp. 3d at 527; see also Cruise Lines Int’l Ass’n, Inc. v. Suganuma,
15

ID #:933
825 F. Supp. 3d 1170, 1192 (D. Haw. 2025) (finding standing because “the Federal Gov-
ernment has an interest in enforcing its statutes and maintaining federal supremacy”).
Courts thus hold that “the United States’ sovereign interests are harmed when its laws are
violated.” United States v. Idaho, 623 F. Supp. 3d 1096, 1107 (D. Idaho 2022). That is
especially true when a “domain of federal authority,” such as immigration, is “undermined
by impermissible state” action. United States v. Alabama, 691 F.3d 1269, 1301 (11th Cir.
2012).
Here, the United States has sovereign authority to regulate alien voting in federal
elections. See Bluman, 800 F. Supp. 2d at 283; Arizona, 567 U.S. at 394. Congress exer-
cised that authority by criminalizing noncitizen voting in federal elections. 18 U.S.C.
§ 611. California impedes this authority by sending and tabulating vote-by-mail ballots to
aliens it knows are likely to be noncitizens who are participating in the federal election.
The clash between state officials knowingly allowing likely noncitizens to vote in a federal
election and federal law prohibiting such voting is a “concrete harm to [the United States’]
sovereignty” to regulate alien voting in federal elections. LUPE, 604 F. Supp. 3d at 526;
see also Arizona, 567 U.S. at 406 (finding that state action “would interfere with the care-
ful balance struck by Congress” in a preempted field). Such unlawful voting also dilutes
lawful voting and unduly influences who runs the Federal Government—which is about
as direct of an injury to the United States as one can imagine. Compl. ¶ 10.
Second, “[i]nterference with the federal government’s interest in enforcing federal

85; accord United States v. Ekblad, 732 F.2d 562, 563 (7th Cir. 1984) (finding “standing
to seek relief from actual or threatened interference with the performance of its proper
governmental functions”). The United States has alleged such interference: “by sending
ballots to likely noncitizens despite the Federal Government notifying California of seri-
ous concerns,” California has “made it nearly impossible for the United States to investi-

A. California Cannot Knowingly Facilitate Unlawful Voting in Violation of 18

1. Under the Supremacy Clause, the “Constitution, and the Laws of the United
17
Case 2:26-cv-12025-DOC-ADS Document 62-1 Filed 10/21/26 Page 28 of 41 Page
ID #:935
States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof . . . shall be the supreme Law of the
Land[.]” U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2; Arizona, 567 U.S. at 399 (“The Supremacy Clause pro-
vides a clear rule that federal law” is supreme over state law). Federal preemption princi-
ples ensure the constitutional supremacy of federal law. Under those principles, contrary
“state law must yield to the regulation of Congress within the sphere of its delegated
power.” Savage v. Jones, 225 U.S. 501, 533 (1912). And federal law can preempt state
law “[e]ven without an express provision for preemption.” Crosby v. Nat’l Foreign Trade
Council, 530 U.S. 363, 372 (2000). That includes where “state law stands as an obstacle
to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress,” id.
at 373 (citations omitted) (cleaned up), or otherwise “directly conflict[s]” with federal law,
Kansas v. Garcia, 140 S. Ct. 791, 808 (2020) (Thomas, J., concurring). State action must
“give way” when it conflicts with “a considered judgment” embodied in federal law. Ari-
zona, 567 U.S. at 399, 405.
Such conflicts can arise in different ways and through different forms. One through-
line, though, is that States cannot avoid preemption by facilitating a private party to violate
federal law. “It is . . . axiomatic that a state may not induce, encourage or promote private
persons to accomplish what it is constitutionally forbidden to accomplish.” Norwood , 413
U.S. at 465. For example, if a state purported to subsidize federal crimes, that would con-
flict with the considered judgment of Congress. See, e.g., Musta v. Mendota Heights Den-
tal Ctr., 965 N.W.2d 312, 321-27 & n.11 (Minn. 2021) (federal law preempted a require-
ment that an employer subsidize the purchase of a federally prohibited drug), cert denied,
142 S. Ct. 2834 (Mem.) (2022).
When evaluating the obstacle that state action poses to federal aims, courts “ex-
amin[e] the federal statute as a whole and identify[] its purpose and intended effects[.]”
Crosby, 530 U.S. at 373. To illustrate, when a State “authorizes [persons] to engage in
conduct that the federal [law] forbids, it ‘stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and
execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.’” Mich. Canners and Freezers
18
Case 2:26-cv-12025-DOC-ADS Document 62-1 Filed 10/21/26 Page 29 of 41 Page
ID #:936
Ass’n, Inc. v. Agricultural Marketing and Bargaining Bd., 467 U.S. 461, 478 (1984) (quot-
ing Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941)). At bottom, obstacle preemption exists
wherever a state “undermines the intended purpose and natural effect” of a federal statute.
Crosby, 530 U.S. at 373 (internal quotation marks omitted).
These preemption principles apply when state officials act in a manner that obstructs
or conflicts with federal law. “It has long been a settled principle that federal courts may
enjoin unconstitutional action by state officials.” Puerto Rico v. Branstad, 483 U.S. 219,
228 (1987) (emphasis added) (citing Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908)). Accordingly,
whether state and local officials “act[] through . . . procedures rather than a court order or
a general law . . . is irrelevant to [the] analysis.” Golden State Transit Corp. v. City of Los
Angeles, 475 U.S. 608, 614 n.5 (1986). Preemption does not turn on the form of state
action. It “turns on the actual content of [a state’s] policy and its real effect on federal
rights.” Livadas v. Bradshaw, 512 U.S. 107, 119 (1994) (citing and adopting Livadas v.
Aubry, 987 F.2d 552, 561 (9th Cir. 1991) (Kozinski, J., dissenting) (“The point is, the
Commissioner is applying state law and his own policy in a way that impinges on protected
federal rights.”).
Courts have thus held that federal law preempts a wide range of conflicting state

6 III. The United States Faces Irreparable Harm Absent Preliminary Relief
For three reasons, the United States will suffer irreparable harm absent injunctive
relief. First, irreparable harm necessarily results from the operation of preempted state
action. See California, 173 F.4th at 1069 (granting injunction pending appeal because vi-
olation of Supremacy Clause was irreparable); Valle del Sol v. Whiting, 732 F.3d at 1029
(finding irreparable harm based on a Supremacy Clause violation); United States v. Ari-
zona, 641 F.3d 339, 366 (9th Cir. 2012) (same), aff’d in part, rev’d on other grounds, 567
U.S. 387 (2012). The United States also suffers a “per se irreparable harm” when state
actions violate the Supremacy Clause. United States v. Texas, 557 F.Supp.3d 810, 821
(W.D. Tex. 2021) (collecting cases); Daimler, 2025 WL 3049944, at *17 (same).
Second, the “inability to enforce its duly enacted [law] clearly inflicts irreparable
harm on” the United States. Abbott v. Perez, 585 U.S. 579, 602 n.17 (2018); see also Mar-
yland v. King, 567 U.S. 1301, 1303 (2012) (Roberts, C.J., in chambers) (same). The Su-
preme Court has recently and repeatedly found that the Federal Government would be
irreparably harmed if it could not enforce federal law. See, e.g., Dep’t of Homeland Sec.
v. D.V.D., 145 S. Ct. 2153 (Mem.) (2025); Noem v. Doe, 145 S. Ct. 1524 (Mem.) (2025);
Noem v. Nat’l TPS All., 145 S. Ct. 2728 (Mem.) (2025); see also Hollingsworth v. Perry,
558 U.S. 183, 190 (2010) (per curiam) (must show irreparable harm to obtain a stay).
Because California is frustrating the United States’ ability to effectively enforce 18 U.S.C.
§ 611—which is a priority—the United States would suffer irreparably absent an injunc-
tion. Cf. Trump v. CASA, Inc., 606 U.S. 831, 859–60 (2025) (recognizing that the Federal
Government “will suffer irreparable harm” if it is prevented from “enforcing its policies”).

IV. The Balance of Equities and the Public Interest Weigh in the United States’
Favor
This action seeks to protect the interests of the United States, so the burdens that
will result without injunctive relief are directly tied to the public benefits that will be pro-
tected if this Court issues the requested injunction. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 435
(2009). That is all the more true given the longstanding “recognition that preventing a
violation of the Supremacy Clause serves the public interest.” United States v. California,
921 F.3d 865, 893–94 (9th Cir. 2019). Indeed, the Ninth Circuit has long held that “plain-
tiffs who “establish[ ] a likelihood that [a] policy violates the U.S. Constitution . . . have
also established that both the public interest and the balance of the equities favor a prelim-
inary injunction.” California, 173 F.4th at 1069 (quoting Ariz. Dream Act Coal. v. Brewer,
757 F.3d 1053, 1069 (9th Cir. 2014)).
Moreover, injunctive relief will preserve the United States’ sovereign authority.
“Frustration of federal statutes and prerogatives are not in the public interest[.]” Alabama,
691 F.3d at 1301; United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Coop., 532 U.S. 483, 496–
98 (2001) (courts of equity must respect Congress’s policy choices as set forth in a statute);
Am. Trucking Associations, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles, 559 F.3d 1046, 1060 (9th Cir.
2009) (similar). Because the challenged conduct here frustrates the purpose of 18 U.S.C.
§ 611 to prevent noncitizen voting, allowing California to proceed would not be in the
public interest. By contrast, an injunction will not burden California, as it does not have
the authority to permit noncitizen voting. So an injunction would maintain that status quo.
On the other side, California has expressed concern about administering the United
States’ request to set aside vote-by-mail ballots cast by likely noncitizens. See Ex. B at 3.
Those concerns cannot stand as an impediment to administering an election free from
