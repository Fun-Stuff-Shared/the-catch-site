The record is incomplete in eight consequential respects.

1. **The envelope dispute began with North Carolina's voter-ID law, not the 2024 memo.** The two-envelope system was introduced in 2023 because the state's photo-ID requirements, enacted in 2018 and made enforceable in April 2023, required voters' identification documents to remain separate from their ballots. Previously, mail voters used a single return envelope. The Board's September 2023 guidance addressed how to handle the new system, and its August 2, 2024 declaratory ruling expressly upheld that interpretation. This matters because the dispute arose from the interaction of an older voter-ID mandate with a newly introduced envelope system—not simply from administrators relaxing an established two-envelope rule in 2024. **Source:** [State Board's February 2, 2026 declaratory ruling](https://s3.amazonaws.com/dl.ncsbe.gov/State_Board_Meeting_Docs/Orders/Declaratory%20Rulings/NCSBE%20Declaratory%20Ruling%2002022026%20-%20Absentee%20Voting%20Errors.pdf).

2. **The record omits the political and legal mechanism that gave Republicans control of the Board—and a consequential development in that fight.** After Republicans failed to secure a different elections-board appointment structure in 2023, the legislature overrode Gov. Roy Cooper's veto of Senate Bill 382 in December 2024. That law transferred appointment authority from the governor to Republican State Auditor Dave Boliek. His May 2025 appointments gave Republicans 3–2 majorities on the state Board and all 100 county boards. On September 16, 2026, a divided Court of Appeals upheld the transfer; **on October 21, Stein petitioned the state Supreme Court to review that decision**. This is the direct explanation for why the Board reversed its earlier position, and it establishes that the authority behind the new majority remains contested in separate litigation. **Sources:** [Court of Appeals decision in Stein v. Hall](https://law.justia.com/cases/north-carolina/court-of-appeals/2026/25-652.html); [Stein's Supreme Court petition](https://statecourtreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-10/stein-petition.pdf).

3. **The Wasserberg settlement did not require the Board to change its interpretation.** The chronology presents Republican litigation and a consent judgment leading to the February 2026 reversal, but leaves out what the agreement actually required. The December 4, 2025 settlement required reconsideration of the Republicans' petition; it **expressly did not oblige the Board to alter its reading of the statutes**. The Republican committees themselves acknowledged this in their October 6, 2026 federal filing. The distinction changes whether readers understand the September rule as a change compelled by a court settlement or a substantive choice made by the newly constituted Board after reconsideration. **Sources:** [Republican committees' memorandum, pages 5–6](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-10-06-MTI-RNC-Memo.pdf); [Board's February 2026 declaratory ruling](https://s3.amazonaws.com/dl.ncsbe.gov/State_Board_Meeting_Docs/Orders/Declaratory%20Rulings/NCSBE%20Declaratory%20Ruling%2002022026%20-%20Absentee%20Voting%20Errors.pdf).

4. **The challengers' principal legal case, and the precedents the judge relied on to reject it preliminarily, are missing.** The brief describes their argument largely as a complaint that late technical changes cost votes. Their federal case is more specific: they argue that discarding a ballot for an unsealed envelope violates the **Civil Rights Act's materiality provision**, which restricts denial of voting rights for paperwork errors unrelated to voter qualifications. The Board and Republican intervenors argue that the provision concerns **who qualifies to vote**, not **how a qualified voter must cast a ballot**. Judge Schroeder addressed that distinction directly, relying on Third and Fifth Circuit decisions in finding the challengers unlikely to prevail. The challengers also raised equal-protection, due-process and undue-burden claims. Omitting the legal theories leaves the reader with an incomplete account of what the pending lawsuit can decide and why the judge found the plaintiffs unlikely to win, independently of election timing. **Source:** [October 19 federal opinion, especially pages 35–41 and 48–52](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-10-19-Opinion-and-order.pdf).

5. **The matrix treats two potentially different measures as a single historical trend.** The U.S. Election Assistance Commission's primary 2022 data explicitly defines North Carolina's final **2.2%** as *mail ballots rejected divided by mail ballots returned*. The Board's interim **2.6% in 2022 and 3.1% in 2026** are described as *spoiled ballots*—a category that can include ballots reissued and subsequently replaced. The underlying Board calculations have not been published. Consequently, the decline from 2.6% during 2022 voting to 2.2% after the election does **not**, by itself, demonstrate that early-returned ballots are rejected more often than later ones; it may partly reflect how temporary spoils are resolved and classified. This weakens the matrix's confirmation of explanation C. It does not establish that the Board's **same-stage** 2022 and 2026 figures are incomparable, but that equivalence also remains unaudited. **Sources:** [EAC's 2022 North Carolina data brief](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2023-10/2022_EAVS_Data_Brief_NC_508c.pdf); [PolitiFact's October 23 account of the Board figures](https://politifact.com/factchecks/2026/oct/23/josh-stein/fact-checking-steins-claims-about-mail-in-ballot-rejections-in-north-carolina/).

6. **The matrix rules out more than the statewide totals can establish.** The fact that North Carolina had **1,349** spoiled ballots at this stage in 2026 versus **1,829** in 2022 disproves a claim that more ballots had already been spoiled *in total* than at the comparable 2022 point. It does **not** test how many additional 2026 ballots were spoiled **because of the new sealing requirement, compared with what would have counted under the previous rule**. That is a different counterfactual, and the court's account confirms that some ballots spoiled in 2026 would have counted under the former policy. Stein's claim that the statewide rate doubled remains unsupported, but the lower gross count cannot independently rule out a substantial additional burden attributable to the rule. Treating those propositions as interchangeable makes the evidence matrix more conclusive than its data permit. **Sources:** [October 19 federal opinion](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-10-19-Opinion-and-order.pdf); [Democracy Docket's October 20 report](https://www.democracydocket.com/news-alerts/exclusive-thousands-of-mail-ballots-set-to-be-rejected-after-gop-imposed-strict-new-rules-data-shows/).

7. **A missing direct county account materially strengthens the evidence that sealing is driving rejections outside Wake County.** On October 22, Mecklenburg County Elections Director Michael Dickerson told WFAE that, as of the preceding day, **259 ballots had been rejected specifically because the inner envelope was unsealed**, and only **60** affected voters had successfully voted again. The record lists this report as an unlocated lead and instead uses WBTV's roughly 250 rejections attributed to unspecified “signing requirements,” with 50 voters recasting. The direct statement establishes both the reason and the replacement outcome in a second large county, without relying on volunteer estimates. It changes the evidentiary basis for whether Wake's experience is isolated. **Source:** [WFAE, October 22: Mecklenburg elections director's figures](https://www.wfae.org/politics/2026-10-22/hundreds-of-mecklenburg-county-mail-in-ballots-rejected-under-new-rule).

8. **The deadline's legislative origin and its effect on replacement ballots are missing.** Senate Bill 747, enacted over Cooper's veto in 2023, eliminated the former **three-day post-election receipt grace period** for qualifying mailed ballots. The current November 3 deadline therefore leaves voters whose original ballots are spoiled late in the election with less time to receive and return a replacement than they previously had. Moreover, the Board's September 2026 instructions require a **complete new absentee package**, including resubmission of photo-ID documentation or an exception form, even if the voter supplied it with the first ballot. These requirements change the practical meaning of the assertion that a spoiled ballot is not a lost vote: a legal opportunity to vote again is not equivalent to a ballot ultimately counted, particularly close to Election Day. **Sources:** [Session Law 2023-140, amendments to G.S. 163-231](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/SessionLaws/HTML/2023-2024/SL2023-140.html); [State Board Numbered Memo 2021-03, section 3.2](https://www.ncsbe.gov/blog/2021/06/11/numbered-memo-2021-03-absentee-container-return-envelope-deficiencies).
