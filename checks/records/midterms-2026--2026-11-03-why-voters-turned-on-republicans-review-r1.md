The record is incomplete. The most consequential gaps concern what the polls actually establish about causation, the turnout-versus-switching question, several answers the matrix discounts without testing, and developments after Election Day.

### Findings

**1. The deciding issue ranking has a serious competing measurement.** The brief treats the five prompted “crucial” responses as its principal test of why Republicans lost. But the Economist/YouGov survey of 3,166 registered voters, conducted November 4–6, asked voters an open-ended question about their *single most important reason* for voting as they did. Party loyalty ranked first at 25%, followed by opposition to Trump at 13%, the economy at 12%, candidate qualities at 7%, government corruption and democracy at 5%, and foreign policy and war at 2%. This does not invalidate the SSRS figures: the questions measure different things. It does establish that calling an issue “crucial” is not equivalent to identifying it as the principal reason for a vote. Party loyalty is also a serious answer missing from the explanation matrix. The distinction directly affects the story's central conclusion.

Source: [Economist/YouGov, November 4–6 survey toplines](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/econtoplines_5a6y297.pdf).

**2. A published survey tests whether voters blamed the Iran war for rising prices—the central distinction the brief calls untestable.** A tabulation of the Narrative Strategies/535 News survey reports that, when respondents could select two causes of rising costs, 44% selected the Iran war, 38% tariffs and 30% gasoline prices. Among Republicans, 33% selected the war. The survey covered 1,006 registered voters, fielded October 24–November 3, and was weighted in part to the SSRS exit poll. Its underlying Politico-hosted PDF could not be independently retrieved during this review, so those figures retain a provenance qualification.

Separately, an Institute for Global Affairs/YouGov survey fielded October 29–November 2 found 44% of adults less likely to support a candidate who backed the war and 56% preferred avoiding higher domestic prices to fully achieving U.S. military objectives. Those are not validated explanations for individual voting decisions, but they test the war-to-prices mechanism more directly than ranking the war and affordability separately. The fourth-place “crucial” ranking therefore cannot rule out the war as a major upstream cause of price-driven voting.

Sources: [Narrative Strategies/535 News survey PDF](https://www.politico.com/f/?id=000001a1-b247-def8-a5f7-f25f8fc30000); [published poll tabulation and methodological qualifications](https://members.rationalground.com/every-poll); [Institute for Global Affairs/YouGov findings](https://instituteforglobalaffairs.org/2026/11/iran-war-poll-pre-election/).

**3. Turnout versus switching has a more direct test than the record acknowledges.** The brief leaves that question substantially to future voter-file research. The November 4–6 Economist/YouGov survey also included registered voters who **did not vote** in 2026. It found that 78% of self-identified Democrats voted Democratic, compared with 64% of Republicans who voted Republican. Among respondents reporting a 2024 Trump vote, 65% voted Republican in 2026, 6% voted Democratic, and the remainder either abstained or gave another response. The survey found substantial Republican nonparticipation, especially among non-MAGA Republicans. These self-reports are not validated turnout histories, but they directly support an asymmetric-turnout explanation alongside switching. The brief's deciding matrix currently relies almost entirely on a voters-only sample to infer who stayed home.

Source: [YouGov's analysis of 2026 voters and nonvoters](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/55923-independents-broke-strongly-for-democratic-candidates-in-2026-congressional-elections-november-4-6-2026-economist-yougov-poll).

**4. The two principal surveys materially disagree about 2024 nonvoters.** The SSRS row says only **4%** of 2026 voters had not voted in 2024. The NORC-based Washington Post analysis says **about 15%** of 2026 voters had not voted in the preceding presidential election. The brief includes both figures but does not confront the eleven-point discrepancy. Nor can its statement that the surveys independently support the same turnout story resolve it. Since the share of newly participating voters is central to explanation G, the disagreement affects more than precision: it changes the estimated importance of mobilizing people who sat out 2024. Neither figure is an administrative turnout count, and the underlying survey definitions and weighting need reconciliation before treating either as established.

Sources: [Washington Post/NORC analysis](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/11/04/ten-key-factors-behind-americans-midterm-votes/); [SSRS final methods statement](https://ssrs.com/wp-content/uploads/The-Voter-Poll-by-SSRS-Methodology-Statement_Final.pdf).

**5. Trump's account of immigration and the election changed substantially on November 8.** In his *60 Minutes* interview, Trump said immigration enforcement had both helped and hurt Republicans. He specifically blamed deporting people who had jobs and no violation beyond entering the country illegally, acknowledged that the policy might have been “too tough,” and said that ultimately hurt him. He also gave “bad messaging” as an explanation for the Republican defeat, arguing that the administration failed to communicate economic achievements. These are direct statements from the principal Republican actor, not interpretations by his critics.

They matter because K is evaluated chiefly through how voters who prioritized enforcement voted. That does not test whether other voters opposed the administration because enforcement went too far. The interview introduces both a stronger version of K and a distinct explanation concerning Republican messaging.

Source: [CBS News, full November 8 interview transcript](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/donald-trump-2026-post-midterm-elections-interview-60-minutes-transcript/).

**6. Explanation J is discounted by evidence that cannot establish its verdict.** The matrix cites CBS's observation that democracy has long concerned Democratic voters, then concludes that democracy voters “were Democrats already.” The cited passage does not identify how those particular 2026 voters voted in 2024. A group voting Democratic now could include consistent Democrats, Trump-to-Democrat switchers and newly mobilized voters. Without that cross-tab, prior Democratic allegiance cannot be inferred from current issue priorities. This matters because democracy was the second-ranked *single top issue* in the SSRS questionnaire, yet the verdict discounts its potential effect on switching or turnout without evidence distinguishing those possibilities.

Source: [CBS News, 2026 exit-poll analysis](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/cbs-news-exit-polls-midterm-elections-2026/).

**7. Explanation F is also ruled down by a test that does not address its mechanism.** The fact that 91% of participating 2024 Trump voters supported Republican House candidates in 2026 cannot establish that candidate weakness did not materially contribute to Republican losses. It does not measure candidate evaluations among people who abstained, nor does it test particular Senate candidates or the effect of Trump's endorsements. In the separate Economist/YouGov survey, 7% spontaneously identified candidate qualities as their most important voting factor, while 49% of registered voters said Trump's campaign rallies hurt Republican candidates. These figures do not prove candidate weakness caused the national result, but they leave it a serious explanation rather than a hypothesis defeated by House-party retention alone.

Sources: [Economist/YouGov survey](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/econtoplines_5a6y297.pdf); [YouGov post-election analysis](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/55922-understanding-big-democratic-midterms-win-2026-election-november-4-6-2026-economist-yougov-poll).

**8. The redistricting verdict omits both the governing decision and the district results that test it.** The matrix treats Democratic victories in statewide races as evidence against redistricting explaining the swing. Statewide results can rule out redistricting as a *complete* explanation of the election; they cannot establish that altered boundaries made no difference to the House result.

The omitted chain begins with Trump's 2025 push for Texas to redraw its map and the Democratic response in California. It includes the Supreme Court's April 29, 2026, decision in *Louisiana v. Callais*, which significantly narrowed voting-rights challenges to district maps, followed by further redraws. Nine states held House elections under new maps. The resulting contests can already be examined: Democrats held four of five seats targeted by Texas Republicans' redraw, while Republicans did gain individual seats in redrawn districts, including Tennessee's 9th. California's new map also contributed Democratic pickups. These are actual tests of H's House-seat mechanism, not hypothetical mobilization effects.

Sources: [Congressional Research Service, Louisiana v. Callais](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11431.html); [Roll Call, district-by-district results updated November 10](https://rollcall.com/2026/11/04/redistrcting-republican-house-seats-midterm-elections/).

**9. Trump's acceptance of the results no longer supports explanation N's stated evidentiary test.** On November 9, Trump publicly questioned whether the election had been honest, raised unsupported suspicions about early-vote batches, and suggested voting machines were involved. This followed his election-night acceptance. There remains no substantiated evidence that widespread fraud changed the outcome, and the absence of a broad wave of court challenges remains relevant. But the matrix expressly uses Trump's acceptance as evidence against an irregular-count narrative. That particular basis ceased to hold before November 11. The distinction matters because it changes the losing side's documented reaction, not because Trump's suspicions establish wrongdoing.

Source: [Washington Post, November 9, reproducing Trump's public statements](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/11/09/trump-casts-doubt-democrats-historic-midterm-wins/).

**10. The Iran-war lineage lacks the events that explain the price increase and the positions of both sides.** The record begins the war in February and follows gasoline prices, but the intervening chain is indispensable to interpreting those prices. A Congressional Research Service history documents the February 28 U.S.-Israeli strikes, Iran's attacks on shipping and efforts to restrict traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, an April ceasefire, a June memorandum intended to end the conflict, its collapse, and renewed fighting. The disruption to a major oil-and-gas shipping route provides the mechanism connecting military decisions to consumer energy prices.

The same report establishes the administration's stated aims: eliminating threats from Iran's missiles and navy and preventing a nuclear weapon, with Trump also discussing the possibility of regime change. The Congressional Budget Office estimated U.S. military operations had cost **$38 billion by August 1**. Without this chain, the war appears mainly as an unpopular February decision followed by expensive gasoline; the evidence instead describes a continuing military, diplomatic and economic crisis whose costs and objectives were contested throughout the campaign.

Source: [Congressional Research Service, U.S. Conflict with Iran, September 18, 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R48887.html).

**11. The tariff lineage is missing a Supreme Court ruling and the replacement policies in force during the election.** On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court rejected the administration's use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose tariffs. The administration then imposed a temporary 10% tariff under Section 122 on February 24; that expired July 24. On July 24, the U.S. Trade Representative imposed tariffs of 10–12.5% under Section 301 on goods from more than 80 countries. The Congressional Budget Office estimated the effective tariff rate had fallen from about 15% in November 2025 to 10% by July 2026.

This changes how explanation C is understood. “Trump's tariffs” were not one unchanged policy throughout the period of rising prices. The legal authority, coverage and rates changed substantially, while Trump's justification remained protection of American industry. The record correctly separates voters' perceptions from measured economic causation, but without the actual tariff sequence, readers cannot determine which policies the rival explanations concern.

Source: [Congressional Budget Office, August 20, 2026](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62704).

**12. The health-cost explanation lacks the policy sequence and measured consequences necessary to evaluate it.** Enhanced Affordable Care Act premium tax credits originated in pandemic relief legislation, were extended by the Inflation Reduction Act, and expired at the end of 2025 after Republicans blocked an extension. The July 2025 reconciliation law also tightened ACA eligibility and enrollment rules. Republicans argued at the time that the enhanced credits encouraged higher insurer charges and improper enrollments; that stated rationale is missing from the record.

KFF found that average Marketplace premium payments increased **58%** in 2026, average deductibles increased **37%**, and effectuated enrollment fell from **21.8 million in 2025 to 19.2 million in 2026**. Those outcomes affect Marketplace enrollees, not every voter, but they establish that a major contemporaneous affordability shock existed independently of gasoline. Explanation M is consequently more than a poll showing dissatisfaction with health costs: it has a traceable legislative cause and measurable consequences.

Source: [KFF, October 6 midterm update on ACA marketplaces](https://www.kff.org/elections/midterm-election-update-the-affordable-care-act-marketplaces/).

**13. The leading corruption finding is missing evidence of what voters were reacting to.** The record establishes that corruption ranked first among the five prompted “crucial” factors, but does not test the underlying concern. CBS's reporting of the consortium poll found that approximately six in ten voters believed Trump put his personal interests ahead of the country's. That connects the abstract corruption response to a judgment about the president's conduct, although it does not establish which particular transactions or controversies caused that judgment.

The distinction matters because a concern about presidential self-interest is different from generalized frustration with corruption in government, and either interpretation could imply a different reason for the Democratic advantage. It also bears directly on the separate democracy-and-overreach answer, which cannot be adequately assessed from issue rankings alone.

Source: [CBS News, national exit-poll findings](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/cbs-news-exit-polls-midterm-elections-2026/).

**14. November 10–11 developments materially change the account of what Democratic control means next.** A Reuters/Ipsos poll published November 10 found **71%** of adults wanted Congress to pass legislation ending the Iran war, including 51% of Republicans, and **62%** supported repealing Trump's tariffs. That directly connects the two central disputed causes of the election to voters' demands of the incoming Congress.

On November 11, reporting established that White House counsel Will Scharf had warned incoming House Oversight leader Robert Garcia that certain investigations and subpoenas could cross into criminal conduct. Garcia rejected the warning. Democrats were preparing inquiries concerning the president's financial interests, the Epstein investigation, immigration enforcement and other administration actions. This is already a concrete confrontation over the congressional oversight powers Democrats gained, not merely a future possibility. At the same time, Senate procedural requirements and presidential veto authority mean congressional majorities will not automatically enable Democrats to end the war or repeal tariffs. The stakes therefore extend beyond the remaining election counts to an active dispute over governing power, economic policy and accountability.

Sources: [Reuters/Ipsos, November 10 poll](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/most-americans-want-new-democratic-congress-end-trumps-iran-war-reutersipsos-2026-11-10/); [Associated Press, November 11 oversight confrontation](https://apnews.com/article/12acd4e233792f687b80eb9636aea18d); [Reuters, November 10 congressional leadership decisions](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/week-after-huge-election-losses-republicans-choose-their-leaders-congress-2026-11-10/).
