## Findings

**The record is incomplete.** The most consequential gaps concern the Army's prior knowledge of deteriorating lead azide, the legal standard governing detonation, the grounds of the lawsuit, and what residents can actually expect on Monday.

### 1. The Army knew about deteriorating lead azide at this plant more than 20 years before the March 2026 discovery.

A March 21, 2014, U.S. Army publication states that a research program begun in **2004** responded to Defense Department concerns about a **40-year-old stockpile of deteriorating lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant**.

This materially changes the lineage. The problem did not first enter the military's institutional awareness in March 2026. The earlier concern was serious enough to motivate research into replacement explosives.

The source does not establish whether that stockpile consisted of the same 68 drums scheduled for destruction. That connection remains unverified.

Source: [U.S. Army, March 21, 2014](https://www.army.mil/article/122127/Picatinny_aims_for_green_with_new_type_of_primary_explosive/). [www.army.mil](https://www.army.mil/article/122127/Picatinny_aims_for_green_with_new_type_of_primary_explosive/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 2. Question 4's matrix improperly treats the absence of early plume maps as the absence of earlier public warnings.

On October 8, the Army publicly announced that it intended to destroy deteriorating lead azide through controlled detonation in November. It identified the explosive hazard, explained why transportation was considered unsafe, and warned of potential window damage and temporary evacuation.

The Army had also publicly identified the material as hazardous waste in July.

These records do not establish that people throughout the eventual plume area received direct notification. They do, however, invalidate the matrix's categorical inference that general notice of the danger began November 15.

The distinction matters because Telle's claim contains two separate propositions: that the Army communicated about the explosive danger from March, and that it warned the **modeled impact area** from March. The evidence does not justify treating them as a single proposition that has been conclusively disproved.

Sources: [Army announcement, October 8](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/576739/us-army-officials-iowa-army-ammunition-plant-will-host-public-open-house-oct-15th); [Army storage announcement, July 10](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/printable/569717). [DVIDS](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/576739/us-army-officials-iowa-army-ammunition-plant-will-host-public-open-house-oct-15th)

### 3. The legal precedent governing the Army's choice of detonation is missing.

EPA explains that it **prohibited open burning and open detonation of hazardous waste in 1980**, subject to an exception for explosives that cannot safely be treated another way.

EPA also states that operators must demonstrate and periodically redemonstrate their eligibility for that exception and use safe alternative technologies where available.

This changes the significance of the alternatives dispute. The question is not merely whether environmental groups prefer a different treatment. It concerns the legal conditions under which open detonation is permitted.

The 1987 Subpart X environmental performance standards and EPA's subsequent guidance are part of the regulatory chain behind the November emergency permit.

Source: [EPA, Explosive Hazardous Wastes](https://www.epa.gov/hwpermitting/explosive-hazardous-wastes). [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/hwpermitting/explosive-hazardous-wastes)

### 4. The record omits a distinct, central ground of the lawsuit: the permit's environmental performance standards.

The November 19 emergency motion explicitly challenges EPA's alleged failure to incorporate the environmental performance standards of **40 CFR 264.601** and to impose conditions adequate to protect people and the environment.

The groups argue that the permit does not adequately address releases into air, water and soil; effects on crops and animals; or the information needed to evaluate those effects.

This is distinct from the three grounds identified in the plan: alternatives, public comment and notice.

Its omission changes the case from a dispute principally about whether the Army considered safer technology into a broader challenge to **whether the authorized detonation and its safeguards satisfy the applicable permitting requirements**.

These are allegations in the filing, not established violations.

Source: [Emergency motion, November 19, pages 11–18](https://earthjustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/2026-11-19-emergency-motion.pdf). [Earthjustice](https://earthjustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/2026-11-19-emergency-motion.pdf)

### 5. Question 1 incorrectly rules out operational urgency as an influence on the choice of treatment.

The matrix rules out answer C because the Army submitted alternatives information in August and September, before the approaching cold weather.

But the Army's environmental assessment explicitly identifies **“Mission Compatibility and Timeline”** as one of its five criteria for determining whether an alternative is viable.

The stated objective includes lifting facility-wide access restrictions and resuming medium- and large-caliber ammunition production as quickly as possible.

That establishes that operational timing was part of the Army's selection criteria. The August and September dates do not eliminate it.

The available record does not establish that speed outweighed explosive safety. It also does not support ruling out speed as a causal consideration.

Source: [Army environmental assessment, section 2.2.1](https://d34w7g4gy10iej.cloudfront.net/pubs/pdf_78364.pdf). [DVIDS Media CDN](https://d34w7g4gy10iej.cloudfront.net/pubs/pdf_78364.pdf)

### 6. Question 2's rejection of the 100-mile concern improperly narrows the groups' actual claim.

The matrix correctly distinguishes airborne lead from lead deposited on surfaces. However, it uses that distinction to rule out the groups' description of a plume affecting communities up to 100 miles away.

Their court filing explicitly identifies **two different risks**: modeled deposition extending approximately 25 miles in the later forecast, and airborne dispersion extending more than 100 miles.

The Earthjustice release likewise says lead-containing dust could travel 100 miles **through the air**.

The evidence rules out an assertion that the maps forecast surface deposition across the entire 100-mile distance. It does not rule out the groups' separate concern about potential inhalation exposure across that distance.

That distinction changes the geographic extent of the potential exposure being contested.

Sources: [Emergency motion, pages 13–14](https://earthjustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/2026-11-19-emergency-motion.pdf); [Earthjustice statement, November 19](https://earthjustice.org/press/2026/groups-sue-to-halt-planned-detonation-at-ammunition-plant-in-iowa). [Earthjustice](https://earthjustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/2026-11-19-emergency-motion.pdf)

### 7. A subsequent emergency-management notice invalidates the brief's geographic prediction for Monday.

Linn County Emergency Management issued an update after the postponement stating that the earlier Army atmospheric models were based on the weather expected for **Friday, November 20**.

It explicitly warned:

> Previous forecast maps should not be relied upon to determine potential impacts associated with the rescheduled operation.

The record's opening assertion that Monday's blast will send lead dust **north over southeastern Iowa** therefore goes beyond the available forecast evidence.

The previous maps establish what was projected for Friday, not the direction or extent of Monday's plume. This also affects which communities may need protective measures.

Source: [Linn County Emergency Management, November 19 postponement update](https://linncounty-ema.org/index.asp?DE=69CE87EA-8E07-4DC6-AFEA-792AF9FC6053&SEC=B970B908-1B12-422F-93BB-29606907EE06). [Linn County EMA](https://linncounty-ema.org/index.asp?DE=69CE87EA-8E07-4DC6-AFEA-792AF9FC6053\&SEC=B970B908-1B12-422F-93BB-29606907EE06)

### 8. Iowa's public-health instructions contain an important exposure-duration discrepancy and still show the canceled date.

Iowa Health and Human Services' fact sheet, dated November 20, recommends that potentially affected residents keep windows and doors closed for **24–48 hours after detonation**, unless subsequent guidance changes that precaution.

The Army's November 15 announcement, by contrast, said the dust was expected to linger for **no more than one hour**, depending on wind speed.

These statements need not represent incompatible scientific estimates—the state's precaution may be deliberately more conservative—but they represent substantially different protective instructions.

Both Iowa HHS's general fact sheet and its school and child-care guidance also continued to identify **Friday, November 20, around noon** as the planned detonation time when checked, despite the Army's November 19 postponement.

That matters to the record's account of public communication and what residents and schools are being instructed to do.

Sources: [Iowa HHS public-health fact sheet, November 20](https://hhs.iowa.gov/media/19822/download?inline=); [Iowa HHS school guidance](https://hhs.iowa.gov/media/19826/download?inline=); [Army November 15 advisory](https://www.army.mil/article/296772/iaaap_public_advisory?utm_source=chatgpt.com). [Health & Human Services](https://hhs.iowa.gov/media/19822/download?inline=)

### 9. The 24-hour sampling requirement is materially narrower than the five-sentence account indicates.

The EPA permit requires the Army to initiate soil and surface-wipe sampling within 24 hours at specified parks, public facilities and properties for which it has obtained entry rights.

But that requirement is geographically limited to locations **inside the post-detonation modeled deposition area of at least 100 micrograms per square foot**.

The permit separately models a wider area receiving at least **5 micrograms per square foot**.

The distinction matters because readers could otherwise understand the 24-hour requirement as covering all parks and consenting homes in the affected plume area.

This finding concerns that specific mandatory sampling provision; it does not establish that no other environmental monitoring can occur outside the narrower area.

Source: [EPA emergency permit, Condition IV.U.2.ii and Appendix B](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-11/iaaap-emergency-permit-for-treatment-and-storage-final_redacted.pdf). [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-11/iaaap-emergency-permit-for-treatment-and-storage-final_redacted.pdf)

### 10. The claimed signed November 18 finding of no significant impact is not supported by the publicly posted primary document.

The Army's publicly available *Mitigated Finding of No Significant Impact* ends with blank signature and date lines for installation commander Lt. Col. Daniel V. Nosse.

The document recommends that the commander approve and sign the finding. Its publication on November 18 does not itself demonstrate that he did so.

This changes the provenance of the record's assertion that a **signed FONSI** existed on November 18.

The publicly posted document is evidence of the Army's environmental conclusions, but not, on its face, evidence that the decision document was executed. A separate signed copy may exist; one was not verified.

Source: [Army's published Mitigated Finding of No Significant Impact, page 6](https://d34w7g4gy10iej.cloudfront.net/pubs/pdf_78363.pdf). [DVIDS Media CDN](https://d34w7g4gy10iej.cloudfront.net/pubs/pdf_78363.pdf)
