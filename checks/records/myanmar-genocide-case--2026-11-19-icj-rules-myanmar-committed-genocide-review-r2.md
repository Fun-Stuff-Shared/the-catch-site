## 1. The case has not ended; the compensation phase remains pending

**What is wrong:** Sentence 3 says the November 19 judgment “ends a case The Gambia brought in 2019.” The judgment ends the merits phase, but the court expressly reserved the determination of compensation and other reparations for a subsequent phase.

Paragraph 625 requires Myanmar to submit a report within two years. Paragraph 626 does not terminate the proceedings, and paragraph 625 anticipates further judicial consideration of compensation. The court also states that its findings on responsibility cannot be reopened during the reparations phase.

**Why it matters:** The genocide finding is final and cannot be appealed, but the litigation is not finished. The court still has a role in determining remedies, even though it lacks independent enforcement powers.

**Source:** [ICJ judgment, paragraphs 625–626](https://www.refworld.org/sites/default/files/2026-11/178-20261119-jud-01-00-en.pdf). [Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/sites/default/files/2026-11/178-20261119-jud-01-00-en.pdf)

## 2. The ordered remedies are substantially broader than the record conveys

**What is missing:** Paragraph 615 of the judgment identifies five categories of restitution, not merely return and compensation.

Beyond facilitating return and family reunification, Myanmar must return or replace lost property, help locate missing people and recover and rebury victims' remains, provide physical and psychological rehabilitation, and protect Rohingya from discrimination and persecution.

Paragraph 616 expressly includes protection against discrimination in accessing citizenship and freedom of movement.

**Why it matters:** The plan correctly establishes that the court declined to order citizenship for every Rohingya. But the distinction is more consequential than that: the court still imposed an obligation concerning discriminatory access to citizenship.

The ruling also creates obligations concerning land, rehabilitation, missing relatives and discrimination that are independent of the eventual monetary award. These are significant parts of what compliance would require.

**Source:** [ICJ judgment, paragraphs 603–604 and 615–617](https://www.refworld.org/sites/default/files/2026-11/178-20261119-jud-01-00-en.pdf). [Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/sites/default/files/2026-11/178-20261119-jud-01-00-en.pdf)

## 3. The dissent's position is materially different from agreeing that soldiers committed “genocidal acts”

**What is wrong:** Sentence 4 says the six dissenters agreed that Myanmar's soldiers committed genocidal acts in three villages.

Their joint dissent accepts that Myanmar's security forces committed acts constituting the material elements (*actus reus*) of genocide under Article II(a) and (b), particularly killings and serious bodily or mental harm.

It does not accept that those acts constituted genocide, because the judges concluded that genocidal intent was not established.

**What is also missing:** Their disagreement goes beyond whether evidence from three villages established a nationwide pattern.

The dissent specifically challenges the majority's interpretation of destruction under the Genocide Convention. It argues that the judgment effectively broadens the meaning of destruction by attaching too much significance to displacement, discrimination and the destruction of a group's social foundations.

It also argues that the majority's two-stage examination of genocidal intent effectively shifts the burden onto Myanmar to establish a competing explanation.

**Why it matters:** The distinction between proving prohibited physical acts and proving genocide is the central legal dispute. The dissent argues both that the evidence was insufficient and that the majority changed the method by which intent is established.

That latter objection is particularly relevant to the ruling's implications for other genocide cases.

**Source:** [ICJ summary, joint dissent, paragraphs 4–10 and 15](https://www.refworld.org/sites/default/files/2026-11/178-20261119-sum-01-00-en.pdf). [Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/sites/default/files/2026-11/178-20261119-sum-01-00-en.pdf)

## 4. The matrix overstates unanimity on the counter-insurgency explanation

**What is wrong:** Question 3 concludes that Myanmar's counter-insurgency explanation was ruled out by all fifteen judges.

The majority rejected genuine counter-insurgency as an alternative explanation for the conduct established by the evidence. But it acknowledged a geographical and temporal correlation between ARSA attacks and military operations.

The six dissenters likewise accepted that serious atrocities went beyond military necessity. However, paragraph 22 of their joint dissent specifically treats ARSA's activities as relevant context, observing that the clearance operations ended relatively soon after the threat posed by ARSA had been eliminated.

**Why it matters:** Agreement that counter-insurgency cannot justify atrocities is not equivalent to unanimous rejection of counter-insurgency as a genuine component of Myanmar's military campaign.

The majority's conclusion is that counter-insurgency does not reasonably explain the totality of Myanmar's conduct without genocidal intent. The dissent does not reach that conclusion.

The matrix's evidence does not establish the stronger proposition that all fifteen judges ruled out the presence of a genuine counter-insurgency objective.

## 5. Myanmar's own investigations and genocide legislation are missing from the account of its defense

**What is missing:** Myanmar did not simply deny that serious crimes occurred.

Its government-appointed Independent Commission of Enquiry concluded in January 2020 that war crimes, serious human rights violations and violations of domestic law occurred during the 2017 operations. It found reasonable grounds to believe Myanmar security personnel were involved, while rejecting the existence of a genocidal plan.

Myanmar also conducted military prosecutions.

The court records that seven military personnel convicted over the killing of ten Rohingya men at Inn Din received ten-year sentences but were pardoned by Min Aung Hlaing after serving eight months.

Myanmar additionally introduced genocide-specific legislation in 2021.

The court found that legislation deficient because it excluded birth-prevention measures authorized by existing laws and did not adequately implement other offenses under Article III, including conspiracy and incitement.

**Why it matters:** These findings explain two independently important aspects of the judgment.

First, Myanmar's defense included an account in which serious crimes occurred but did not establish genocidal intent.

Second, the court did not find that Myanmar had undertaken no investigations or legislative measures. It found that the measures taken did not fulfill the Convention's requirements.

The inadequate investigations, pardons and legislative exceptions explain why the court found continuing violations and ordered additional action.

**Source:** [ICJ judgment, paragraphs 539–548 and 570–574](https://www.refworld.org/sites/default/files/2026-11/178-20261119-jud-01-00-en.pdf). [Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/sites/default/files/2026-11/178-20261119-jud-01-00-en.pdf)

## 6. The record converts a limitation of the judgment into an affirmative finding about present-day genocide

**What is wrong:** The plan says the court declined to order a cessation of violence because it “found the genocide not ongoing.”

Paragraph 595 is narrower. The court says genocide and related incitement have **not been found to be ongoing**. It expressly limits that determination to the cessation of violations established in the case.

This is not an affirmative determination that genocide has ceased everywhere in Myanmar or that subsequent conduct cannot amount to genocide.

**What is missing:** The UN's 2026 human rights reporting documents continuing atrocities against Rohingya in Rakhine State by both the military and the Arakan Army.

An August 2026 OHCHR report describes arbitrary detention, torture, sexual violence, forced recruitment, confiscation of land and renewed displacement.

**Why it matters:** The court's inability to order cessation of genocide on the evidence before it does not establish that Rohingya are safe or that subsequent atrocities have been adjudicated.

The distinction matters especially because the judgment concerns 2017, while the proposed remedies require returns to territory where serious abuses continue.

## 7. The lineage omits the institutional chain that produced The Gambia's lawsuit

**What is missing:** The record moves from the 2017 operations directly to The Gambia's November 2019 filing, without the intervening decisions that explain why the case was brought.

The missing chain includes:

- **August–September 2018:** The UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission called for senior Myanmar generals, including Min Aung Hlaing, to be investigated and prosecuted for genocide.
- **2018–2019:** The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation established an ad hoc committee chaired by The Gambia. In February 2019, that committee adopted a plan involving ICJ litigation; the OIC subsequently endorsed the plan.
- **May 2019:** The OIC's Makkah summit called on the Gambian-led committee to initiate proceedings against Myanmar.
- **Jurisdictional distinction:** Bangladesh, the country directly receiving the refugees, had entered a reservation requiring the parties' consent for an Article IX dispute to be submitted to the ICJ. The Gambia had no corresponding reservation.

**Why it matters:** The Gambia did not independently emerge as a litigant merely because the Convention permits any party to invoke another state's responsibility.

The case followed a specific sequence of UN investigations and OIC decisions. Bangladesh's treaty reservation also helps explain why the country most directly affected by the refugee influx was not the applicant.

Those events are essential to understanding the origin of this unusual interstate case and Myanmar's subsequent objection that The Gambia was acting as the OIC's proxy.

## 8. Bangladesh announced a specific post-judgment diplomatic initiative on November 20

**What is missing:** The record lists Bangladesh's proposed 12–18-month repatriation timeline among unadmitted leads, stating that it could not be verified at the source.

The Bangladesh Sangbad Sangstha published a direct report on November 20 confirming that State Minister for Foreign Affairs Humaiun Kobir announced the initiative.

Bangladesh intends to intensify discussions with the UN and bilateral partners to establish conditions allowing Rohingya to return safely within 12–18 months.

Kobir expressly conditioned that objective on adequate security arrangements in Rakhine State.

Reuters separately reported the initiative on November 20, while emphasizing the continuing obstacles to repatriation.

**Why it matters:** This is a concrete governmental response to the judgment from the country hosting approximately 1.2 million Rohingya refugees.

It does not constitute a repatriation agreement or establish that returns will occur within that period. Nevertheless, it is a newly announced diplomatic objective directly relevant to the judgment's implementation.

It also establishes that Bangladesh intends to pursue negotiations involving actors beyond the government subject to the court's order.
