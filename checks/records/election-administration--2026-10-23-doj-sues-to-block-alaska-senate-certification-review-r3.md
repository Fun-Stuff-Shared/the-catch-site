## Findings

**The record is incomplete.** The central distinction between stopping certification after a particular hypothetical result and blocking the election itself is supported by the complaint. The remaining gaps concern the earlier litigation, the legal basis for the suit, the people responsible for the ballot design, and what an injunction would interrupt.

### 1. The earlier court ruling rejected more than an allegation of voter confusion.

**What is missing or wrong:** The June 26 Alaska Superior Court order did not merely find insufficient evidence that Daniel J. Sullivan intended to confuse voters. It held that the Division of Elections had imposed a new, legally unauthorized *good-faith candidacy* requirement. The judge also found that the division accepted Republican Party allegations while disregarding Sullivan's explanations.

Those explanations included that his previous party, the Alaskan Independence Party, had dissolved; that he said he switched to the Republican Party because of his conservative views; and that he promptly corrected an initial request to appear as “Dan S. Sullivan” to “Dan J. Sullivan.” The court acknowledged that the original name request was concerning but found it insufficient to establish the alleged deception.

**Why it matters:** The federal complaint relies on an administrative finding that was vacated not just because its factual case was weak, but because the state lacked authority to impose the eligibility standard it used. The record's account makes the earlier judgment look narrower than it was. It also leaves Question 3 testing possible voter confusion without adequately testing the separate allegation of deliberately deceptive candidacy.

### 2. Question 4 omits relevant, binding appellate interpretation of the statute.

**What is missing or wrong:** On April 28, 2026, the Ninth Circuit decided *Public Interest Legal Foundation v. Nago*. In its opinion—not merely the case summary—the court explicitly characterized § 20501(b) as the National Voter Registration Act's **preamble**, describing its provisions as statements of congressional purpose. It distinguished those purposes from the Act's operative voter-registration requirements.

The record identifies this ruling as a lead but excludes it because the captured summary does not contain that language. The opinion itself does.

**Why it matters:** Alaska is in the Ninth Circuit. Although *Nago* concerned access to voter-registration records and did not decide whether § 20501(b)(3) authorizes an injunction against election certification, its interpretation directly bears on the Justice Department's theory. The matrix treats the department's claim of an enforceable duty and the opposing interpretation as equally unresolved without considering existing appellate authority that substantially favors the latter.

### 3. The record omits the governor's involvement in the ballot-design decision central to the suit.

**What is missing:** Public records obtained by Alaska's News Source show that Gov. Mike Dunleavy requested a June 29 meeting with Lt. Gov. Nancy Dahlstrom, Elections Director Carol Beecher and Acting Attorney General Cori Mills, hours after the Alaska Supreme Court reinstated Daniel J. Sullivan.

The timing matters because the June 26 trial-court order had expressly directed that Sullivan appear with his Republican affiliation. The Alaska Supreme Court subsequently left the precise ballot presentation to the Division of Elections, which printed him without a party label.

Dahlstrom later explained that the division wanted to **maximize differentiation** between the candidates. Daniel J. Sullivan publicly objected that the resulting design favored the incumbent. The governor's actual instructions, if any, remain unknown; substantial portions of the relevant communications were redacted.

**Why it matters:** The ballot's distinctive labeling is the strongest concrete evidence against the Justice Department's confusion theory. The record treats it as a technical election-administration decision. The available documents establish that senior political officials were involved in the discussions that produced it, and that the other Sullivan disputed its fairness. They do not establish that Dunleavy ordered the particular design.

### 4. The ballot-removal effort had national legal backing months before the federal lawsuit.

**What is missing:** Attorneys general from **14 Republican-led states** submitted an amicus brief supporting Alaska's authority to exclude Daniel J. Sullivan during the June state Supreme Court litigation. The Honest Elections Project also supported the effort.

**Why it matters:** The lineage is not simply a state Republican complaint followed months later by Trump's direction to the Justice Department. A substantial interstate legal coalition had already mobilized around the same candidacy. This does not prove coordination with the October federal filing, but it establishes that the attempt to keep the namesake off the ballot was a nationally supported legal campaign well before the department intervened.

### 5. The timing understates how many votes were already beyond the reach of a ballot change.

**What is missing:** The Alaska Division of Elections reported **31,138 ballots received** for the November 3 general election as of **9:15 a.m. on October 23**. That included 20,438 early-voting ballots and 9,362 returned by mail.

**Why it matters:** The record says early voting was underway when the suit was filed, but the scale changes the practical significance. Tens of thousands of voters had already used the disputed ballot design before the court could act. The Justice Department's proposed post-election investigation therefore concerns an extensive body of already-cast ballots, not merely a potential problem with future voting.

These are ballots received statewide, not Senate votes counted or evidence of confusion.

### 6. The consequences of withholding certification are more substantial than the record establishes.

**What is missing:** Alaska Statute § 15.15.450 requires the elections director, after completion of the state ballot-counting review, to certify the winner and issue a certificate of election. Alaska also provides statutory recount and election-contest procedures, with deadlines tied to completion of the state review. The Division of Elections' own guidance describes recount applications as following certification.

**Why it matters:** The federal relief would interrupt a statutory obligation to declare and certify the winner, potentially complicating the established mechanisms for challenging the result afterward. That is materially different from an administrative delay with no effect on the state's prescribed post-election process.

It does not establish that those remedies would become permanently unavailable; their interaction with any federal injunction would depend on the order entered.
