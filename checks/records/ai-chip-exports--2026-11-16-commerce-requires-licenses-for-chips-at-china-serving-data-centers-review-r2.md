## Findings — November 17, 2026

### 1. Congress introduced legislation on November 17 because the new rule does not resolve the Megaspeed case.

Sen. Pete Ricketts announced the **MEGA Act** on November 17. The proposed legislation would immediately require licenses for transactions involving Megaspeed, subject to a presumption of denial, and require Commerce to decide within 30 days whether to add the company to the Entity List.

Ricketts explicitly distinguished the November 16 rule from action against Megaspeed, noting that Commerce had not designated the company.

**Why it matters:** The administration's response to the broader remote-access loophole has not resolved the specific company dispute that helped bring the issue to public attention. The central question of whether Commerce will blacklist Megaspeed remains open, and Congress is pursuing a separate intervention.

### 2. House investigators secured testimony from Huang and Nvidia's general counsel on November 17.

The chairmen of three House committees announced that former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and Nvidia General Counsel Tim Teter had voluntarily agreed to transcribed interviews.

Huang is scheduled for **December 1**, and Teter for **December 2**, before the House Foreign Affairs, Intelligence and China Select committees.

House Foreign Affairs Chairman Brian Mast also sent Commerce Under Secretary Jeffrey Kessler a new demand for information concerning Megaspeed, Speedmatrix, chip smuggling and export licenses.

**Why it matters:** The record's forward-looking account concentrates on Lutnick's December Senate appearance. The investigation has now secured testimony from Nvidia's former chief executive and its top lawyer, while a separate inquiry continues into Commerce's handling of export controls.

### 3. The administration's internal dispute has a longer and more specific history than the October approval.

Axios reported on November 11, citing six high-level sources, that Trump had approved action against suspected Nvidia-chip diversion on at least three occasions: **May, August and November 5, 2026**.

The report describes a November 5 Situation Room confrontation followed by an Oval Office meeting at which Trump again authorized action. It says Lutnick subsequently proposed returning with additional options rather than immediately executing the decision.

Commerce spokesperson Kristen Eichamer denied the reported confrontation between Bessent and Lutnick.

The distinction between these earlier decisions and the November 16 regulation matters: Axios describes decisions to act against suspected diversion, particularly Megaspeed, rather than establishing that the exact text of the November 16 rule was approved on all three occasions.

The May 2025 BIS promise is also narrower than the record's causal matrix assumes. BIS promised a replacement for the AI Diffusion Rule but did not specifically promise the November 2026 remote-access controls.

**Why it matters:** The record identifies prior planning but does not fully distinguish repeated alleged presidential decisions, Commerce's resistance to particular enforcement measures, and the development of this specific regulation. Those are different explanations for the delay.

Sources: [Axios, November 11 investigation](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/11/trump-lutnick-nvidia-china-chip-export-controls?lctg=601786508c8adc252e0e0c62&stream=top&utm_source=chatgpt.com) · [BIS, May 13, 2025 announcement](https://www.bis.gov/press-release/department-commerce-announces-rescission-biden-era-artificial-intelligence-diffusion-rule-strengthens?source=pmbug.com&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 4. The agency's affirmative defense of its Megaspeed enforcement record is excluded from the tested causal account.

The Commerce–Justice joint statement appears in the record's reactions, but its enforcement chronology is held out of the explanation of the administration's disagreement.

In a joint statement dated November 16, the agencies said Commerce initiated an investigation in 2024, requested Nvidia sales records in May 2025, conducted an on-site end-use check in June 2025 and subsequently transferred the lead role in the criminal investigation to Justice.

The agencies expressly defended Commerce's enforcement record under Lutnick.

**Why it matters:** There is a material difference between Commerce failing to investigate Megaspeed and Commerce investigating the company without placing it on the Entity List. The competing accounts concern the adequacy and timing of particular enforcement decisions, not whether all government action had been absent.

The agency's position does not resolve whether Lutnick resisted blacklisting, but it directly qualifies the broader allegation of inaction.

Source: [Commerce–Justice joint statement](https://www.justice.gov/opa/media/1472536/dl?inline=)

### 5. The rule raises an unresolved question about Commerce's authority to regulate remote computing services.

A January 13, 2009 BIS advisory opinion concluded that providing computational capacity, without transmitting controlled commodities, software or technology to the customer, was not itself subject to the Export Administration Regulations. The opinion recognized exceptions for certain prohibited end uses and activities.

The November 2026 rule takes a different operational approach: a change in the use of chips already installed overseas can constitute a regulated in-country transfer requiring authorization.

WilmerHale's November 17 analysis identifies a substantive legal uncertainty. It notes that Congress has not enacted the Remote Access Security Act, which would expressly expand export-control authority over remote access.

**Why it matters:** The November rule is not simply a conventional licensing requirement for physical chip shipments. It asserts regulatory authority over certain changes in how computing infrastructure is used after export.

That distinction matters to the rule's reach and potentially to its legal durability. No judicial determination invalidating the new approach was identified.

Sources: [BIS's January 2009 advisory opinion](https://www.bis.gov/media/documents/application-ear-grid-cloud-computing-services.pdf) · [November 2026 interim final rule](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf) · [WilmerHale legal analysis, November 17](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20261117-us-government-restricts-remote-access-to-advanced-ai-chips-and-formally-rescinds-the-ai-diffusion-rule)

### 6. The record understates which customers and intermediaries are covered.

New §744.24 extends beyond companies headquartered in restricted countries.

It also covers companies whose **ultimate parent** is headquartered in a restricted jurisdiction. Its treatment of computing provided *for* such companies can reach arrangements involving an intermediary that trains or supplies models on their behalf.

The 12-million-TPP threshold can aggregate capacity across digitally or physically linked data centers.

Separately, the exemption for U.S.-headquartered recipients in the five newly restricted countries applies to the destination-based licensing requirement, subject to reporting. It does not create a general exemption from the separate remote-access end-use control.

**Why it matters:** The rule can affect data centers serving customers that are not themselves headquartered in China, including intermediaries and subsidiaries. Overseas facilities operated by American companies are not necessarily exempt from the remote-access restrictions.

Source: [Federal Register public-inspection rule, §§742.6 and 744.24](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf)

### 7. The commercial-recommender exemption and the 90-day deadline contain unresolved contradictions.

Section 744.24(b)(2) excludes computing provided exclusively for commercial recommender systems when specified information is submitted to BIS.

That exemption could matter to online retailers and social-media companies—the categories in which some prominent Chinese technology firms operate. It does not cover generative AI systems.

However, the rule's preamble separately says certain existing operators providing access for commercial recommender systems may continue for 90 days but then must obtain a license.

WilmerHale identifies three unresolved implementation questions:

- Whether the 12-million-TPP threshold is measured separately for each customer or across customers.
- What constitutes significant computing power for the existing-service deadline.
- Whether the recommender-system exemption continues after the initial 90 days.

**Why it matters:** The record treats the threshold, exception and transition period as more definite than the operative text permits. These uncertainties affect which data centers require authorization and when continued operations become restricted.

Sources: [Federal Register rule](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf) · [WilmerHale, November 17 analysis](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20261117-us-government-restricts-remote-access-to-advanced-ai-chips-and-formally-rescinds-the-ai-diffusion-rule?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 8. The May 2026 guidance does not establish blanket permission for Chinese companies to continue renting restricted computing power.

The record interprets BIS's May 31 guidance as tolerating existing remote-access arrangements until the November rule's 90-day deadline.

The guidance is more limited. It expressly maintained the existing license requirement for items destined for companies headquartered in restricted jurisdictions, wherever those companies were located.

Its assurance about continuing operations applied to bona fide data-center operators **otherwise complying with the Export Administration Regulations**. It addressed ongoing use, storage, disposal and servicing of advanced computing items.

It did not expressly authorize otherwise prohibited transactions.

The November rule also states that operators whose existing arrangements violated the earlier end-user control should submit voluntary self-disclosures.

**Why it matters:** A 90-day transition for newly covered operations is not equivalent to retroactive authorization of every earlier arrangement. Some activities may already have required licenses before November 16, and potential past violations are not necessarily cured by the new deadline.

Sources: [BIS guidance, May 31, 2026](https://media.bis.gov/media/documents/bis-guidance-may-31-2026.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com) · [November 2026 rule, discussion of §744.23](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf)

### 9. The regulation adds a substantive manufacturing control that the account of its effects excludes.

The Federal Register rule includes changes affecting semiconductor foundries and outsourced semiconductor assembly and testing companies.

New §744.23(a)(3)(i)(C) establishes a license requirement for specified advanced-node integrated circuits designed by companies that are neither approved nor authorized IC designers and exported or transferred by covered manufacturers.

Related amendments narrow the circumstances in which manufacturers can overcome presumptions that particular chips are subject to advanced-computing controls.

These changes can apply to manufacturing transactions worldwide, independently of whether the chips are destined for the five newly listed countries or a data center serving Chinese firms.

**Why it matters:** The November 16 regulation is broader than the two licensing changes described in the five-sentence account. It also creates or tightens manufacturing-related obligations affecting foreign chip-production supply chains.

Unlike the rescinded Biden-era provisions that were not enforced, these are operative changes under the new rule.

Sources: [Federal Register rule, §§744.23 and 3A090](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf) · [Milbank, November 17 legal analysis](https://www.milbank.com/a/web/s3az6BJ7TYoxRunxpYE51X/grns-client-alert_bis-issues-new-rule-restricting-remote-access-to-advanced-chips-nov-2026.pdf)

### 10. November 19 is not an absolute cutoff for shipments already underway.

The rule contains a **30-day savings clause**.

Certain shipments that lose their eligibility for license-free export or a license exception may proceed under their previous eligibility if they were already en route aboard a carrier to a port of export, reexport or transfer on the publication date and the transaction is completed within 30 days.

This is separate from the 90-day deadline for existing computing arrangements.

**Why it matters:** The record correctly identifies the scheduled legal effective date but omits a material transition provision. Some transactions initiated before publication can be completed afterward without satisfying the newly applicable licensing requirements.

Source: [Federal Register rule, Savings Clause](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf)
