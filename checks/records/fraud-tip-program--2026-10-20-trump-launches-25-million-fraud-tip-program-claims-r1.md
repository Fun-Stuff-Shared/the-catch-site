### Findings

**C2 — [terms-amount-at-charging]**

**Claim:** “The program's written terms pay $10,000 to $30,000 per tip, at or around the time criminal charges are filed.”

**Record:** `doj-whip-terms-2026-10-20`

> “Should an individual meet the criteria below, including that the tip leads to criminal charges, he or she may be eligible for a reward of between $10,000 and $30,000, to be paid at or around the time charges are filed.”

The terms also state:

> “An individual is eligible for one award between $10,000 and $30,000 for each distinct tip.”

And:

> “Rewards are entirely discretionary and a reward is not guaranteed.” records records

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That the written terms establish a payment of $10,000 to $30,000 for each submitted tip that produces charges. In fact, awards are discretionary, eligibility is conditional, and the department determines whether multiple tips are distinct. The claim and its short form do not preserve those limitations.

**C3 — [terms-charges-defined]**

**Claim:** “Under the terms, a tip qualifies only if it leads the Justice Department to obtain and unseal an indictment, information or criminal complaint.”

**Record:** `doj-whip-terms-2026-10-20`

> “Criminal charges means the Department obtained and unsealed an indictment, information, or criminal complaint.”

But the immediately following provision states:

> “No reward is payable while the relevant criminal charge or charges remain under seal or otherwise non-public, unless the Department determines in its sole discretion that such a reward should be paid prior to unsealing.” records

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That unsealing is an absolute prerequisite to receiving an award. The terms expressly reserve discretion to pay before charges are unsealed. The claim's “only if” formulation excludes that exception, and its short form presents unsealing as an unconditional trigger.

It establishes the published terms, but not the subsequent observation asserted by the claim and its short form.

**C25 — [fca-share]**

**Claim:** “Under the False Claims Act, a whistleblower who brings a successful lawsuit receives 15 to 30 percent of the recovery.”

**Record:** `usc-31-3730`

The statute provides:

> “receive at least 15 percent but not more than 25 percent of the proceeds”

For cases the government does not pursue:

> “The amount shall be not less than 25 percent and not more than 30 percent of the proceeds”

However, the statute also provides an exception for certain actions based primarily on previously disclosed information:

> “the court may award such sums as it considers appropriate, but in no case more than 10 percent of the proceeds”

It separately permits reduced awards for people who planned and initiated violations and denies awards to certain convicted participants. records

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That every successful False Claims Act whistleblower lawsuit produces an award of at least 15 percent. The statutory exceptions allow smaller awards, including awards of no more than 10 percent in specified circumstances, and some successful cases may produce no award for the person who initiated the action.

The short form, “False Claims Act: 15 to 30 percent,” also presents the range without those exceptions.

**C38 — [fof-charges-since-2022]**

**Claim:** “More than 90 people had been charged since 2022 in the Feeding Our Future case before Shirley's video.”

**Record:** `npr-2025-12-31`

NPR reported on December 31, 2025:

> “more than 90 individuals have been charged since 2022 in the ongoing case of a Minnesota nonprofit that prosecutors say misappropriated some $250 million”

The same record states:

> “Nick Shirley, a 23-year-old self-described ‘independent YouTube journalist,’ posted the 42-minute video on X and YouTube the day after Christmas.” records

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That the record establishes more than 90 charges had already been filed by December 26, 2025, when Shirley published his video.

NPR's count is reported as of December 31, five days after publication. The record does not establish precisely how many of those charges preceded the video.

The distinction matters because the claim specifically uses the count to establish the investigative timeline before Shirley's reporting.

**C39 — [tipster-exposure]**

**Claim:** “The terms let a tipster's identity come out in court or go to other agencies, require the tipster to cooperate and testify, and pay no one whose identity is not verified.”

**Record:** `doj-whip-terms-2026-10-20`

On cooperation, the terms state:

> “An individual must cooperate with the Department in its investigation of related conduct and criminal or civil actions.”

They continue:

> “This includes but is not limited to providing truthful and complete testimony and evidence, whether in interviews, before a grand jury, or at any trial or other court proceeding”

The terms separately require production of evidence when called upon and proactive assistance if requested. records

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That every tipster must testify as a mandatory step in obtaining an award. The terms impose a cooperation obligation that can include testimony, but they do not establish that every tipster must testify in a proceeding.

The claim's provisions concerning disclosure of identity to defendants or other agencies, and verification of identity before payment, are supported. records
