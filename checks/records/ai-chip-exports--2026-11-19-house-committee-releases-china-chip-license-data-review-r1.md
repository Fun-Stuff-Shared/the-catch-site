3. **The analysis of the 1,888,950 chips returned without action omits several legally recognized explanations.** BIS's own regulatory definition says an application can be returned at the **applicant's request**, because a **license exception applies**, because the items are **outside Commerce's jurisdiction**, or because required information was not supplied. The brief considers ordinary paperwork deficiencies but not voluntary withdrawal or a determination that authorization was unnecessary. This matters because an RWA is not necessarily evidence that Commerce turned down an export. The matrix also incorrectly treats Alibaba's separate China-based RWA as ruling out an offshore-specific concern; BIS could have had different reasons for different returns. 4. **The offshore-data-center rule is materially more consequential than the brief establishes.** Commerce's interim final rule was **published and took effect November 19**, the day of the committee disclosure. It introduces destination-based licensing for advanced chips sent to **Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand**—precisely the three countries involved in the 1,118,500-chip offshore requests. It also controls significant remote computing access supplied to China-headquartered companies, subject to thresholds and exceptions, including commercial recommendation systems. These are distinct controls, and they operate prospectively: they cannot establish why BIS returned applications before May 11. Their practical importance is already evident: Alibaba's chief executive warned November 19 that the restrictions could affect its 2027 products and data-center expansion. 12. **The policy lineage starts before the December 2025 decision.** Commerce's **October 7, 2022** controls restricted China's access to advanced computing chips because of concerns about military modernization, weapons development and surveillance. The **October 2023** revisions explicitly sought to close ways around those restrictions. Trump's December 2025 decision to permit H200 sales was therefore a reversal of an established national-security policy, not the beginning of this licensing dispute. His administration's competing rationale was that controlled sales of older-generation American chips would preserve U.S. technological leadership and commercial influence while withholding newer processors. Without the original controls and both administrations' stated reasons, the licensing dispute appears narrower than the policy choice Congress is now examining.
