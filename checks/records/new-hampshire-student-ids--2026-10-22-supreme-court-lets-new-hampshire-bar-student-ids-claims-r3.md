### C11 — Short form misstates when the restriction took effect

**Claim:** “On April 23, Secretary of State David Scanlan's office announced that from June 2 student IDs could not be used as proof of ID to register, to request an absentee ballot, or to check in to vote.”

**Short form:** “Apr. 23: Secretary bars student IDs to register, vote absentee or check in”

**Record:** `sos-release-2026-04-23`

**Exact words:**
> “Effective June 2, 2026, student IDs cannot be used as proof of ID when registering to vote, requesting an absentee ballot, or checking in to vote in an election.” records

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** The short form makes April 23 sound like the date the restriction became operative. The record establishes that April 23 was the announcement date and June 2 was the effective date. The full claim is supported; the short form loses the timing distinction.

### C23 — Short form turns the state's position into an unqualified legal conclusion

**Claim:** “Someone registering at the polls on election day must prove identity and domicile, and also age and citizenship unless officials confirm they were already registered in New Hampshire; from October 22 the state says a student ID cannot be used to prove identity.”

**Short form:** “Registering: student ID does not count for identity”

**Records:** `sos-register-vote-2026-10-24`; `rsa-654-12`

**Exact words:**
> “Note: effective October 22, 2026, registrants cannot use student identification cards to prove identity.” records

The registration statute separately permits:

> “Any other evidence that reasonably establishes that it is more likely than not that the person is who they claim to be.” records

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** The short form presents the exclusion as an unequivocal determination of what the statute permits. The full claim carefully attributes the exclusion to the state's current position. The underlying legal question remains disputed, notwithstanding the Supreme Court's stay. The short form drops that distinction, which is central to the case.

### C36 — Short form removes the estimate's population and evidentiary qualifications

**Claim:** “The challengers' expert estimated that about 6.56 percent of eligible New Hampshire residents aged 18 to 24, about 7,747 people, lack a qualifying ID; he did not estimate how many of them hold a student ID.”

**Short form:** “About 7,747 aged 18 to 24 lack a qualifying ID”

**Record:** `dnh-orders-appendix-2026`

**Exact words:**
> “Among ‘young’ voters—which the plaintiff defines in this litigation as voting-eligible citizens between the ages of 18 and 24—Dr. Herron estimated that approximately 6.56% lack qualifying identification, representing approximately 7,747 individuals.”

The court continued:

> “Dr. Herron did not estimate how many of those individuals possess student identification cards.” records

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** The short form can be read as an established count of all New Hampshire residents aged 18–24 lacking qualifying identification. The record instead supplies an expert estimate for **voting-eligible citizens** in that age group. The short form also removes the attribution that identifies the figure as an estimate rather than a verified count.
