**The record is incomplete.** Eight findings materially affect the explanations, the legal history, or the account of what happened to voters.

1. **The legal requirement to establish that a voter moved is missing from the central causal test.** Mississippi Code §23-15-152 requires officials to have *reliable information* that a voter moved before sending the confirmation notice that triggers inactive status. But Experian’s own TrueTrace technical documentation defines its “Best Address” as the place a consumer is most likely to be reached, expressly says it does **not verify residency**, and acknowledges that a mailing address may differ from a residence. The question is therefore not only whether the data contained errors. It is whether a commercial contact address, without independent verification, satisfied the statute’s threshold for treating someone as having moved. The question 2 matrix treats the mailing of a notice as the decisive legal step but does not test the grounds for mailing it.

   Sources: [Mississippi Code §23-15-152](https://law.justia.com/codes/mississippi/title-23/chapter-15/article-3/subarticle-f/section-23-15-152/); [Experian TrueTrace FAQ, 2023](https://sos.ga.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/Experian%20FAQ.pdf). [Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/codes/mississippi/title-23/chapter-15/article-3/subarticle-f/section-23-15-152/)

2. **The lineage starts too late, and that omission affects the historical comparison.** Watson says his statewide push for more aggressive maintenance began in **January 2021**, when seven counties had registration totals exceeding their estimated voting-eligible populations. A similar confirmation-notice and inactive-status provision was proposed in **2022**, but a House floor amendment struck it from HB 1510. The Legislature enacted the mechanism in **2023**, effective January 2024. Supporters, including then-Senate Elections Committee Chair Jeff Tate, argued that inflated rolls presented opportunities for fraud; opponents, including Zakiya Summers, warned that the broadly defined authority could suppress legitimate voters. Federal law—the 1993 National Voter Registration Act and 2002 Help America Vote Act—supplies the baseline for maintenance and eventual removal, but did not require Mississippi to adopt this particular immediate-inactivation rule. Consequently, the record's 2016–2022 comparisons describe trends under an earlier state-law regime. They establish that the 2026 rise is unusual; they do not, by themselves, isolate Experian as its cause.

   Sources: [Watson's November 16 statement](https://www.sos.ms.gov/news/mississippis-election-integrity-held-strong); [2022 Mississippi House Journal](https://www.sos.ms.gov/content/documents/ed_pubs/pubs/H_Journal_2022_v9.pdf); [2023 legislative debate and positions](https://mississippitoday.org/2026/03/09/credit-data-checked-voters-addresses/); [EAC discussion of federal requirements](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/Voter_List_Maintenance_Study_508.pdf). [MS SOS](https://www.sos.ms.gov/news/mississippis-election-integrity-held-strong)

3. **The strongest documented precedent supporting commercial address data is absent from question 2.** The EAC report recounts Orange County, California's earlier use of Experian: across four data runs involving **401,000 voter records**, the county obtained **74,100 voter-confirmed information updates** after contacting voters. That is evidence that the product can help locate people whose registration information needs updating, not merely an untested vendor claim. It does **not** establish the accuracy of Mississippi's flags or the proportion of Mississippi voters who moved; Orange County sought confirmation rather than treating an address match as verified residence. Without this distinction, the matrix understates the substantive case for using the data while leaving its use for immediate inactivation untested.

   Source: [EAC Voter List Maintenance Study, August 2025](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/Voter_List_Maintenance_Study_508.pdf). [U.S. Election Assistance Commission](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/Voter_List_Maintenance_Study_508.pdf)

4. **Question 1 rules out a broader version of Watson's position than he actually advances.** Its answer B says the total accumulated over years and therefore there was *no spike*, then rejects B partly because Watson acknowledged the rise caused by integrating Experian data. His published defense instead disputes claims that **all 230,000** were newly inactivated or removed at once; he does not need to deny the 2024–2026 increase to make that point. The real unresolved dispute concerns how many additional inactivations resulted specifically from the commercial data, how counties reviewed the flags, and how many were justified. Rejecting an assertion that the cumulative total is not a one-time action does not settle those questions.

   Sources: [Watson's November 16 statement](https://www.sos.ms.gov/news/mississippis-election-integrity-held-strong); [Associated Press, November 19](https://apnews.com/article/7a9282987612197b43c73cf05f7e7d31). [MS SOS](https://www.sos.ms.gov/news/mississippis-election-integrity-held-strong)

5. **The statewide affidavit-ballot comparison can be tested against turnout; the matrix leaves that test open.** The University of Florida Election Lab estimates approximately **998,000 ballots cast in Mississippi in 2026**, compared with **1,250,000 in 2024**. Using the secretary of state's affidavit counts, affidavit ballots represented approximately **2.15% of 2026 turnout**, compared with **1.71% in 2024**—a roughly **26% higher rate** despite nearly identical raw counts. These are preliminary turnout estimates, and the calculation does not establish that Experian caused the increase. It does, however, materially weaken the inference that an unchanged statewide count demonstrates an unchanged voter experience.

   Sources: [University of Florida, 2026 turnout estimates](https://election.lab.ufl.edu/2026-general-election-turnout/); [University of Florida, 2024 turnout estimates](https://election.lab.ufl.edu/2024-general-election-turnout/); [Mississippi secretary of state's affidavit totals](https://www.sos.ms.gov/news/mississippis-election-integrity-held-strong). [UF Election Lab](https://election.lab.ufl.edu/2026-general-election-turnout/)

6. **Question 3 omits Watson's stronger explanation for the rejected ballots.** The matrix gives him the position that affidavit-ballot volume was normal and most ballots counted. His November 16 statement goes further: he asserts that the thousands of rejected affidavits were **improper votes that the system correctly prevented from counting**. That is a substantive rival explanation to the Democrats' concern that eligible voters lost votes. Neither the statewide acceptance percentage nor the cited county anecdotes establish which explanation accounts for the roughly 5,400 rejections. Without statewide rejection reasons tied to registration status, the record cannot test Watson's affirmative claim any more than it can establish the Democrats' suspected number of wrongly rejected votes.

   Source: [Watson's November 16 statement](https://www.sos.ms.gov/news/mississippis-election-integrity-held-strong). [MS SOS](https://www.sos.ms.gov/news/mississippis-election-integrity-held-strong)

7. **There is reported evidence of failure to provide the affidavit-ballot safeguard, not just a theoretical possibility.** The record acknowledges that voters who left without voting are excluded from the arithmetic comparing rejected affidavits with Scott Colom's 8,031-vote margin. But ACLU of Mississippi executive director Jarvis Dortch told Axios that **some voters were turned away instead of being offered affidavit ballots**. This is an attributed allegation, not an independently verified count. It nevertheless changes the significance of the margin test: the approximately 5,400 rejected affidavits are not an established upper bound on voters potentially prevented from voting. There is no evidence in the cited account that the uncounted group approached the Senate margin.

   Source: [Axios, November 6](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/06/mississippi-colom-hyde-smith-inactive-voter-registrations). [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/06/mississippi-colom-hyde-smith-inactive-voter-registrations)

8. **The November 19 demands extend beyond the proposed procedural changes described in the record.** In the Associated Press account updated after Thursday's news conference, caucus leaders also seek to **repeal or amend laws permitting third-party data to be used in determining voter eligibility**. That is a materially different potential remedy from Taylor's planned transparency requirements and opportunity for voters to respond before inactivation: it raises the possibility of restricting the underlying use of commercial data, including by the state's replacement vendor. The same account says lawmakers want the investigation and legislative changes completed before the **2027 elections**. This development is not captured by the record's account of what happens next.

   Source: [Associated Press report updated November 19, 3:30 p.m. EST](https://www.post-gazette.com/wire/mississippi-midterms-inactive-voters-P7FR2INU7JURVIOPHPTKA5AZDA/). [Pittsburgh Post-Gazette](https://www.post-gazette.com/wire/mississippi-midterms-inactive-voters-P7FR2INU7JURVIOPHPTKA5AZDA/)
