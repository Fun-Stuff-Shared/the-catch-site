**The record is incomplete.** The central finding—that the U.S.-held account did not transfer billions to the U.S. government—is supported. But the record conflates transfers out of custody with payments to final recipients, treats two different sets of Venezuelan financial data as one traceable flow, and rules out a broader corruption allegation on evidence that addresses only one possible mechanism.

### Findings

**1. The legal and political origins of the account are incomplete.**

The lineage begins with the January 9 executive order, but the arrangement was announced earlier. On **January 7**, the Department of Energy said Trump had announced a U.S.–Venezuela energy deal on January 6, that the U.S. had begun marketing Venezuelan crude, and that proceeds would be distributed for the benefit of **both Americans and Venezuelans**, at U.S. discretion. The January 9 order chiefly protected the funds against creditor attachment; the January 29 OFAC general license established important requirements governing payments into the designated accounts.

This changes the significance of Trump's claim that the oil benefited the United States. The custody ledger disproves direct transfers from that account to U.S. agencies, but it does not by itself resolve the broader arrangement or the administration's original stated objectives.

Sources: [Department of Energy, January 7](https://www.energy.gov/articles/fact-sheet-president-trump-restoring-prosperity-safety-and-security-united-states-and); [OFAC General License 46, Federal Register](https://stg2.ofac.treasury.gov/system/files/2026-05/fr91_24719vz.pdf).

**2. The $12.11 billion transferred out of custody was not all disbursed to final recipients by September 30.**

The report's master ledger shows **$12,092,621,673.35** in disbursements through its four listed mechanisms, plus **$15,000,526.20** still sitting in the Venezuelan central bank's operational account. That balance was restricted and required further written U.S. authorization before use.

Separately, **$192,855,200** of the banknote allocation was still awaiting authorization, as the record acknowledges. The account's stated **$168,558,474.25** ending balance also excludes **$50,000** remaining in the interim Qatar account.

The central claim about money leaving U.S. custody survives. The stronger implication that nearly all of it had already reached Venezuelan businesses, individuals, or the economy does not.

Source: [State Department/KPMG report, pp. 42–43](https://bitacoraeconomica.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Report-on-Venezuela-Custody-Account-Operations-January-September-2026.pdf).

**3. The $9.37 billion business and $2.87 billion individual totals cannot be attributed entirely to custody-account money.**

The report explicitly states that the central bank's operational account also received **$346 million withdrawn from Venezuela's IMF reserve tranche**. Downstream recipient reporting combines activity funded from that source with activity funded through the U.S. custody arrangement.

The two published recipient totals add up to approximately **$12.24 billion**, exceeding the **$12.11 billion** transferred out of custody. The report warns that custody transfers should not be expected to equal recipient-level disbursement data.

This is a material provenance problem in the answer to Question 1. The report describes downstream dollar distribution, but the specific amounts attributed to businesses and people are not an exclusively traceable accounting of the U.S.-held oil proceeds.

Source: [State Department/KPMG report, pp. 41–42 and 48–49](https://bitacoraeconomica.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Report-on-Venezuela-Custody-Account-Operations-January-September-2026.pdf).

**4. The recipient identities are withheld from the public, not absent from the underlying records.**

The report says Venezuelan banks submitted downstream transaction information to the central bank, which then provided it to State and KPMG. Recipients were classified using Venezuelan tax identification numbers.

This changes the evidence matrix's treatment of Question 1E. The public cannot determine which businesses received the dollars or how concentrated the allocations were, but the report does **not** establish that no recipient-level accounting exists. It establishes that the underlying information was available for government review and that the public version publishes aggregated results.

The distinction matters especially for the preferential-dollar explanation: the unpublished transaction data, rather than the published sector totals, are what could test concentration among favored buyers.

Source: [State Department/KPMG report, pp. 42 and 48–49](https://bitacoraeconomica.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Report-on-Venezuela-Custody-Account-Operations-January-September-2026.pdf).

**5. Rodríguez's payroll claim is neither mathematically equivalent to the reported receipts nor linked to the custody disbursements.**

Rodríguez reported **$15.603 billion received**, **$14.860 billion spent**, and **$14.710 billion** spent on wages and pensions. The last figure is approximately **99% of reported spending**, but only **94.3% of reported receipts**.

Her government's transparency portal also explicitly covers only the **Social Protection Fund and National Infrastructure Fund**, excluding other government funds and programs.

The more consequential gap is the missing connection between the two governments' figures. The State Department describes dollars sold through banks in exchange for bolívars; Rodríguez describes expenditures over a different reporting period. The record contains no reconciliation demonstrating that the bolívars generated by the custody-account interventions were the funds used for her reported payroll expenditures.

That leaves Question 1C and Question 1G plausible, but not verified as a continuous financial trail.

Sources: [Rodríguez's November 12 figures and the portal's stated scope](https://eldiario.com/2026/11/12/ingresos-petroleros-salarios/); [State Department/KPMG report](https://bitacoraeconomica.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Report-on-Venezuela-Custody-Account-Operations-January-September-2026.pdf).

**6. The audit contradiction is substantially stronger than the record establishes.**

On **June 3**, Secretary of State Marco Rubio personally told the House Foreign Affairs Committee that KPMG had been hired to audit every expenditure. Later in the same hearing, he said **every single disbursement was audited by KPMG** and agreed to provide that information to the committee.

The record relies primarily on lawmakers' secondhand quotation of Michael Kozak's April assurance. Rubio's direct testimony is stronger primary evidence of what the administration represented to Congress.

The distinction changes the significance of KPMG's November statement that its services **did not constitute audit or attestation services under professional standards**. This is not merely a discrepancy with an April description by a State official; it conflicts with a specific, repeated assurance from the secretary himself.

Sources: [June 3 House Foreign Affairs hearing transcript](https://hearinghighlights.com/hearings/119/house/house-foreign-affairs/2026-06-03/department-of-state-fy-2027-budget-request-a-commitment-to-america-first-foreign-policy); [State Department/KPMG report](https://bitacoraeconomica.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Report-on-Venezuela-Custody-Account-Operations-January-September-2026.pdf).

**7. The KPMG contract's financial provenance is stronger than the record indicates.**

The brief treats the contract as unavailable and relies on senators' description of an **$84 million award**. Federal procurement data identify State Department contract **19AQMM26F0357** and distinguish approximately **$84.4 million in potential contract value** from approximately **$14.9 million obligated**.

Those amounts are not interchangeable with the **$18.18 million** the custody ledger records as paid to KPMG. The discrepancy cannot be resolved from the public reporting alone, but neither can the $84 million properly be treated as money already committed or paid.

This matters because KPMG's compensation is part of the dispute over the independence and nature of its oversight.

Sources: [Federal contract record derived from USAspending data](https://primerfp.com/intel/contract/19AQMM26F0357); [USAspending federal award search](https://www.usaspending.gov/search?utm_source=chatgpt.com).

**8. The matrix improperly rules out the senators' broader politically connected payments concern.**

The matrix treats the **1.1% vendor-payment share** as evidence against Question 1F. That finding rules out **billions of dollars in direct vendor payments from this account during the reporting period**. It does not rule out the broader mechanism the senators actually identified.

Their September letter expressly warned that the administration could misuse its control by **encouraging or coercing Venezuela to request particular purchases or payments**, as well as by directly disbursing funds. Selection of oil marketers, transaction terms, preferential exchange allocations, and beneficiaries outside the vendor ledger are not tested by the vendor percentage.

The evidence supports a narrower exclusion than the matrix makes. It does not establish politically connected payments, but it does not eliminate the senators' stated concern.

Source: [Senate Banking Committee, September 17, quoting the senators' letter](https://www.banking.senate.gov/newsroom/minority/wyden-warren-whitehouse-press-trump-administration-over-revenue-from-venezuelan-oil-and-gold-deals).

**9. The record omits consequential congressional oversight already underway before the incoming Congress.**

On **September 15**, House Foreign Affairs Democrats attempted to subpoena documents concerning the U.S.–Venezuela oil arrangements; committee Republicans voted against the effort. On **October 2**, the committee's Democrats disclosed that State had supplied **one document** relating to the government's arrangement involving North American Blue Energy Partners, while other requested material remained outstanding.

Separately, Schumer and Schiff introduced the **Venezuela Oil Proceeds Transparency Act** on **February 11**, proposing a government audit of the U.S.–Venezuela energy deal.

These developments materially change the account of what has been tried, what the administration has already disclosed, and what remains contested. Oversight did not simply consist of unanswered letters followed by a November report and the prospect of Democratic control in January.

Sources: [House Foreign Affairs Committee Democrats, October 2](https://democrats-foreignaffairs.house.gov/2026/10/committee-democrats-issue-statement-on-state-department-s-partial-production-of-venezuela-oil-deal-documents?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [S. 3838, Government Publishing Office](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119s3838is).

**10. Congress reportedly received the report before its public release and before the Times story.**

Reuters reported that the KPMG document was **dated October 29** and, according to a State Department official, **transmitted to Congress in early November**. The Times reported on a confidential copy on November 10; State published the document on November 12.

The record correctly identifies November 12 as the first public release, but its chronology omits the earlier reported delivery to Congress. That distinction changes what the November publication represents: it was a public disclosure of a report that officials said lawmakers had already received, not necessarily the first accounting provided to Congress. The delivery date remains attributed to State rather than independently established.

Source: [Reuters, November 12](https://www.investing.com/news/economy-news/us-handled-12-billion-of-venezuelas-oil-revenues-report-says-5023141?utm_source=chatgpt.com).
