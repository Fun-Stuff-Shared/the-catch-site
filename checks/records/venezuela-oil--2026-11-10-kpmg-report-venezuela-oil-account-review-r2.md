### 1. The legal mechanism that created the oil-payment system is incomplete.

The brief attributes the requirement that oil buyers pay into a U.S.-controlled account to Executive Order 14373. The order establishes custody protections and controls over the funds, but the operative payment requirements also come from Treasury's Venezuela sanctions licenses.

OFAC General License 46C authorizes specified oil transactions otherwise prohibited by sanctions, on conditions that include depositing payments to blocked Venezuelan parties into designated funds or another Treasury-directed account. It expressly permits exceptions for certain local taxes, permits and fees.

**Why it matters:** The $12.26 billion is not simply oil revenue the United States collected after Maduro's removal. It represents a defined, sanctions-governed payment stream. The distinction affects both the scope of the account and why Washington controls it.

### 2. The matrix omits a substantial U.S. government financial interest outside the account.

The brief tests Trump's claim of financial gains against transfers into the U.S. Treasury, discounted oil purchases and the unpriced NABEP barrels. It does not account for the **35% equity interest in NABEP's corporate parent** that the White House announced on August 31.

The administration described that interest as potentially representing hundreds of billions of dollars in value and dividends. The same agreement gave the State Department rights to buy 20% of NABEP's output at production cost and first refusal on the remainder.

**Why it matters:** The account ledger rules out billions in direct transfers from that account to U.S. agencies. It does not rule out substantial U.S. economic interests obtained through separate oil arrangements. Conversely, the White House's projected value is not money already received or a demonstrated recovery of military costs. Question 1's answer B remains insufficiently tested outside the custody account, and answer F's test does not encompass potential benefits conferred through oil concessions.

### 3. A prior Trump statement directly addresses the missing destination of the money.

On **July 27**, a reporter asked Trump where the reported $13 billion taken in from Venezuela was going. Trump repeated that the war had been paid for many times over. When pressed about the destination, he said the money went toward running the country and, after the reporter specified the United States, added that it could go to the military and that Congress would have to approve it.

**Why it matters:** The November 11 quotation is ambiguous on its own, but it is not the administration's only explanation of the claimed financial gain. The July exchange connects the claim directly to an approximately $13 billion figure and raises the possibility of U.S. government spending. It does **not** establish that such spending occurred; it establishes a more specific presidential claim against which the account's actual disbursements can be tested.

### 4. The record does not adequately test whether dollar allocations favored a concentrated group.

The publicly available bank figures show that **five of the 20 participating banks received approximately 83% of distributed dollars**. More consequentially, since March 31 the central bank has allocated dollars at a fixed exchange rate it sets, rather than through the earlier competitive auctions. That mechanism accounted for approximately $9.32 billion.

The report does not disclose the transaction exchange rates or establish whether recipients obtained dollars below their market value.

**Why it matters:** Whether the allocations were advantageous to selected businesses depends partly on the rate paid, not merely on the destination of the dollars. The matrix leaves answer D unproven because buyer names are unavailable, but does not adequately test this separate, economically decisive variable. Concentration among intermediary banks is **not proof** of concentration among ultimate buyers; it does, however, identify an important concentration the current assessment does not examine.

### 5. The audit question has an omitted third possibility.

The report's vendor ledger records a **$155,000 payment to BDO for limited-assurance work covering May and June**. The available account does not include the review's results.

That does not substantiate Rubio's claim that KPMG audited every disbursement. Nor does limited assurance necessarily constitute a financial-statement audit. But the report's disclaimer about **KPMG's** professional services does not establish that no other firm performed independent assurance work.

**Why it matters:** Question 2 is framed as a binary choice between KPMG auditing the account and no audit. The separate BDO engagement requires distinguishing a comprehensive audit of disbursements from narrower independent assurance work. The categorical answer currently goes further than the cited KPMG disclaimer permits.

### 6. The military-cost claim lacks an available benchmark and the earlier chain of events.

The lineage begins with the January 3 capture of Maduro, without the preceding U.S. military buildup and operations. An April 23 study published through Brown University's Costs of War project estimates **at least $4.7 billion** in U.S. spending on operations Southern Spear and Absolute Resolve between **August 1, 2025, and March 31, 2026**.

The researchers describe the figure as partial. It includes operations across Venezuela, the Caribbean and the eastern Pacific, not just the January 3 raid; it is not an official Pentagon accounting.

**Why it matters:** The brief treats the cost of the war as wholly unquantified, even though an independently documented estimate exists. That estimate cannot establish what Trump meant or validate his claim. It does, however, give the claim a meaningful financial reference point and shows that the intervention was preceded by months of military expenditure rather than beginning on January 3.
