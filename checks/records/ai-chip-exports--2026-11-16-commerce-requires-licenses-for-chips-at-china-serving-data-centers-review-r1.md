**RECORD INCOMPLETE.** The review found material gaps in the rule’s legal scope, the policy decisions that preceded it, the evidence connecting the Megaspeed scandal to the rule, and developments on November 17.

### Findings

1. **The 12-million-TPP threshold does not establish that smaller arrangements are legal.** The first matrix marks the threshold as evidence that no license is needed below it. That inference does not hold. Other export controls remain applicable regardless of the new threshold, including controls involving Chinese-headquartered end users and the separate requirements for the five specified destinations. The five-country exclusion also depends on the ultimate consignee or end user being U.S.-headquartered and on reporting compliance—not merely on having an American buyer. This changes which transactions the rule permits and undermines part of the matrix’s reasoning. Sources: [November 16 interim final rule](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf); [BIS guidance, May 31, 2026](https://media.bis.gov/media/documents/bis-guidance-may-31-2026.pdf).

2. **A case-by-case license path does not establish that approval is likely.** The matrix correctly rejects a categorical ban under the new data-center provision. But the same rule retains a **presumption of denial** for many advanced-chip transactions involving Chinese or Macau destinations, headquarters, or ultimate parents. BIS says overlapping licensing policies must be applied. Consequently, some transactions may be considered under the new case-by-case provision while still facing a restrictive policy under another provision. The distinction matters because the five sentences contrast a ban with a licensing system without establishing how much access that system is likely to allow. Source: [Interim final rule, revised §742.6 licensing policies](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf).

3. **A direct predecessor to the 90-day deadline is missing.** On **May 31, 2026**, BIS expressly reaffirmed that the preexisting restrictions on advanced-chip exports for Chinese-headquartered entities abroad were still enforceable, despite the administration’s decision not to enforce the Biden AI Diffusion Rule. Crucially, it also said otherwise compliant data-center operators did **not** have to stop ongoing use, storage, disposal, or servicing until further notice. The November measure replaces that open-ended position with a deadline for covered existing services. This is a direct regulatory transition, not merely a return to controls abandoned in May 2025. Sources: [BIS guidance, May 31, 2026](https://media.bis.gov/media/documents/bis-guidance-may-31-2026.pdf); [November 16 rule](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf).

4. **The policy lineage omits a planned replacement and an important change in direct chip sales to China.** On **May 13, 2025**, BIS said it intended to issue a replacement for the AI Diffusion Rule and announced additional controls concerning Malaysia and Thailand. That establishes a policy process predating the Megaspeed revelations. Separately, on **January 13, 2026**, BIS moved Nvidia H200, AMD MI325X, and comparable chips to case-by-case licensing for approved Chinese customers, subject to conditions. The record’s categorical account of Chinese firms being barred from purchasing advanced chips since 2022 obscures this distinction between restricted sales and an absolute prohibition. Sources: [BIS announcement, May 13, 2025](https://www.bis.gov/press-release/department-commerce-announces-rescission-biden-era-artificial-intelligence-diffusion-rule-strengthens?f_link_type=f_linkinlinenote); [BIS announcement, January 13, 2026](https://media.bis.gov/sites/default/files/documents/DoC%20Revises%20License%20Review%20Policy%20for%20Semiconductors%20Exports.pdf).

5. **The November 16 rule also removes controls, rather than only adding them.** Beyond formally rescinding the unenforced AI Diffusion Rule, it removes the worldwide regional-stability licensing provision, eliminates the AI model-weights classification and associated foreign-direct-product control, and discards the previous country-tier and data-center authorization framework. These are substantive changes to the regulation's scope, not merely administrative cleanup. The five sentences present the event as a one-directional tightening; the full rule is a replacement framework combining new targeted restrictions with withdrawals of broader controls. Source: [Interim final rule, Sections II and IV](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf).

6. **The record conflates the Megaspeed Entity List dispute with the data-center rule.** Bessent’s November 13 statement that Commerce “has the pen” referred specifically to **placing Megaspeed on the Entity List**. It was not a statement about who drafted the broader overseas-data-center regulation. Axios also reported presidential decisions about action against suspected chip diversion in May, August, and November, and Commerce disputed part of its account of the interagency confrontation. These sources establish a related dispute over enforcement against one company, but they do not by themselves establish what caused the November 16 regulation to be published. BIS’s attribution of the rule to Treasury and the NSC is primary evidence of authorship; the claimed causal sequence remains less certain. Sources: [Axios, November 13](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/13/bessent-lutnick-megaspeed-nvidia); [Axios investigation, November 11](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/11/trump-lutnick-nvidia-china-chip-export-controls?lctg=601786508c8adc252e0e0c62&stream=top).

7. **The strongest congressional alternative is missing, leaving the U.S.-data-center gap insufficiently tested.** The House-passed Remote Access Security Act would give Commerce authority to regulate **remote access itself**, including access from abroad to chips physically located in the United States. That goes materially further than the November rule. In a November 13 letter, Democratic Sen. Lisa Blunt Rochester and Republican Sen. John Curtis explicitly urged geographically unrestricted controls and restrictions involving U.S. subsidiaries of Chinese companies. Their position is not simply support for tighter overseas export licensing: it is a competing answer to whether regulating chip shipments can close the loophole. This also establishes a serious congressional policy alternative without proving Congress compelled the November rule. Sources: [House-passed legislation](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr2683rfs/html/BILLS-119hr2683rfs.htm); [Senators’ November 13 letter and statement](https://www.bluntrochester.senate.gov/news/press-releases/news-senators-blunt-rochester-curtis-demand-trump-administration-strengthen-ai-chip-export-controls/).

8. **The Megaspeed account lacks material findings and denials from the jurisdiction investigating it.** Singapore’s Ministry of Home Affairs disclosed that its independent investigation began in **February 2025**. It concluded in February 2026 that there was insufficient evidence to establish Alice Huang’s involvement in the suspected false-declaration offenses. Singapore said it had **not investigated her for espionage**, although it warned her over inaccurate information supplied to police and barred her reentry. Investigations involving Megaspeed and others remain ongoing. Megaspeed has separately denied wrongdoing and said it is investigating its compliance. These facts neither disprove U.S. intelligence suspicions nor resolve possible export violations. They do change the evidentiary picture: the scandal involves distinct, unresolved allegations and different investigative findings, not an established diversion-and-espionage scheme. Sources: [Singapore Ministry of Home Affairs, November 12](https://www.mha.gov.sg/media-room/newsroom/singapore-investigations-into-alice-huang-huang-le-and-megaspeed-international-pte-ltd/); [Megaspeed’s response reported by CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/nvidia-alice-huang-jensen-ai-china-singapore-megaspeed/?intcid=CNR-02-0623&utm_source=chatgpt.com).

9. **China issued a consequential official response on November 17, and the summit-timing alternative is not ruled out.** China's Ministry of Commerce condemned the measure, said it contradicted understandings reached by the two countries’ leaders, and threatened countermeasures if Washington persisted. This establishes a concrete diplomatic consequence beyond the foreign ministry criticism listed as an unadmitted lead. It also exposes a problem with the second matrix: an American official’s assertion that the Megaspeed matter would not affect Trump’s trip does not establish that the regulation’s publication was unrelated to diplomatic calculations. Chinese objections do **not** prove the rule was deliberately timed as leverage; they mean that explanation remains unproven rather than excluded by the cited evidence. Source: [China Ministry of Commerce statement, November 17](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/syxwfb/art/2026/art_3a69f2058b724dd39cd8ecddcaa4ab87.html).

10. **The record misses Nvidia’s November 17 governance response.** Nvidia announced that its board had established a standing **National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee**, with outside advisers and direct reporting from its security and trade-compliance teams to the committee and interim CEO Colette Kress. This is a concrete company decision after the scandal and the rule, not merely a market reaction. Because Nvidia is the central exporter identified in the five sentences, the action changes the account of what the company is doing in response to scrutiny of its compliance. Source: [Nvidia’s November 17 announcement](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-board-of-directors-establishes-national-security-and-export-control-oversight-committee).

11. **The estimate of 100 additional annual licenses does not establish how many approvals Commerce will grant.** The figure appears in the rule’s Paperwork Reduction Act discussion as an estimate of increased licensing workload. That section separately estimates roughly **970 net additional annual reporting submissions**. Neither number is a projection of successful applications, the number of affected data centers, or future approval rates. The distinction matters because the central factual question is whether a case-by-case licensing regime will permit continued Chinese access; an administrative workload estimate cannot answer it. Source: [Interim final rule, paperwork estimates](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf).
