### 5. The Iraq precedent does not establish that the Venezuela selection was abnormal

**Finding — Question 3; incorrect use of historical precedent.**

The matrix uses Iraq's 2003 oil tenders to reject the administration's claim that its selection process was consistent with ordinary practice.

However, the comparison combines different kinds of decisions.

In Iraq, the Iraqi State Oil Marketing Organization sold crude directly to purchasers.

The UK government's March 2004 parliamentary answer explicitly described three different methods of selling Iraqi crude: spot transactions, competitively tendered cargoes and long-term supply agreements.

Therefore, not all Iraqi oil was sold through open tenders.

Furthermore, UN Security Council Resolution 1483 required Iraqi oil sales to follow international market practices and established an external audit framework.

Those requirements governed oil-sale transactions under a distinct international legal arrangement.

They do not establish how OFAC should select companies receiving authorization to engage in otherwise prohibited transactions under American sanctions.

**Why it matters:** Whether the U.S. government should have competitively selected Venezuela's marketing intermediaries remains a legitimate question.

But Iraq's tendering of crude cargoes does not establish that choosing intermediaries without a tender was unprecedented or outside ordinary sanctions practice.

The matrix's rejection of answer B, and its conclusion that answer C alone survives, therefore depend on a comparison that does not resolve the question.

### 6. The record does not trace how U.S. policy helped create the urgency used to justify the contracts

**Finding — Missing lineage.**

The record begins its explanation of commercial urgency with Venezuela's production and storage problems after Maduro's capture.

But the relevant sequence extends much further back.

On January 28, 2019, the U.S. Treasury sanctioned PDVSA as part of its campaign to pressure Maduro's government.

In 2025, the Trump administration withdrew or restricted significant sanctions authorizations that had permitted oil-sector activity.

During December 2025, American tanker seizures further disrupted exports.

Reuters reported on December 23, 2025, that Venezuela had resorted to storing crude aboard ships because its onshore storage was filling.

The International Energy Agency subsequently reported that Venezuelan crude exports fell from approximately 880,000 barrels per day in December to around 300,000 barrels per day in early January, affected by the American blockade.

On January 7, the Energy Department officially described the new arrangement as a way to market an initial 30 million to 50 million barrels of Venezuelan crude.

**Why it matters:** The administration's speed justification is presented as an answer to Venezuela's preexisting economic problems.

The fuller record shows that American sanctions and enforcement actions also contributed to the immediate export and storage emergency.

That does not disprove the need to move oil quickly. It changes the explanation of why the emergency existed and why Washington had unusual control over the available solutions.
