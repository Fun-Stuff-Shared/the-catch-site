### Finding: C44 — [no-deadline]

**Claim:** “The five-year limit requires an indictment or information within five years of each statement, so prosecutors have until July and September 2028.”

**Record: usc-18-3282**

> “Except as otherwise expressly provided by law, no person shall be prosecuted, tried, or punished for any offense, not capital, unless the indictment is found or the information is instituted within five years next after such offense shall have been committed.” records

**Record: usc-18-3161**

> “Any information or indictment charging an individual with the commission of an offense shall be filed within thirty days from the date on which such individual was arrested or served with a summons in connection with such charges.” records

The same statute specifies periods of delay excluded from calculating that 30-day limit. records

**Record: usao-dc-release-2026-10-23**

> “Nikki Cole Floris, 46, of Alexandria, Virginia, and Bradley Benavides, 56, of Aiken, South Carolina, surrendered today on arrest warrants issued after a criminal complaint charged them with lying to Congress” records

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That prosecutors can leave the existing criminal complaints pending until July and September 2028 before obtaining an indictment or filing an information.

The five-year statute of limitations generally establishes the outer deadline for initiating prosecution through an indictment or information. But because Floris and Benavides surrendered on October 23, 2026, the Speedy Trial Act's separate 30-day charging deadline has been triggered, subject to statutory exclusions and extensions.

The claim correctly identifies the ordinary five-year limitations dates but incorrectly treats them as the operative deadlines for the pending cases.
