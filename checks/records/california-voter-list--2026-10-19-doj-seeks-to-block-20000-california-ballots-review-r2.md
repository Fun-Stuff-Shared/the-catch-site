**Finding 1 — The record omits the federal directives that initiated the voter-list review and explicitly encouraged prosecution of election officials.**

President Trump’s March 25, 2025, Executive Order 14248 directed DHS, working with DOGE, to compare state voter rolls against federal immigration databases and directed DOJ to prioritize noncitizen-voting enforcement. His March 31, 2026, Executive Order 14399 went further, instructing DOJ to prioritize investigations and appropriate prosecutions of state and local officials who issue federal ballots to ineligible people.

These orders do not establish the motive for the October 13 deadline. They do establish that the database-matching campaign and threats against election administrators were explicit administration policies predating the California dispute. That is a more direct lineage than the September DHS email alone.

**Finding 2 — A September Supreme Court ruling and California’s response are missing from the chronology of the citizenship-verification dispute.**

On September 25, 2026, the Supreme Court stayed a lower-court ruling that had blocked the administration’s expansion of the federal SAVE database. The stay allowed expanded citizenship checks, including bulk queries using Social Security records, to continue during litigation.

That same day, Secretary of State Shirley Weber publicly said California had **no intention of using that database** for the November election.

This establishes that the federal government had already won an important legal ruling permitting expanded verification and that California had publicly rejected participation before receiving DOJ’s October list. It changes the understanding of both sides’ positions. The SAVE system and the particular USCIS/HSI list in this case must still be distinguished; the ruling does not validate the 20,000 names.

**Finding 4 — A later county filing supplies a materially different figure for the sample on which the story turns.**

The October 20 sworn declaration reports **4.6% inactive registrations** among the sampled records. Los Angeles County’s October 21 motion to dismiss instead reports **14.6% inactive**, while retaining the same 42.9% canceled and 42.4% active figures.

The later percentages total 99.9%, rather than 89.9%. They also fit an apparent denominator of 198 classified records: approximately 85 canceled, 84 active and 29 inactive. That denominator would match the 86 records mailed ballots plus the 112 not mailed ballots. But neither filing expressly establishes that denominator or accounts for the other two records in the 200-name sample.

The later motion appears to resolve the ten-percentage-point discrepancy identified in the brief, but **the sworn declaration was not corrected in the material examined**. Because this sample supplies the decisive outside check on DOJ’s list, the conflict between the primary records remains a provenance finding, not merely unexplained arithmetic.

**Finding 5 — The county’s sworn evidence describes a risk to election operations affecting voters outside the disputed list.**

Los Angeles County’s election-systems official testified that complying with DOJ’s request would require changes to its central election database, mail-ballot sorting equipment and electronic pollbooks during an established election-system freeze. He said the central system could not be modified without shutting it down, that the pollbook changes could not safely be completed before Election Day, and that implementation could temporarily stop ballots moving to tabulation.

He also warned that an improperly functioning change might flag voters **not on DOJ’s list**.

This is a materially broader consequence than holding ballots belonging to 20,000 listed people. It is the county’s firsthand technical evidence that the requested injunction could interrupt election administration for other voters as well. It is a sworn assessment of risk, not an established prediction that those failures would occur.

**Finding 6 — Question 4 omits independent voting-rights objections and the controlling appellate precedent behind California’s quiet-period argument.**

In *Mi Familia Vota v. Petersen*, decided February 25, 2025, the Ninth Circuit held that a program systematically canceling registrations based on database comparisons violated the NVRA’s 90-day restriction. The court expressly rejected the proposition that subsequently notifying individual voters made an otherwise systematic program individualized.

Los Angeles County’s October 21 filing relies on that ruling to argue that holding ballots indefinitely based on a bulk database match achieves the exclusion the NVRA prohibits. It also identifies distinct objections under the Voting Rights Act, constitutional due process and federal requirements for uniform treatment of voters. In particular, the county argues that DOJ’s proposed order provides **no notice or opportunity for an affected voter to contest a hold**, and singles out mail ballots rather than comparable in-person votes.

These arguments change the legal question from simply whether segregation is allowed to whether a citizen can be deprived of having a ballot counted without being told or given a means of correction. The Ninth Circuit precedent directly concerns registration cancellation, not ballot segregation; whether it extends to the requested hold remains disputed.

**Finding 7 — The requested statewide remedy faces a geographic limit that the matrix does not test.**

Los Angeles County’s October 21 motion argues that Registrar Dean Logan has no authority to hold, process or count ballots outside his county. It calculates that approximately **13,000 people** on DOJ’s statewide list fall outside that jurisdiction. The motion further observes that DOJ has not identified any listed Los Angeles registrant who actually returned a ballot there.

This matters because the proposed injunction is presented as a means of holding ballots associated with the entire statewide list, while the official given the list and specifically asked to act cannot implement that remedy elsewhere. California and its secretary of state are also defendants, so the argument does not conclusively establish that no statewide relief is available. It does expose an untested distinction between the reach of DOJ’s requested order and the powers of the officials it seeks to compel.
