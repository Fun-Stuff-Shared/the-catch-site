## Stated reasons

- **The committee, November 19:** withholding the information “is contrary to the national interest.” That is the statutory predicate written on the summary.
- **Meeks, November 19:** the public has a right to see how BIS handled H200 and equivalent AMD licenses, because the record shows delays alongside approvals “with little scrutiny,” and because the administration’s handling “has put our national security and technology edge at risk.”
- **Mast, press release and the auto-transcript:** the record shows approvals for companies on the June 2026 Chinese Military Companies list, and Nvidia applications that name what he calls smuggling fronts. On November 16 he said Americans deserve to know how advanced chips could reach China.
- **Trump, December 8, 2025:** allow H200 sales to approved customers “under conditions that allow for continued strong National Security,” collect “$25%,” protect the AI lead and jobs, and keep Blackwell and Rubin out of the arrangement. Reuters added, from a person familiar, that officials saw this as a compromise between sending Blackwell and sending nothing, which they thought would help Huawei. That compromise sentence is not Trump’s.
- **BIS, January 15, 2026 rule summary:** move qualifying chips from a presumption of denial to case-by-case review if U.S. supply, foundry capacity, recipient security, and U.S. testing conditions are met.
- **BIS, November 16, 2026:** stop remote-access workarounds through third-country data centers. The bureau said Treasury and the National Security Council directed the drafting.
- **Commerce, November 19, to Reuters:** the licenses sit on that January case-by-case rule and its extra security requirements.
- **Nvidia:** conditions were strict and volumes limited, and it has stopped H200 shipments to China. Kress gave the stop and said guidance includes no licensed Hopper revenue from China. Neither statement, as captured here, gives a reason for the stop.

## Competing explanations

- **Public-right-to-know / national-interest disclosure.** Held by the committee’s statutory finding and by Meeks. The release exists so the public and Congress review one record of how BIS handled H200 and MI325X licenses. Meeks adds that the record shows a contradiction: slow reviews, then approvals with little scrutiny.
- **Enforcement failure, including possible smuggling fronts.** Held by Mast. Nvidia’s applications named Maginfra and New Matrix, which he calls fake companies and smuggling fronts, to install chips in China, and BIS still approved large volumes, including for firms later on the military-company list. The November 7 Times reporting is the factual basis the committee cites, in a weaker form.
- **The licenses were the published policy, with conditions.** Held by Commerce’s November 19 statement and by the January 15 rule. After Trump’s December 8 decision, BIS replaced a presumption of denial with case-by-case review if supply, security, and U.S. testing conditions were met. Nvidia’s version is that what it shipped was licensed, conditioned, limited, and later stopped.
- **A deliberate dependence strategy.** Held by Fedasiuk as a description of one Trump-administration faction, and by some replies under Mast’s November 16 video. Selling H200s, while withholding Blackwell and Rubin, keeps Chinese buyers on Nvidia’s software stack and slows Huawei. Trump’s own stated reasons are security conditions, jobs, the AI lead, and the 25 percent payment.
- **Beijing, not BIS, is why the chips did not move.** Held by CNBC’s unnamed “analysts” and by Fedasiuk. Labs wanted the chips, but Chinese security officials stalled import approval, so U.S. licenses produced little on-the-ground compute. That fits “Approved, not shipped” on May 11, and it has to be set against Nvidia’s later $2.4 billion Hopper quarter and Kress’s November 17 stop.
- **The dump is pressure timed to the Huang scandal and the new rule, not neutral transparency.** Held by @zhou1492987 on November 20. The licenses are an old May ledger, published a week after Huang’s resignation and as the remote-access rule took effect, to show the door had been open and then to shut the overseas-data-center door. https://x.com/zhou1492987/status/2123712133661917349
- **Kessler fast-tracked approvals over other agencies.** Held by The Information’s unnamed sources. The House release is evidence of the volume he is said to have pushed, not, by itself, proof of an override.
- **Private financial interest.** Held as a question, not a conclusion, by Meeks, Raskin, and Kamlager-Dove: Lutnick family exposure to Nvidia stock may have shaped enforcement and the Megaspeed delay. Their letter asks for records. It does not establish the link.
- **The military-company list is being applied backward.** Held by @shanaka86 and the Bloomberg account. Three of the four companies the headlines call Pentagon-listed were added in June 2026, after the May 11 approvals.
