### C35 — Scope of the right to bring a genocide case

**Claim:** “The Genocide Convention lets any party bring a case over genocide anywhere, even if it is not affected.”

**Record:** `nyt-icj-myanmar-genocide-2026-11-19`

> “international law gives countries the ability to act against genocide anywhere in the world — even if they are not affected.”

**Controlling record:** `icj-myanmar-judgment-2026-11-19`, paragraph 585, quoting the court's 2022 judgment:

> “any State party to the Genocide Convention may invoke the responsibility of another State party, including through the institution of proceedings before the Court”

The judgment establishes standing between parties to the Convention, subject to the court's jurisdiction. The claim does not preserve the requirement that the respondent also be a party or the limits on the court's jurisdiction. 

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** Any state party can automatically bring any country before the ICJ for genocide, regardless of whether the respondent is bound by the Convention or has accepted the relevant jurisdiction.

### C52 — ICC arrest-warrant application versus formal charges

**Claim:** “In November 2024 the ICC prosecutor applied for an arrest warrant for Min Aung Hlaing on charges of the crimes against humanity of deportation and persecution of the Rohingya.”

**Record:** `icc-bangladesh-myanmar-page`

> “On 27 November 2024, the ICC Prosecutor issued a statement publicising his Office’s application for an arrest warrant”

The prosecutor stated that the office:

> “has concluded that there are reasonable grounds to believe that Senior General and Acting President Min Aung Hlaing [...] bears criminal responsibility for the crimes against humanity of deportation and persecution of the Rohingya”

The record establishes an application supported by allegations and a reasonable-grounds assessment. It does not establish that formal criminal charges had been filed against Min Aung Hlaing. 

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** Min Aung Hlaing had already been formally charged by the ICC, rather than being the subject of a prosecutor's application for an arrest warrant.

### C57 — Scope of the two-year report and compensation procedure

**Claim:** “Myanmar must report to the court within two years on what it has done, and the court will then set the amount of compensation.”

**Record:** `icj-myanmar-judgment-2026-11-19`, paragraph 625:

> “a report setting out the measures of restitution that it has adopted and, to the extent that full reparation by restitution is materially impossible, specifying the amount and modalities of compensation.”

The judgment continues:

> “Thereafter, and after ascertaining the views of the Parties, the Court will make such orders as may be required for the further conduct of the proceedings”

The report concerns restitution and compensation, not Myanmar's compliance with every aspect of the judgment. The court also reserves further procedural steps and consultation with the parties before determining compensation. 

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** Myanmar must file a comprehensive compliance report covering all orders, after which the court will automatically determine compensation. Neither the report's scope nor the subsequent procedure is that simple.

### C78 — Dissenters' acceptance of material acts versus a finding of genocide

**Claim:** “The dissenters wrote they were fully convinced that Myanmar's forces committed genocidal acts, including killing children, at Min Gyi, Chut Pyin and Maung Nu, many going far beyond what fighting ARSA required.”

**Record:** `icj-myanmar-joint-dissent-2026-11-19`, paragraph 30:

> “We have no doubt that Myanmar’s security forces committed a large number of acts constituting forms of the actus reus of genocide”

The dissenters also stated:

> “many of those acts went far beyond what was necessary to effectively confront the ARSA insurgency.”

But their ultimate conclusion was:

> “Our conclusion is that the commission of genocide by Myanmar has not been established.”

The distinction matters. The dissenters accepted that killings and serious bodily or mental harm satisfied material elements of genocide under Article II. They rejected the conclusion that the evidence established the necessary genocidal intent.  records(20261120-105346)

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** The six dissenters agreed that genocidal acts, in the full legal sense, were proved at the three villages and disputed only their geographical extent. Their disagreement extended to whether the necessary intent had been established.
