### 1. The November 19 news conference produced demands beyond those in the record.

The brief relies on coverage announcing the conference, but an updated Associated Press report confirms it occurred and establishes the caucus's full demands.

The lawmakers requested a public hearing, disclosure of the instructions counties received about Experian data, and an accounting of why some inactive voters' affidavit ballots were accepted while others were rejected. They also called for repealing or amending laws permitting third-party data in voter-eligibility decisions, establishing early voting and online registration, and allowing voters to correct absentee-ballot problems.

The caucus wants action before the 2027 elections. This is a broader challenge to Mississippi's election administration than an investigation followed by one proposed verification bill.

**Source:** [AP, updated November 19](https://www.thetelegraph.com/news/politics/article/mississippi-saw-a-spike-in-inactive-voters-for-22538976.php) [The Telegraph](https://www.thetelegraph.com/news/politics/article/mississippi-saw-a-spike-in-inactive-voters-for-22538976.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 2. A voter reported an actual rejected ballot after an incorrect Experian address was identified.

At the November 19 conference, Holmes County voter Kameisha Smith said an election commissioner told her that her record was associated with an address in San Antonio, Texas, where she had never lived.

Smith said Experian subsequently acknowledged the address was inaccurate and removed it from her report. She also said she learned that her November 3 affidavit ballot had been rejected.

The record establishes that eligible voters encountered erroneous addresses but treats the consequences for ballots as largely unmeasured. Smith's account supplies a concrete instance of a reported rejected ballot following such an error.

The official rejection reason has not been independently established, so her account does not prove the incorrect address was the legal reason the ballot was rejected.

**Source:** [AP's November 19 report, including Smith's account](https://www.thetelegraph.com/news/politics/article/mississippi-saw-a-spike-in-inactive-voters-for-22538976.php)

### 3. The principal numerical conclusion rests on two different data series, not one continuous federal series.

The 165,778 inactive voters reported for November 2024 comes from the federal Election Administration and Voting Survey (EAVS). The 230,107 figure for October 2026 comes from a separate compilation using Mississippi's voter-registration database.

There is no 2026 EAVS observation in that comparison.

Consequently, the calculated increase of 64,329 is not a change established entirely within one federal series. The matrix's rejection of Question 1, Answer C, relies on precisely that unsupported premise.

The distinction matters because AP's separate historical data gives approximately 171,000 inactive voters in 2024, producing a different two-year increase. The evidence strongly indicates a substantial rise, but the record has not established that the two endpoints use identical definitions and extraction procedures.

**Sources:** [2024 EAVS comprehensive report](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2025-07/2024_EAVS_Report_508.pdf); [October 2026 inactive-voter database coverage](https://www.mpbonline.org/blogs/news/over-230k-mississippi-voters-still-marked-inactive-days-before-midterm-elections/?ceid=3738446&emci=b89f4c31-edd9-f111-bf5b-000d3a54442e&emdi=f4a491ca-f1d9-f111-bf5b-000d3a54442e); [AP's historical comparison](https://apnews.com/article/mississippi-voters-ballots-democrats-experian-7a9282987612197b43c73cf05f7e7d31).

### 4. The federal Experian pilot report directly addresses the dispute but was not admitted as evidence.

The August 2025 Election Assistance Commission study is more consequential than the record's references to the pilot or Experian's contractual disclaimer.

The study found that credit-bureau searches could identify substantially more potential new addresses than traditional sources. That supports the state's argument that commercial data can reveal addresses existing methods miss.

But the EAC explicitly said it could not determine whether the original voter-registration address or the credit-bureau address was correct because the necessary verification had not been performed. It called for further research before acting on the information and recommended using commercial data to supplement, rather than replace, existing sources.

This changes the evaluation of Question 2. The federal study supports using the information to identify **possible address changes**; it does not establish that most flagged people actually moved.

Therefore, the record's treatment of Answer B as surviving must not be mistaken for evidence that its central prediction—most flags represented real movers—has been demonstrated. Equally, the study does not establish the proportion of erroneous flags.

The report also identified demographic correlations in one anonymized jurisdiction, but did not identify that jurisdiction as Mississippi. Those findings cannot establish a Mississippi-specific racial disparity.

**Source:** [U.S. Election Assistance Commission, August 2025 pilot study, pages 15–20](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/Voter_List_Maintenance_Study_508.pdf).

### 5. The federal-law justification omits which restrictions were Mississippi's own choices.

The federal National Voter Registration Act of 1993 and Help America Vote Act of 2002 establish requirements for maintaining registration records and safeguards before removing voters.

They do not require Mississippi's particular practice of changing a voter's status to inactive when an address-confirmation notice is mailed, before the voter has an opportunity to respond.

That consequence follows Mississippi's 2023 HB 1310, sponsored by Republican legislators and approved by Gov. Tate Reeves.

This distinction matters because the state presents its actions partly as compliance with federal legal obligations. Federal law explains the obligation to maintain accurate rolls; Mississippi's legislation explains why an address flag can immediately prevent someone from casting a regular ballot.

The statutory chronology also has a missing safeguard: systematic inactivations are prohibited during the 90 days before a federal election. The secretary of state's office said no voters were inactivated during that period before November 3. The relevant increase therefore should not be understood as a series of systematic inactivations continuing through Election Day.

**Sources:** [EAC explanation of federal and state responsibilities](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/Voter_List_Maintenance_Study_508.pdf); [Mississippi HB 1310, enacted text](https://legiscan.com/MS/text/HB1310/2023); [Secretary of state's statement on the 90-day restriction](https://www.wapt.com/article/mississippi-inactive-voter-list-election-day/74275614).

### 6. The Legislature already attempted an affidavit-ballot reform in 2026.

HB 788, sponsored by Rep. Timaka James-Jones and other legislators including Cheikh Taylor and Zakiya Summers, passed the Mississippi House **116–4 on February 4, 2026**.

Among other provisions, it would have returned inactive voters to active status after they cast affidavit ballots and allowed certain affidavit-ballot procedures to update registration or precinct information.

The measure died in the Senate Elections Committee on March 3.

This changes the legislative outlook. The proposed 2027 reforms are not the first legislative response to problems involving inactive registration and affidavit ballots. There was already overwhelming House support for a related measure, followed by a specific Senate obstacle.

HB 788 did not contain the same advance-verification proposal Taylor now describes, so the two proposals are related rather than identical.

**Sources:** [Mississippi Legislature, HB 788 as passed by the House](https://billstatus.ls.state.ms.us/documents/2026/html/HB/0700-0799/HB0788PS.htm); [HB 788 legislative history and recorded vote](https://legiscan.com/MS/bill/HB788/2026).

### 7. Existing law already provides an election-audit mechanism relevant to the disputed ballots.

Another portion of the same 2023 law, codified at Mississippi Code § 23-15-615, authorizes procedural audits of the 2026 general election.

Those audits encompass affidavit ballots, their envelopes and receipt books, and whether officials processed and counted them according to law. The statute provides for findings about potentially disenfranchised voters and reports to state leaders.

This does not substitute for the caucus's requested investigation into commercial address data. The existing audits concern election procedures, not a comprehensive examination of Experian's accuracy.

Nevertheless, the brief's account of what happens next omits an already established accountability process that could examine part of the disputed ballot handling without a newly scheduled legislative hearing.

Whether a relevant 2026 audit has begun or been completed remains unverified.

**Source:** [Mississippi Code § 23-15-615](https://law.justia.com/codes/mississippi/title-23/chapter-15/article-17/subarticle-c/section-23-15-615/).

### 8. The evidence does not establish that inactive-status errors could not have changed the Senate result.

The record rules out an effect on the Senate outcome because approximately 5,800 rejected affidavit ballots were fewer than Scott Colom's 8,031-vote margin.

There are two problems.

First, the secretary of state's **November 16** statement supersedes the November 13 partial processing figures used for that calculation. It says approximately **75% of all 21,484 affidavit ballots were accepted**, implying roughly 5,400 rejected, rather than the earlier estimate of 5,800.

Second, even an accurate rejected-ballot total establishes only the maximum effect of those rejected ballots themselves. It cannot establish how many eligible voters discovered inactive status and left without voting, never completed an affidavit ballot, or were otherwise prevented from voting.

There is no evidence establishing enough additional affected voters to reverse the Senate result. But the record's conclusion that the result **could not** have changed is stronger than the evidence permits.

**Sources:** [Secretary of state's November 16 final acceptance-rate statement](https://www.sos.ms.gov/news/mississippis-election-integrity-held-strong); [AP's November 19 election figures](https://www.thetelegraph.com/news/politics/article/mississippi-saw-a-spike-in-inactive-voters-for-22538976.php).

### 9. The affidavit-ballot rate comparison uses different denominators.

Question 3's matrix compares approximately 973,000 votes cast for U.S. Senate candidates in 2026 with 1,225,176 **people who voted** in Mississippi's 2024 election, according to EAVS.

Those are different measures. One counts votes in a particular contest; the other counts people who participated in the election.

The matrix then uses the resulting rates—approximately 2.2 versus 1.7 affidavits per 100 voters—to rule out the state's explanation that the overall affidavit volume was consistent with an ordinary election.

The decline in statewide turnout is plausible, and the increase in DeSoto County affidavit ballots remains independently significant. But the statewide normalized rate does not establish that conclusion without comparable participation totals for both elections.

**Sources:** [EAC 2024 Mississippi voting and turnout data](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2025-07/2024_EAVS_Report_508.pdf); [AP's November 2026 Senate election data](https://apnews.com/article/mississippi-voters-ballots-democrats-experian-7a9282987612197b43c73cf05f7e7d31).

### 10. A documented subset of Hinds County's rejections provides a more concrete competing explanation.

The record includes commissioners' statements suggesting that wrong-precinct voting and unprocessed registration applications contributed to the unusually high rejection rate in Hinds County.

An earlier local report gives a specific breakdown for **212 rejected affidavit ballots cast by Jackson State University students**:

- 188 were classified by county officials as not registered.
- 18 were cast in the wrong precinct.
- Six involved voters registered in another county.

The figures establish that documented registration and precinct problems accounted for at least one identifiable group of rejections. They do not establish whether registration applications were mishandled or why the remaining approximately 1,400 Hinds County affidavits were rejected.

This strengthens the evidence for a serious explanation other than Experian errors, without establishing that explanation for the countywide decline in acceptance.

**Source:** [WLBT, November 10, reporting the Hinds County Election Commission's breakdown](https://www.wlbt.com/2026/11/11/24-affidavit-ballots-were-rejected-statewide-including-some-hinds-county/?outputType=amp).

### 11. The replacement vendor does not necessarily end the disputed use of credit data.

The record identifies IDI Data as the state's replacement for Experian but does not establish what changed in the underlying information used.

AP reported on November 19 that the new vendor, Interactive Data, draws on information from **all three major credit bureaus**.

Taylor also said he had not seen evidence that the replacement would produce substantially different results.

This changes the prospective significance of the vendor switch. The state has changed providers, but that does not establish that reliance on commercial credit-derived address information—or the possibility of incorrect matches—has ended.

**Source:** [AP, November 19, on Interactive Data and Taylor's response](https://www.thetelegraph.com/news/politics/article/mississippi-saw-a-spike-in-inactive-voters-for-22538976.php).
