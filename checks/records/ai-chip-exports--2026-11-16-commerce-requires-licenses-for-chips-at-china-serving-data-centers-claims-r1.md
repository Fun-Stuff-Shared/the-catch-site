**7 findings across 54 claims.** The principal problems concern the scope of the new licensing requirements, exceptions to those requirements, the existing U.S. data-center exemption, and two claims whose wording exceeds what the cited records establish.

### Findings

**C2 — [data-center-license]**

**Claim:** “The rule requires a license to export, reexport or transfer advanced AI chips when the seller knows they are headed to a data center anywhere that provides more than 12,000,000 ‘aggregate TPP’ of computing power to companies headquartered, or whose ultimate parent is headquartered, in Macau or a Country Group D:5 country such as China.”

**Record:** `fr-pi-2026-23529`, § 744.24(a)(1), specifies that the requirement covers facilities providing computing power:

> “to or for companies headquartered in, or whose ultimate parent company is headquartered in, Macau or a destination specified in Country Group D:5”

The rule also expressly includes linked data centers and indirect service to customers training models for restricted companies. Section 744.24(b)(2) provides an exception when computing power is used exclusively for commercial recommender systems and the required information is submitted.  

The rule's summary specifically describes overseas data centers. McGuire separately states:

> “The biggest gap is the remote access controls do not apply to data centers in the United States.”



**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That the new control applies equally to domestic U.S. data centers, that only computing power supplied directly to restricted companies is covered, and that every data center exceeding the threshold necessarily requires a license regardless of the commercial-recommender exception.

**C5 — [five-countries]**

**Claim:** “The rule requires a license for any export of advanced AI chips to or within Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brazil and Thailand, except to buyers headquartered in the United States that file reports to BIS.”

**Short form:** “License for all shipments to five countries”

**Record:** `fr-pi-2026-23529`, § 742.6(a)(6)(iii)(A)(1), provides:

> “The license requirements in this paragraph (a)(6)(iii)(A)(1) do not apply to exports, reexports, and transfers (in-country) to an ultimate consignee or end user headquartered in, or whose ultimate parent company is headquartered in, the United States, provided the exporter, reexporter, or transferor follows the reporting requirements in § 743.10”

The reporting provisions require monthly submissions by exporters, reexporters or transferors using the exclusion.  

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That the exemption turns on whether the buyer is U.S.-headquartered and that the buyer files the reports. The rule instead specifies the ultimate consignee or end user, including qualifying companies with U.S.-headquartered ultimate parents, and places reporting obligations on the exporter, reexporter or transferor.

The short form independently overstates the rule by saying **“all shipments”** despite the exemption.

**C40 — [wsj-proposed]**

**Claim:** “The Wall Street Journal's first version of its story called it a ‘new proposed rule’; a version captured later that day no longer did.”

**Scope:** The version captured at 17:00 UTC on November 16.

**Record:** `wsj-crackdown-2026-11-16` contains:

> “The new proposed rule would add Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and Brazil to a list of countries subject to export restrictions.”

The saved record does not identify its capture time, establish that it was the first version, or contain the later version purportedly removing “proposed.”

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That the evidence establishes a documented correction or revision by The Wall Street Journal on November 16. The saved record establishes only that one version used the words “new proposed rule.” The chronology and subsequent change are not established by this record.

**C46 — [presumption-of-denial]**

**Claim:** “Applications to export advanced chips for a company headquartered in China, Macau or another D:5 country face a presumption of denial under the rule's licensing policy, which governs where more than one policy applies.”

**Short form:** “Chips for Chinese firms themselves: presumption of denial”

**Record:** `fr-pi-2026-23529`, § 742.6(b)(10)(iii)(A)(2), first establishes:

> “There is a case-by-case license review policy for license applications for exports of commodities with a TPP [...] less than 21,000, and a ‘total DRAM bandwidth’ [...] less than 6,500 GB/s, when destined to end-users located in China or Macau, provided the applicant provides the additional information described in supplement no. 2 to part 748 under paragraph (dd).”

It then states:

> “All other applications for exports, reexports, or transfers (in-country) to or within Country Group D:5 and Macau or for an entity headquartered or with an ultimate parent headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau will be reviewed under a presumption of denial.”



**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That the presumption of denial applies without exception to chip exports involving Chinese-headquartered companies. The rule explicitly retains case-by-case review for qualifying exports to end users located in China or Macau.

The short form carries the same overstatement.

**C47 — [may-31-tolerance]**

**Claim:** “In May 2026, BIS said a license had been required since November 2023 for advanced chips sold to China-headquartered companies wherever they were, and that its guidance did not require data center operators otherwise following the rules to stop using the chips they had, until further notice.”

**Record:** `bis-guidance-2026-05-31` states:

> “a license requirement continues to apply under § 742.6(a)(6)(iii)(A) of the EAR to all destinations outside the United States for these advanced computing items when such items are for entities headquartered in, or whose ultimate parent company is headquartered in, Country Group D:5 or Macau.”

The guidance also states:

> “Bona fide operators of data centers who are otherwise engaged in activities consistent with the EAR are not required to cease the ongoing use, storage, disposal, or servicing of advanced computing items because of this guidance, until further notice from BIS.”

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That the May guidance established an export-license requirement for sales to China-headquartered companies even when the chips remain inside the United States.

The record expressly limits the destination scope to locations **outside the United States**. The second half of the claim, concerning continued use by compliant data-center operators, is supported.

**C49 — [h200-case-by-case]**

**Claim:** “In January 2026, BIS began reviewing case by case licenses to sell Nvidia H200 and similar chips to approved Chinese buyers.”

**Short form:** “Jan 13, 2026: H200 sales to China under license”

**Record:** `bis-h200-policy-2026-01-13` states:

> “BIS will now review export license applications for the Nvidia H200, AMD MI325X, and similar chips on a case-by-case basis provided certain security requirements are met.”

It also specifies:

> “The rule is effective immediately upon publication in the Federal Register.”

The accompanying `kharon-remote-access-2026-11-16` record identifies the effective date as **January 15, 2026**. 

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That the revised licensing policy became operative on January 13. That was the announcement date; the record places the effective date on January 15.

The main claim's broader January timeframe holds, but the short form supplies a more precise date than the operative record supports.

**C54 — [singapore-huang]**

**Claim:** “Singapore says it found insufficient evidence that Alice Huang committed the offences it investigated, and did not investigate her for espionage.”

**Record:** `mha-singapore-megaspeed-2026-11-12` states:

> “In February 2026, SPF, in consultation with AGC, concluded that there was insufficient evidence to establish that Huang had committed any offences relating to the possible inaccurate declarations for which Megaspeed was being investigated.”

The ministry separately states:

> “Huang was not based in Singapore, and was not investigated for espionage.”

However, it also reports:

> “SPF assessed that Huang had provided information which she knew to be inaccurate to SPF in the course of investigations, to downplay her role in Megaspeed. She was issued a stern warning and barred from re-entering the country.”

records(20261118-015146)

**What a reader would wrongly believe:** That Singapore found insufficient evidence of any wrongdoing investigated in connection with Huang.

The insufficient-evidence finding was specifically about the suspected false-declaration offences. Singapore separately determined that Huang had knowingly supplied inaccurate information to investigators and imposed consequences. The claim's unrestricted reference to “the offences it investigated” loses that distinction.

The statement that Singapore did not investigate her for espionage is supported.
