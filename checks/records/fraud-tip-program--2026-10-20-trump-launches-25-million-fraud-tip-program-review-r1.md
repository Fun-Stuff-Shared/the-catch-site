1. **The law underlying WHIP imposes a limit the record never tests.** The Justice Department invokes 28 U.S.C. § 530C(b)(1)(L) as its authority to pay rewards. That statute generally caps individual rewards at **$3 million**, unless a terrorism exception or another statute authorizes more. It also requires the attorney general's or president's personal approval for rewards of $250,000 or more. Trump's example of a $10 million commission for uncovering $1 billion in fraud would exceed WHIP's cited statutory authority. This changes the central unresolved question: an uncapped 1% program would require a different legal basis, not merely revised DOJ terms. The statute also supplies a limited presidential approval role that Question 2's matrix omits, although it does not apply to WHIP's current $10,000–$30,000 awards. Source: [28 U.S.C. § 530C](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-124/t28/s530C/). [U.S. Code](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-124/t28/s530C/)

2. **The institutional lineage starts earlier, and Treasury's role is substantially greater than the record establishes.** Trump's March 25, 2025, executive order began the Treasury-centered anti-fraud effort. OMB approved a government-wide Fraud.gov reporting form on June 22, 2026, four months before WHIP was announced. A **new October 22, 2026, Federal Register notice** establishes that Treasury receives, reviews, categorizes, deduplicates and routes reports to federal agencies and inspectors general. The form uses AI to suggest classifications and destinations, subject to human review. Its estimate of **80,000 annual reports is a projection, not an actual tip count**. DOJ controls WHIP award determinations, but Treasury controls a consequential earlier stage: where allegations are sent. Sources: [2025 executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/03/protecting-americas-bank-account-against-fraud-waste-and-abuse/); [OMB approval](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/PRAViewICR?ref_nbr=202606-1505-003); [October 22 Federal Register notice](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-10-22/html/2026-21578.htm). [The White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/03/protecting-americas-bank-account-against-fraud-waste-and-abuse/)

3. **The president has a documented supervisory role in the broader enforcement apparatus, though not in WHIP's written award decisions.** Section 3(c) of the March 16 executive order places the fraud task force under the president's direct supervision and control, with agencies coordinating its work on his behalf. That does not override DOJ's sole discretion over $10,000–$30,000 payments. It does, however, materially narrow Question 2's choice between presidential involvement and an independent departmental process: the White House already has formal authority over the task force's priorities, potentially affecting which fraud allegations receive attention before any award decision. Source: [Executive Order 14395, Section 3](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/03/establishing-the-task-force-to-eliminate-fraud/). [The White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/03/establishing-the-task-force-to-eliminate-fraud/)

4. **The record misses a binding privacy decision affecting people named in tips.** On October 6, Treasury finalized a rule, effective **November 5, 2026**, exempting qualifying law-enforcement investigatory material in its fraud-tip records system from several Privacy Act requirements. For those records, individuals may be unable to inspect allegations about themselves, seek amendments or learn where the information was disclosed. Treasury also explains that information may be retained while its accuracy or relevance is still uncertain. The exemptions do **not** cover every report automatically. This changes Question 5: the consequences of an unsubstantiated allegation extend beyond whether its author receives money or prosecutors file charges. Source: [Treasury's October 6 Privacy Act final rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/06/2026-20469/privacy-act-exemptions). [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/06/2026-20469/privacy-act-exemptions)

5. **The federal case associated with Shirley's video has a documented history predating the video itself.** A federal charging document filed **February 1, 2024**, in *United States v. Hoda Ali Abdi* alleges fraudulent transactions involving **“F.M.” of Future Leaders Early Learning Center**. DOJ subsequently identified Fahima Mahamud as that center's owner. Its account of Mahamud's case describes approximately **$854,000 in improper meal reimbursements** and **$4.6 million in childcare-assistance payments obtained through false copayment certifications**. Those are specific financial schemes, not proof that the daycare was empty. This matters because the record currently establishes only that Mahamud was charged before WHIP existed; the earlier charging document shows that federal prosecutors had already documented allegations involving her center **before Shirley published his December 2025 video**. Neither document establishes that his video caused Mahamud's prosecution. Sources: [February 2024 federal charging document, pages 5–6 (PDF)](https://files.americanexperiment.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Hoda-Ali-charge.pdf?v=1706925597); [DOJ's Mahamud case summary](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-fraud/health-care-fraud-unit/2026-minnesota-hcf-case-summaries). [American Experiment](https://files.americanexperiment.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Hoda-Ali-charge.pdf?v=1706925597)

6. **Shirley and his critics had already confronted the underlying allegations in Congress.** At a **January 21, 2026, House Judiciary subcommittee hearing**, Shirley testified about his Minnesota investigations. Dylan Hedtler-Gaudette of the Project on Government Oversight testified at the same hearing and, when questioned about Shirley's video, pointed to contradictory evidence gathered by journalists and oversight officials. The record treats POGO largely as a critic responding to the October reward announcement. The primary hearing record establishes an earlier, public dispute over the reliability of the investigations the administration later chose to reward. It also documents that Shirley had received congressional attention long before WHIP's launch. Source: [January 21 House hearing transcript](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-119hhrg62657/html/CHRG-119hhrg62657.htm). [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-119hhrg62657/html/CHRG-119hhrg62657.htm)

7. **Question 3's matrix does not disprove Vance's 99% claim.** The record marks the claim as contradicted because the False Claims Act applies broadly to knowingly false claims against the government. But Vance's actual proposition concerned the **proportion of fraud the administration says it is finding**, and the Act's statutory reach supplies neither that denominator nor the percentage falling within it. The figure remains unsupported; it is not disproved by the cited passage. There is also a missing policy fact: Section 6 of Trump's March executive order explicitly instructs DOJ to **promote private False Claims Act lawsuits** and review them promptly. The administration was expanding that mechanism while developing WHIP, not simply replacing a mechanism it considered inadequate. Sources: [October 20 transcript](https://rollcall.com/factbase/trump/transcript/donald-trump-remarks-fraud-whistleblower-initiative-october-20-2026/); [Executive Order 14395, Section 6](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/03/establishing-the-task-force-to-eliminate-fraud/). [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/factbase/trump/transcript/donald-trump-remarks-fraud-whistleblower-initiative-october-20-2026/)

8. **Two substantive limits attributed to WHIP are overstated, and an important difference from Trump's proposal is absent.** DOJ's FAQ expressly permits consideration of rewards for **unsuccessful fraud schemes and schemes causing little or no financial loss**, provided qualifying information produces criminal charges. Thus, unlike Trump's proposed percentage of recovered money, WHIP can pay when there is effectively nothing to recover. Separately, Question 5 treats participants in fraud as categorically ineligible, but DOJ expressly allows awards to individuals whose involvement was sufficiently minimal. Finally, the matrix treats possible penalties for frivolous submissions as ruling out payments arising from false tips; those penalties are discretionary, and an unsealed charge is not a conviction or a definitive finding that every allegation was true. These distinctions change both the financial incentive and the strength of the safeguards being assessed. Sources: [DOJ reward-program FAQ, questions 7 and 18](https://www.justice.gov/fraud/criminal-tip-rewards-pilot-program-faqs); [DOJ program terms, culpability and false-submission provisions](https://www.justice.gov/fraud/national-fraud-enforcement-division-criminal-tip-rewards-pilot-program). [Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/fraud/criminal-tip-rewards-pilot-program-faqs)
