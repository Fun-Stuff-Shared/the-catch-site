## Findings

### 1. The record overstates when a voter without government photo ID must be denied a ballot

**Problem — Unsupported conclusion; Question 2.**

The five-sentence story says a registered student without government photo ID can receive a ballot *only* if an election official personally knows them. The matrix acknowledges an unresolved alternative but does not carry that uncertainty into its conclusion.

RSA 659:13(I)(c)(3) separately requires supervisors of the checklist to determine whether a voter's identity can be verified. It does not expressly limit that determination to personal knowledge. RSA 659:13(II)(b) also provides for using information in the statewide voter registration database to satisfy identification requirements.

RSA 654:12(VI) further provides for election officials to access registration, motor vehicle and vital records to help establish voter qualifications.

**Why it matters:** The Supreme Court's stay prevents the use of student IDs under the challenged guidance. It does not, by itself, eliminate every alternative method of identity verification. The record therefore cannot establish that personal recognition is the only route for a registered student, or that every otherwise eligible student arriving with only a college ID must necessarily be rejected if officials can independently verify their qualifications.

### 2. A major predecessor lawsuit involving the same plaintiff is missing from the lineage

**Problem — Missing legal history and operative court ruling.**

On September 12, 2024, Governor Chris Sununu signed HB 1569, which eliminated several existing affidavit protections for New Hampshire voters. Those restrictions took effect November 11, 2024.

The Coalition for Open Democracy, alongside other plaintiffs, challenged that law.

On May 28, 2026, following a nine-day trial, federal Judge Samantha Elliott ruled that eliminating the Qualified Voter Affidavit and Challenged Voter Affidavit violated constitutional protections.

Her permanent injunction restored the citizenship-affidavit option for registration and the challenged-voter-affidavit protection for voters facing successful eligibility challenges. It did not restore affidavits as a general substitute for proving identity.

On September 4, 2026, the First Circuit denied the state's request to stay that judgment, specifically considering the approaching September primary and November general election.

**Why it matters:** The October 22 dispute is part of an overlapping series of legal battles over the state's voter-identification safeguards, involving the same voting-rights organization and state officials.

The earlier judgment also explains why citizenship affidavits remain available while other forms of identification remain restricted. Without this lineage, the registration requirements appear to have developed independently of the litigation that determined which safeguards would govern the 2026 election.

### 3. Another 2026 law changed access to the free voter ID offered as an alternative

**Problem — Missing legislation affecting the available remedy.**

SB 660, signed June 19, 2026, and effective August 18, amended RSA 260:21.

The legislation changed the requirements for obtaining a free voter-identification card voucher.

Previously, a person could obtain a voucher through their municipal clerk or the Secretary of State. SB 660 eliminated the Secretary of State as an issuing authority.

It also changed the clerk's certification requirement from confirming that the applicant appeared on the voter checklist to confirming that the applicant presented proof of domicile in that municipality.

The record discusses the delayed mailing of permanent voter ID cards but omits these changes to obtaining the voucher.

**Why it matters:** The alternative offered to students is not simply a free card that takes time to arrive. Obtaining it first requires satisfying a separate documentation requirement through a specific municipal office. That affects the practical accessibility of the alternative and the explanation of why eligible students may lack acceptable identification.

### 4. The record omits the recent Supreme Court precedent expressly cited in the October 22 order

**Problem — Incomplete lineage of the Court's reasoning.**

The October 22 order cites *People Not Politicians v. Onder*, decided September 25, 2026.

That case concerned Missouri congressional redistricting. The Supreme Court stopped federal lower-court orders that would have required Missouri to change congressional maps after voting had already begun.

Its decision emphasized the disruption of changing rules during an election already underway.

The New Hampshire dispute is materially different: Judge Laplante's injunction was issued 32 days before Election Day, did not enjoin HB 323, and addressed the secretary of state's interpretation of the statute.

**Why it matters:** The October 22 order applies the Court's election-timing principle to a different procedural situation from the one addressed in its expressly cited September precedent.

That distinction is central to understanding the disagreement between the Court and Justice Jackson about whether correcting government guidance constitutes an impermissible late change to election rules.

The precedent is not merely additional background: it is authority invoked by the Court itself to justify the stay.

### 5. The stated duration of the Supreme Court's stay is too short

**Problem — Scope mismatch; Question 3.**

The five-sentence story and portions of the plan describe the stay as lasting until the First Circuit appeal ends.

The Supreme Court's order provides for a longer potential duration.

It stays the district court's orders pending disposition of the First Circuit appeal **and** any timely petition for a writ of certiorari.

If certiorari is denied, the stay terminates automatically. If certiorari is granted, it continues until the Supreme Court's judgment is sent down.

**Why it matters:** The stay does not necessarily expire when the First Circuit rules. It can continue through subsequent Supreme Court proceedings.

That distinction changes the answer to how long the restrictions could remain effective, including beyond November 3.
