## Stated reasons

- **Wedgewood, Oct. 20:** test how easily a fabricated firm gets traction, expose “how little scrutinization exists,” force a change in vetting. Only the Nebraska crowdfunded poll was real. Not intended “to sway an election or permanently mislead the public.”
- **FiftyPlusOne and Morris, Oct. 20:** that explanation is a cover, posted after they were confronted and after the firm had already handed over raw data on Oct. 15 and taken donor money. The DM was “Fuck you,” plus someone else’s name.
- **AAPOR, Oct. 21:** there is no acceptable experimental rationale. The responses were invented.
- **Carr, Oct. 21:** a surge of fake and “suppression” polls is meant to interfere with the election and change turnout and enthusiasm. He did not tie that sentence to Wedgewood by name, and he did not show a count.
- **Gomez, Oct. 21 and 23:** the pollsters should answer. Using license threats against stations that reported public numbers in good faith is intimidation, and the news-distortion and hoax rules do not reach that reporting.
- **Former FCC officials, Oct. 21:** Carr is using the fabrication reports to push stations away from polls that show the president’s party losing ground, so a loss can later be called inexplicable.
- **Bice, Oct. 23:** outlets may have reported fabricated numbers in a way that boosted candidates, and the funding might be foreign. The letter asks for an investigation. It does not establish either fact.
- **Nellis, Oct. 21:** they lied, got caught, and the experiment framing is a moral-high-ground claim after the fact. He wants prosecution and the crowdfunded money returned.
- **Silver, Oct. 22:** the stated reason is false. A market-moving motive is plausible because they published in thinly polled races. A second possible motive he names, without adopting it as fact, is to discredit polling in general, which lines up with how the White House already uses “fake polls.”

## Competing explanations

Each is the strongest form I found, from the people who hold it.

- **A real experiment that worked.** Held by Wedgewood in the Oct. 20 statement. Industry veterans watched trust collapse and low-quality methods spread, built a fictional firm, proved national media and aggregators would absorb invented numbers, ran one real Nebraska poll as a baseline, and stopped because the point was made. They say they did not mean to move an election.
- **A cover story issued because they were caught.** Held by FiftyPlusOne and Morris on Oct. 20. They already had the fraudulent microdata, they had asked for comment with a midnight deadline, and the manifesto appeared instead of answers, preceded by an abusive DM. Taking donations and operating as a normal firm does not fit a harmless test.
- **The explanation is false, and the interesting pattern is progressive outliers plus thinly polled races.** Held by Silver, Oct. 22. He does not claim a single client. He says a prediction-market motive is plausible, a motive to discredit the whole industry is also available, and the progressive lean is real in a few races and small in the average.
- **A coordinated betting-market operation, shared with SoCal, not an ideological project.** Held by Morris on Oct. 23, aimed at Tanden’s reading. He calls it “increasingly likely.” He does not publish the trades in the post I read.
- **Fraud that helped progressives, and allies are excusing it.** Held by Tanden on Oct. 23. The harm is an election effect, including on the Michigan primary that produced Abdul El-Sayed, and treating that as acceptable because you support him is “sanctioning fraud.”
- **Straight fraud, with a prosecution at the end of it.** Held by Nellis. The experiment language is what people say after they are caught. Crowdfunded money should come back. Holdsworth supplies the campaign-finance version of the harm: donors used the number to pull money while McMorrow was being outspent by $80 million.
- **A far-right operator posing as a student.** Held, in the search extract, by @davidslosttt. If that is right, both the “veterans” line and the “nice college Democrat” line told to Donnini are covers, and the partisan lean of the numbers is camouflage.
- **A young Democratic-leaning hobby operation that lied its way onto the spreadsheets.** This is what Donnini says he was told, not what he now concludes. It is the account that got them a D grade and inclusion. It conflicts with “industry veterans” and with the far-right-Andrew claim.
- **The point was to move specific campaigns, and it did.** Held by McMorrow about her own race, and by outlets such as Politico in the headline “may have moved some 2026 elections.” Her own second post makes the mechanism a one-two punch with another bad poll plus outside money, not Wedgewood alone.
- **Proof the mainstream poll trackers are fake, which helps Trump’s case against the Times.** Held in the Truth Social text reported by AFP and in the Oct. 24 X screenshots. The Times says it asked identity, method, and funding questions, then removed 15 polls the night of the admission, and that these polls were never in its Trump-approval average.
- **The important event is Carr, not Wedgewood.** Held by Gomez and by Fowler, Wheeler, Tristani, and the other former officials. A fabrication scandal is being used to threaten licensed broadcasters over polls the White House dislikes. Good-faith reporting of a public number, even one that turns out to be invented, is not news distortion and is not a broadcast hoax.
- **There is a real surge of suppression polls, and licensees are on notice.** Held by Carr. He states the purpose as interference with turnout. He does not name the firm, the stations, or a number.
- **Fake polling that favors Republicans is the wider pattern, and Wedgewood is one case inside it.** Held by SPIEGEL’s Oct. 24 post. I have not read the reporting underneath it.
- **One more grift in a year that already had Median Strategies, and maybe SoCal.** Held by AAPOR, the Post’s Pollwatcher, and FiftyPlusOne’s Oct. 22 SoCal item. The through-line they argue is that AI and cheap survey tools make fake microdata and fake methodology PDFs look like the real thing, and that raw-ID checks against vendors such as Pollfish are what caught this one.
