## 1. The district court found that New Hampshire's justification for HB 323 was sufficient at the preliminary stage

**Missing:** The brief explains that Judge Laplante declined to block HB 323 but omits his substantive findings supporting that decision.

In his October 2 order, Laplante applied the framework from *Crawford v. Marion County Election Board* (2008). He concluded that:

- The burden on young voters was real but relatively small.
- The evidence did not establish how many voters actually depended on student IDs.
- The state's interests in preventing improper voting and maintaining orderly elections were sufficiently weighty to justify removing student IDs from the automatically accepted list.
- The challengers had not established a likelihood of success under the First, Fourteenth or Twenty-Sixth Amendments against HB 323 itself.

The judge distinguished those findings from his conclusion that the Secretary's broader directive was likely unconstitutional because the state offered no separate justification for it.

**Why it matters:** The case is not simply about a judge preserving a statute while striking down an administrative interpretation. The district court evaluated the state's substantive defense of the statute and found it persuasive enough to deny preliminary relief.

That distinction changes the reader's understanding of the underlying lawsuit's prospects and the legal basis for the two different outcomes. It also identifies *Crawford* as an important precedent for the state's defense, independently of the Supreme Court's reliance on election timing.

## 2. The legislative lineage omits the 2024 elimination of voter-ID affidavits

**Missing:** The brief traces accepted student IDs back to 2012 and explains the May 2026 ruling restoring certain affidavits. It does not establish the intervening legislative change that made losing a student ID substantially more consequential.

On September 12, 2024, Republican Gov. Chris Sununu signed HB 1569, effective November 11, 2024.

That law eliminated earlier affidavit-based alternatives to documentary identification and repealed the affidavit-ballot procedure.

Previously, voters without qualifying identification could use sworn affidavits under specified circumstances. HB 1569 eliminated those routes.

The May 28, 2026 ruling in *New Hampshire Youth Movement v. Scanlan* restored the citizenship affidavit and challenged-voter affidavit protections, but did not restore the earlier general ability to establish identity through an affidavit.

**Why it matters:** HB 323 did not independently create the entire obstacle facing students without government identification. It removed student IDs from a voting system in which lawmakers had already eliminated important alternatives for establishing identity.

Without that earlier change, readers cannot fully understand why removing one identification category could prevent an otherwise eligible student from receiving a ballot.

The restrictions are successive legislative changes, not one isolated 2026 decision.

## 3. The absentee-voting consequence is missing its governing law and alternative verification method

**Missing:** The brief acknowledges that the October 23 guidance also prohibits student IDs for absentee voting, but does not establish the separate law governing absentee-ballot applications or its alternative verification procedure.

New Hampshire enacted SB 287 on August 1, 2025, effective September 30, 2025.

It created RSA 657:17-c, requiring every applicant for an absentee ballot to verify identity through one of three methods:

1. Include a copy of qualifying photo identification.
2. Have the absentee application notarized.
3. Present qualifying photo identification personally to the municipal clerk.

The identification methods incorporate RSA 659:13, II(a), the list HB 323 subsequently changed.

But the **notarized-application alternative remains available without a qualifying photo ID**.

**Why it matters:** The October 22 ruling also affects voters requesting absentee ballots before November 3, not merely students arriving at polling places that day.

The 2025 statute explains why eliminating student IDs from the accepted list affects absentee applicants. It also establishes that an eligible absentee voter without government photo identification has a legally authorized alternative.

This is a separate practical consequence of the ruling, with a different statutory route to obtaining a ballot.

## 4. A potentially dispositive standing question remains unresolved

**Missing:** The brief identifies the First Circuit appeal and anticipates a trial but omits a threshold legal question that could prevent the case from reaching a final merits judgment.

In Section III of his October 2 order, Laplante expressly **assumed without deciding** that the Coalition for Open Democracy had standing to challenge HB 323 and the Secretary's directive.

He based that assumption on Chief Judge Samantha Elliott's reasoning in the related litigation over HB 1569.

That separate case involves some of the same parties, and its standing determination is under review by the First Circuit in No. 26-1740.

In its September 4 order concerning that litigation, the First Circuit explicitly acknowledged difficult questions about whether the dispute was properly before the court and about the merits, reserving those questions for the appeal.

**Why it matters:** The future of the student-ID challenge does not depend exclusively on whether the state or challengers prevail on the constitutional arguments.

A determination that the Coalition lacks standing could prevent judicial resolution of those arguments.

Consequently, a trial and eventual merits decision are not inevitable. The case also faces an unresolved question about whether its plaintiff can maintain the action.

That is a materially different understanding of what happens next.
