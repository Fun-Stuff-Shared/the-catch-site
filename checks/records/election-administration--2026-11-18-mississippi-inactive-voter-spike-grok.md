## Stated reasons

* **Watson, July 21, 2025 and November 16, 2026.** Federal and state law require list maintenance. Rolls in some counties exceeded estimated eligible population. Commercial data plus death data would “strengthen the integrity of elections” and “prevent voter fraud.” The EAC pilot, he says, worked.

* **Jonson, to AP.** The 230,000 figure “accumulated over more than five years of maintenance by local officials,” and the state “does not classify voters as inactive solely based on the Experian data.”

* **Watson, November 19 interview via AP.** The rise this year was caused by folding Experian data into verification. Separately: cleaning the rolls “is my job.”

* **Experian.** The product was supplemental address information, “not designed or intended to validate voter records.” Officials alone decide registration updates.

* **Karriem and the caucus.** Eligible people were marked inactive in error, found out at the polls, and some affidavit ballots counted while others did not. They want the contracts, the instructions to counties, the warnings, and the response.

* **Civil-rights coalition.** Known errors were already in the file, notices went to bad addresses, and leaving those inactivations in place after dropping the vendor was the harm.

* **England.** Not “nefarious,” but the state should not mark a registered voter inactive “for a reason they shouldn’t be.”

* **Hurst and the Hyde-Smith campaign.** A close race plus a large affidavit pool required preservation and legal votes only. Hurst called the volume historically abnormal. Watson’s 2024 comparison says the cast total was not.

## Claims to check

* **“230,000 voters were purged, or moved inactive, right before this election.”** The stock of people listed inactive was over 230,000 before November 3. AP’s comparison is 171,000 in 2024 and 185,000 in 2022, so the stock rose by nearly 60,000. Watson says the 230,000 is five-plus years of local inactivations, including people who died or moved, and that “taken off the rolls at one time” is false. Both can be argued from the same stock figure. They are not the same claim. His November 19 post says Mississippi Today admitted the “moved before this election” version was not factual. I did not find that admission.

* **“No one was made inactive on Experian data alone.”** That is Jonson’s and Watson’s position: local officials had to review, and other data was in the file. DeSoto commissioners said they inactivated nearly every flagged name. Gunnells and Rivers said they were not equipped or told to catch bad Experian addresses, and some notices went to addresses voters say they never had. The “review” and “not on Experian alone” claims are disputed by the officials who did the marking.

* **“Experian has not served Mississippi since early 2025.”** Anderson said that to AP. The state announced the partnership on July 21, 2025, and Watson says the download was July 2025, with more uploaded in 2026. Those statements do not fit together. I cannot tell from public pages whether “served” means contract end, data delivery, or something else.

* **“Hinds rejected affidavits because of Experian, and that swung or almost swung the Senate race.”** Hinds accepted 817 of 2,426, about 34%, against a statewide rate near 75% and a 2024 Hinds rate of 60% that AP reports. AP says it is not clear Experian caused the drop. A Hinds District 5 commissioner told the Mississippi Free Press that many rejections were registration-drive forms never turned in to the clerk, especially near Jackson State. Of the affidavits Hinds did count, Colom won 703 to 96. His statewide margin, once all counties uploaded, was roughly 8,000, larger than Hinds’s entire accepted-affidavit pool.

* **“Affidavit volume proves a breakdown” or “proves nothing happened.”** Cast affidavits, 21,484 versus 21,321, are almost the same as 2024. That does not measure eligible people who left without voting. Gunnells and Lambright both said some people walked away. It also does not measure how many of the inactive stock were already inactive for ordinary reasons.

* **Vendor name.** AP says “Interactive Data.” Watson, WLOX, the Clarion Ledger, and Mississippi Today say “IDI” or “IDI Data.” I did not find a document that equates the names.

* **Racial skew.** The Atlantic, via the Election Law Blog, says the EAC review found ZIP codes with more Black residents were more likely to be flagged. I did not verify that in the EAC PDF. The caucus asked for a racial breakdown and said it does not have one. DeSoto, the largest single inactivation county in the primary-to-midterm window, is not Mississippi’s Blackest county. Hinds, which is, had the low acceptance rate.

## Competing explanations

Each is the strongest version its holders actually give.

* **Error-driven disenfranchisement.** Held by Karriem, the Black Caucus, One Voice, the pre-election civil-rights coalition, Colom, and Taylor. Mississippi moved people to inactive at the moment of mailing, using commercial addresses the vendor would not guarantee. Eligible voters found out at the poll, some never got the card, some left, and in Hinds most affidavits were rejected. Karriem’s words: “disenfranchisement called by another name.” The coalition’s test: if the data was too unreliable to keep using, it was too unreliable to have stripped active status.

* **Lawful maintenance, misreported as a purge.** Held by Watson and Jonson. The law requires the work. Local officials, not the secretary, change status. Nobody was inactivated without local review or on Experian data alone. Inactive voters can still vote and are restored if the affidavit counts. The affidavit total matched 2024, about three-quarters counted, and the 230,000 figure is a multi-year stock. The election “was fair and just.”

* **The vendor is backing away from a product it sold.** Held by Watson. Experian pitched list maintenance, joined the EAC pilot, and signed a contract whose purpose was roll maintenance, then said the file was not for validating voter records. He calls that “crawfishing” and says the office has the receipts. He has not confirmed a lawsuit.

* **The state used a supplemental file as if it were proof.** Held by Experian. The file was supplemental address information. Registration decisions belong to election officials. Anderson adds that the company has not provided services to Mississippi since early 2025.

* **Counties did not do their legal job.** Held by Watson, including to Gunnells and in the line Democracy Docket quotes from November 10: “I cannot make an election commissioner do his or her job.” Palmer’s version is softer: commercial data is “a lead for investigation rather than a conclusion,” and bad verification argues for tighter safeguards, not for dropping the tool.

* **The state pushed counties to clear the flags.** Held by DeSoto commissioners Snyder and Ferguson, Gunnells, and Rivers. They say they were pressed to work the lists, were not warned the addresses were unverified, and could not check millions of commercial records. Snyder treats false positives as ordinary. Ferguson quotes a state official telling DeSoto it still had 15,000 names “you haven’t worked.”

* **The Hinds rejections are mostly ordinary ballot defects.** Held by Hinds election officials, as reported by the Mississippi Free Press. Wrong precinct and registration applications never submitted, including campus drives, explain the two-thirds rejection rate. That account does not explain the statewide inactive stock.

* **The affidavit pile was the integrity problem.** Held by Hurst and the Hyde-Smith campaign in the week of the count. The volume was “very abnormal,” lawyers should watch it, and affidavits should be separated and preserved for a possible contest. Watson’s later comparison to 21,321 affidavits in 2024 cuts against the “abnormal” half of that claim. Colom’s lawyers said segregating accepted affidavits from other ballots would break the secret ballot.

* **The statute, not the vendor, creates the Election Day surprise.** Held by AP’s comparison, drawing on the MIT lab. Even a correct flag makes the voter inactive before the voter can answer. States that wait for a response do not produce the same poll-book shock. Taylor’s bill is aimed at that sequence. England’s support is for fixing it without calling it sabotage.

* **Bloated rolls were the real defect, and the cleanup worked.** Held by Watson, Snead, and Palmer. Seven counties were over estimated eligible population; three still were in October. Rejected affidavits, Watson says, were “improper votes” the system caught. This treats the rejected share as fraud prevention. It does not identify how many rejected ballots were eligible voters with bad address data.

* **A racial pattern in the flagging itself.** Held in The Atlantic’s account of the EAC study, as relayed by the Election Law Blog: ZIP codes with higher Black population shares were more likely to be flagged for a new address. I did not confirm that finding in the study. The caucus has asked for the disparity count and does not yet have it.

What I could not find: an X post from Karriem, Taylor, Colom, Hyde-Smith, the state parties, One Voice, or the ACLU of Mississippi; the text of any Mississippi Today retraction; a filed lawsuit over the November 19 investigation request; a congressional bill on this episode; and a primary source that equates “Interactive Data” with “IDI Data.”
