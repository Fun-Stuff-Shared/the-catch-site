## Findings

**1. The White House briefing supplies a new, on-record technical explanation that changes the central finding.**

At the Friday evening briefing, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the FAA's aging analog telecommunications equipment had been overwhelmed by data. He specifically identified switches and routers producing inconsistent data, correcting his initial description that they had gone down.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin offered a related but more specific explanation: newer equipment and technology introduced during modernization appeared to have overwhelmed older systems.

This materially changes the evidence record. The brief's central explanation rests on anonymous accounts of a software update causing a reboot and crash. Officials have now publicly identified a different technical failure mechanism, although they have **not established precisely what initiated it** or confirmed the reported software update.

Duffy also placed the beginning of communications and radar instability at approximately **2:26 p.m. Eastern**, preceding the brief's first recorded Chicago ground stop by 44 minutes. The first ground-stop advisory is therefore not the beginning of the known incident.

**2. The matrix incorrectly treats aging air traffic control centers and a network failure as mutually exclusive explanations.**

Question 2 marks the aging-centers explanation as contradicted because multiple facilities experienced problems nearly simultaneously and because reporting identifies network equipment rather than buildings.

That evidence does not eliminate the role of aging infrastructure at the centers. The equipment involved in a shared network can itself be obsolete, and compatibility problems between old telecommunications equipment and newer systems can have geographically widespread consequences.

The distinction is now consequential because Duffy explicitly identified aging telecommunications equipment, while Mullin identified possible incompatibility introduced during modernization.

The Senate Commerce Republicans' claim that Friday's failure specifically justifies replacing or consolidating ARTCCs remains unproven. But the evidence does **not** rule out aging infrastructure as a contributor, as the matrix currently indicates. The political disagreement is partly over which infrastructure should be replaced and funded, not simply whether the failure was caused by old centers or a network.

**3. The nationwide ground stop is not proof that every center experienced the same technical failure.**

The matrix uses the nationwide scope of the order to reject a physical line failure and to strengthen the case for a simultaneous, network-wide malfunction.

The FAA's own ground-stop procedures do not support that inference. A national ground stop is a traffic-management decision made in response to a safety or capacity constraint. Its geographic scope need not match the locations where equipment physically failed.

This matters because the brief combines three different propositions: that all centers were affected, that all centers lost communications or radar, and that one common failure spread to all of them. Those propositions are not equivalent.

The FAA's public statement refers to communications problems affecting **multiple** centers; Duffy described an impact on **all** centers; Mullin said two areas retained communications. None establishes that identical equipment failed at all 22 facilities.

Consequently, a localized initiating failure that propagated operationally remains insufficiently tested. A physical cut or carrier problem should not be considered excluded solely because the resulting ground stop was nationwide.

Sources: [FAA Order JO 7210.3, Chapter 18, Section 13: Ground Stops](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/foa_html/chap18_section_13.html) · [FAA's November 13 statement](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/statements/general-statements). [Federal Aviation Administration](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/foa_html/chap18_section_13.html)

**4. The SMART explanation has both new primary-source evidence and a new official denial.**

Duffy explicitly denied during the evening White House briefing that SMART caused the outage. That is materially different from the brief's position, which has only the developer's denial and Mullin's earlier statement that officials were examining a possible connection.

The FAA's November 10 primary statement is also available. It describes SMART as a cloud-based platform receiving approximately **200 data streams**, including flight paths, weather, traffic flow and staffing information. FAA specialists validate its recommendations before they reach the command center and individual facilities.

Those details establish that SMART processes operational data, but they do not establish whether its integration shares any of the telecommunications components Duffy discussed. Nor does the timing of its rollout establish that it caused an overload.

The new evidence therefore changes the balance: SMART is now **explicitly denied as a cause by the transportation secretary**, while the precise technical relationship between its rollout and the equipment failure remains unestablished.

**5. The claim that every departing flight in the country was stopped has material exceptions beyond the coastal international releases already recorded.**

Alaska Airlines said flights operating within Alaska and flights between Hawaiian islands were **not affected** by the nationwide grounding. It also reported that West Coast–Hawaii flights could continue and that international departures from Honolulu had been cleared.

These are not merely additional examples of international exceptions. They include domestic services operating during an ostensibly universal shutdown.

That changes both the geographical scope of the event and the inference that every part of the national air traffic system had become unusable.

Source: [Associated Press, November 13 live coverage](https://apnews.com/article/6aaa6bd4991ef22eb0c3baa6961a91d1). [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/6aaa6bd4991ef22eb0c3baa6961a91d1)

**6. The latest disruption figures and the consequences after reopening are missing.**

Cirium's count had reached **827 diverted flights by 7:05 p.m. Eastern**: 670 returned to their departure airports and 157 diverted elsewhere. The record stops at an earlier figure of 600.

By **9 p.m. Eastern**, FlightAware reported **more than 6,000 cancellations** during Friday. Reuters separately reported that roughly **700 Saturday flights had already been canceled**, using Flightradar24 figures.

These counts come from different providers and reporting windows and should not be treated as interchangeable or attributed entirely to the outage without qualification.

More importantly, reopening the airspace did not restore airline operations. JetBlue canceled its remaining Friday domestic departures, Air Canada canceled its remaining Friday U.S. services, and airlines warned of further disruptions because aircraft and crews were out of position.

That changes the practical outcome from a four-hour interruption that ended at 8:16 p.m. to an event whose operational consequences extended into the following day.

**7. The funding dispute has a more specific, consequential history than the political positions in the record establish.**

Congress approved **$12.5 billion for air traffic control modernization in July 2025**. On the morning of November 13, before the ground stop, Transportation Secretary Duffy and White House budget director Russell Vought asked Congress for another **$30.1 billion**.

The request was not entirely for air traffic control equipment. Its breakdown included **$10.1 billion for air traffic control modernization**, **$2 billion for control towers**, and **$18 billion for airport projects**. Within the air traffic control allocation, $7.2 billion was earmarked for a common automation platform and controller workstations, and $2 billion for cloud computing.

The timing and allocation matter. Friday's outage interrupted an already active dispute about modernization funding and priorities. Afterward, Duffy publicly pressed Congress for additional emergency funding and warned that similar failures could recur.

That makes the political response more than a disagreement between lawmakers over whether to modernize centers or telecommunications networks. It is also a dispute about an existing multibillion-dollar modernization program, what has already been funded, and where additional money should go.
