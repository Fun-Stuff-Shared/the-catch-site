**4. The lineage omits the April ceasefire, June peace memorandum and July collapse of the agreement.**

The record moves from the May 1 declaration that hostilities had terminated to September's renewed strikes.

That leaves out the central sequence explaining why the United States was still conducting military operations in November:

The United States and Israel began their campaign on February 28. An April 8 ceasefire was followed by renewed fighting. In June, Washington and Tehran signed a memorandum declaring an end to military operations and establishing negotiations toward a permanent settlement.

The June agreement specifically committed the United States to removing its naval blockade.

Fighting subsequently resumed. Trump declared the memorandum finished in July, and CENTCOM announced that the blockade would resume on July 14. The memorandum formally expired in August.

This changes the story from one in which a war supposedly ended in May but continued through intermittent strikes into one involving multiple attempts to stop the fighting, an actual bilateral agreement, its collapse and the restoration of the blockade.

**9. Massie's resolution contains an exception that materially limits its meaning.**

H. Con. Res. 126 directs the removal of U.S. forces from hostilities against Iran but expressly excepts forces necessary to defend the United States or an ally or partner from imminent attack, subject to War Powers requirements.

That distinction matters because the record uses the resolution as evidence that the continuing confrontation constitutes hostilities.

Massie's measure challenges unauthorized offensive hostilities while recognizing a category of permissible defensive military action. Its introduction does not independently determine the legal classification of every U.S. engagement with Iranian forces.

In particular, the resolution does not necessarily require ending the defensive operations used to intercept attacks against Gulf partners.
