As of November 20, 2026, the only new court record is Judge Steven D. Merryday’s 12-page order in *Trump v. New York Times Co.*, No. 8:25-cv-02487 (M.D. Fla.), Document 109, filed November 19, 2026. I read it from the CourtListener PDF. It is a pleading ruling. It does not find that anyone defamed President Trump, and it does not award damages.

The order grants dismissal of Counts III and IV and denies dismissal of Counts I, II, V, and VI. It holds 27 challenged statements not actionable and lets seven statements go forward. ## Stated reasons

**Merryday, November 19, 2026, in the order.** Publishing an accusation of a complex tax or money-laundering crime without “an explicit opinion from a qualified source after a thorough examination of pertinent facts” means the publisher “proceeds with particularized reason to doubt the truth” and with “knowledge of a high degree of probable falsity.” He called expert remarks such as “suspicious,” “could” violate the law, and “smells like a crime,” and an acknowledgment-page thank-you, not enough to remove that doubt. He adopted the complaint’s phrase “purposeful avoidance of the truth,” which is also the phrase from *Harte-Hanks*. The order prints that case as “401 U.S. 657 (1989).” The usual cite is 491 U.S. 657. I did not pull the bound reporter.

**Merryday, on why the other 27 fall.** *Sullivan* and the rest of the actual-malice cases, “whether right or wrong, whether wise or unwise,” as “amply and ably explained by the defendants.” Failure to investigate, ill will, and a departure from journalistic standards are not enough. He says he is applying that law, not endorsing it.

**Trump, September 15, 2025.** The Times has lied about him, his family, his business, and his movement for decades. The suit is how he “hold[s] this once respected ‘rag’ responsible,” as he says he did with ABC and CBS.

**Trump’s legal spokesman, November 19, 2026.** The defendants “intentionally and maliciously” defamed him for a “leftist political agenda.” The ruling is “a victory for honesty.”

**Stadtlander, November 19, 2026.** Almost all claims are gone. The remaining claim “has no legal merit.” The suit is “a baseless attack” on the Times and “the rights of the free and independent press.”

**Stadtlander, September 16, 2025.** The suit has no legitimate legal claim and is meant “to stifle and discourage independent reporting.”

**Kahn, September 18, 2025.** Trump is wrong on the facts and the law. The Times will fight and will not settle.

**Penguin Random House, October 20, 2025.** The refiled suit is meritless. The publisher stands by the book, the authors, and the First Amendment.

**The defendants, in the motion, as quoted in the order.** The statements were political speech ahead of an election. They were supported by a very large document set and by tax experts. A book publisher may rely on experienced authors. Comment was sought. Those facts, they said, defeat actual malice. Their November 2 notice said the Dow Jones dismissal shows that actual malice takes more than a conclusory allegation and that contacting people for comment is not a failure to investigate.

**Stern, November 19, 2026.** The order turns actual malice into judicial supervision of how reporters use experts.

**Richardson of PEN, September 16, 2025.** The point of the suit is cost, fear, and deterrence, not a damages judgment.

The order says the plaintiff relied on “purposeful avoidance of the truth,” and the judge then adopted it. Both descriptions can be true. The phrase in the holding is the judge’s.
- **“Neither Trump nor his father was ever charged, so the reporting is false.”** That is the complaint’s allegation, which the judge used at the pleading stage. It is not a finding. The order does not discuss the Trump Organization’s criminal tax case or the New York civil-fraud judgment. I did not re-verify those cases in this search.
- **“The Times did not consult tax experts.”** The judge accepted that they consulted experts and quoted Kaufmann, Tritt, Gans, and Ledley. He held those consultations were not a formal opinion on a specific transaction.
- **Harte-Hanks volume.** The order says 401 U.S. 657. The standard citation is 491 U.S. 657 (1989).

## Competing explanations

Each is the strongest form I could find from the people who hold it.

**The seven crime accusations were published without the expert opinion the writers knew they needed.** Held by Merryday in the November 19 order, and by Trump’s lawyers as the theory of the complaint. Publishing a charge of complex tax fraud or money laundering without an explicit opinion from a qualified person who examined the facts is purposeful avoidance of the truth. Under *Harte-Hanks*, that is actual malice, not ordinary failure to investigate. The “smells like a crime” quotes make the problem worse because they show the writers never had a real opinion.

**The ruling is a victory for accountability and a defeat for a political press.** Held by the unnamed spokesman for Trump’s lawyers, November 19, 2026. The Times has a long pattern of defaming Trump for a leftist agenda. The defendants will now have to answer for intentional, malicious defamation. Trump himself put the same view on September 15, 2025: the paper is a Democratic mouthpiece, and the suit is of a piece with the ABC and CBS settlements. Herron, November 19, holds a narrower version: actual malice is a high bar, not an absolute shield, and letting the case proceed is accountability, not censorship.

**This is a narrow pleading win, and most of the suit is already gone.** Held by Stadtlander, November 19, and by Moore. The Times is encouraged because almost all claims were dismissed. What remains has no legal merit and will be defeated. The court has not found defamation. Trump still has to prove falsity and actual malice.

**The order misreads actual malice and will chill reporting on powerful people.** Held by Stern, November 19. *Sullivan* exists so judges will not manage reporting. Asking whether experts should have been consulted once more, or should have written a more formal memo, is the inquiry *Sullivan* forbids. Tushnet, Fallow, Cohn, Richardson, and Reich held the same view of the original complaint in September 2025: the suit is a threat dressed as a tort, and the First Amendment bar is too high for it to succeed. Reich’s “no chance” was about final victory. It is in tension with a complaint that has now survived a motion to dismiss in part.

**The reporting was the product of a real investigation, so malice is not plausible.** Held by the Times and Penguin in their motion, as quoted by the judge. Craig and Buettner went through about 100,000 pages, used gift-and-estate specialists, and said so in the book. A publisher may rely on reporters of their experience. Prior reputable reports and a request for comment cut against malice. The November 2 filing adds Gayles’s Dow Jones order: an actual-malice claim needs more than conclusions, and a story that describes an investigation is not purposeful avoidance.

**The suit was filed to punish and to extract a settlement, not to win a trial.** Held by PEN on September 16, 2025, by Reich on September 18, and by Kahn as the reason the Times will not settle. Trump’s own September 15 post treats the ABC and CBS settlements as the model. Kahn’s answer was that those settlements are exactly what the Times will not do.

**The case is in Florida so Trump can avoid a New York court.** Held by the defendants in the venue motion Merryday denied on July 6, 2026. Their argument, summarized from the CourtListener docket entry and not from the brief: the reporting and editing happened in New York. Merryday kept the case. The complaint’s stated reason, as described in that order, is nationwide publication plus injury in the Middle District of Florida, including a claimed drop in the stock of Trump Media & Technology Group.

**The whole suit is political theater and the tax-fraud claims are no different.** Held by Stadtlander on September 19, 2025, when he called the original complaint a political document, and by Cohn and Fallow the same week. Merryday agreed the original 85-page complaint was an improper political broadside, then later decided a rewritten complaint pleaded a real claim on seven sentences.
