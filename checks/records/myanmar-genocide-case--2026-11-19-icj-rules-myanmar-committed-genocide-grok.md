## Stated reasons

- **The Court’s majority, 19 November 2026.** Reuters reports the judgment’s words: “the only reasonable inference that can explain Myanmar's actions is an intention to destroy a substantial part of the protected group namely the Rohingya population in Northern Rakhine state.” AFP reports the Court found no evidence that, in the clearance operations, Myanmar distinguished between civilians and alleged fighters. https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/top-un-court-rule-alleged-genocide-myanmars-rohingya-2026-11-18/

- **The six dissenters.** Genocidal intent was not proved to the “fully conclusive” standard. Displacement, collective punishment, intimidation, and deterrence remain reasonable inferences. Joint dissent, 19 November 2026.

- **Myanmar’s 2017–2019 case, restated through 2026.** The operations were a response to Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army attacks on police posts. ## Competing explanations

Each is the strongest form I found, and who holds it.

- **The operations were genocide by the state.** The only reasonable inference from the killings, sexual violence, attacks on children, destruction of villages, and the flight to Bangladesh is an intent to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya in northern Rakhine. Other motives do not cancel that intent. Held by the majority of nine, The Gambia, the OIC, the UN Secretary-General, the EU, the UK, Australia, Bangladesh, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty, and the Rohingya camp speakers quoted above. 19 November 2026.

- **The crimes were real, but genocidal intent was not proved.** Forcible displacement, collective punishment, intimidation, and deterrence are still reasonable inferences, so the Genocide Convention’s specific intent is not met. Held by the six dissenting judges, 19 November 2026.

- **It was counterterrorism, and the case is unfair.** ARSA attacked police posts. The army cleared insurgents. A divided bench has unjustly accused the country. Held by the Myanmar government in 2019 through Suu Kyi, and again on 20 November 2026 by Khaing Khaing Soe. Suu Kyi’s sharper version was that even disproportionate force would be a war crime or a human-rights violation for Myanmar’s own military courts, not genocide.

- **The Court stretched its jurisdiction.** Whatever happened in 2017, “jurisdiction overreach and expanded interpretation” will not settle the dispute and may complicate it.
