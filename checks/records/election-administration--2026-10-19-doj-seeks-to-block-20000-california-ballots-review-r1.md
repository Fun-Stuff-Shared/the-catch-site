**Why it matters:** This is no longer merely a court fight over the list delivered to Los Angeles County. DOJ is pressing election administrators across California to act before the courts resolve the dispute, with demands for sensitive voter information.

### 3. The central legal dispute is broader than the NVRA quiet period, and DOJ has a Supreme Court precedent for its appeal

On October 21, **California and Los Angeles County filed motions to dismiss the underlying lawsuit**. California argues that the federal government lacks standing and cannot use a criminal prohibition on individual noncitizen voting to create a civil power to override state election administration. These objections challenge the court's authority to grant DOJ relief at all, independently of the 90-day quiet period.

The appeal presents another missing legal contest. DOJ relies on the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in *A.A.R.P. v. California responds that Carter had scheduled a hearing before DOJ's own original requested relief date, leaving no appealable order.

**Why it matters:** The record effectively tests the constructive-denial question against the hearing date alone. That omits DOJ's controlling-authority argument and California's threshold jurisdictional objections. The Ninth Circuit could reject the appeal without deciding whether any listed voter is a noncitizen.

### 5. A separate October 21 court ruling contains material evidence about how DHS generated suspected-voter lists

The record acknowledges Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín's ruling but holds it as background without its material findings.

The court's October 21 opinion documents DHS's **August 2026 Unlawful Voter Initiative** and **August 31 surge directive**. It describes tens of millions of voter records obtained from official and independent sources, commercial-data enrichment, and a process directing officers to review at least **40 suspected individuals per day**, with no more than **12 minutes to eliminate a person from a target list**.

The opinion also records USCIS's acknowledgment that similar names, outdated citizenship information and incomplete records can produce errors. The court restricted certain election-related field operations involving suspected U.S. citizens in nine states.

**Why it matters:** The record tests DOJ's claim of individual federal review principally against a county sample. This additional primary record supplies evidence about the broader federal system's data quality, review constraints and recognized sources of error.

**Provenance limit:** The ruling does not establish that every one of those procedures was used to produce the specific 20,000-name California list. That relationship remains unverified.

### 8. A federal court invalidated DOJ's broader voter-list matching policy six days before this lawsuit

On **October 9**, a federal district judge in Washington, D.C., vacated the Justice Department Civil Rights Division's nationwide voter-list maintenance policy in *Common Cause v. DOJ*.

The court found that the policy lacked the statutory authorization DOJ claimed and violated the Administrative Procedure Act, Privacy Act and Paperwork Reduction Act. The decision concerns the broader practice of collecting voter rolls, sharing information and identifying suspected noncitizens through federal databases.

**Why it matters:** The record includes California's earlier voter-file litigation but omits a much more recent decision directly addressing the federal government's authority to conduct nationwide voter-data matching and press states to act on the results.

**Provenance limit:** The October 9 ruling does not itself invalidate the specific California list. DOJ describes the 20,000-name exercise as an HSI/USCIS investigation, which is not necessarily identical to the Civil Rights Division policy the Washington court vacated.

### 9. The statewide October 1 ballot-mailing date is incorrectly generalized from Los Angeles County

The record states that **California mailed ballots to all registered voters on October 1**, relying on DOJ's complaint.

The underlying October 1 announcement is from **Los Angeles County**, which began mailing ballots that day. California's secretary of state instead identifies **October 5 as the statewide deadline for counties to begin mailing ballots**. The state also specifies **active registered voters**, not every registration record irrespective of status.

**Why it matters:** October 1 is the correct reference point for Los Angeles County, but not an established statewide mailing date. This changes the factual basis for describing DOJ's statewide demand as occurring exactly 12 days after ballots went out. It also reinforces the distinction between a name appearing on an older registration roster and that person actually being mailed a ballot.
