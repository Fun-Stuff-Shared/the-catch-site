## Completeness and provenance findings

**1. The October 21 volunteer tally contains an unresolved mathematical contradiction.**

Democracy Docket reports 628 spoiled ballots out of 15,035 returned ballots, alongside a rejection rate of 3.2%. Those counts produce **4.18%, not 3.2%**.

The discrepancy may reflect a different calculation, such as an unweighted average across counties, but the source does not explain that. The record carries all three figures without identifying the conflict.

**Why it matters:** This is the same monitoring operation behind the governor's claim. Until its numerator, denominator and calculation are reconciled, the sample cannot reliably establish the rate being debated.

Source: [Democracy Docket, October 21](https://www.democracydocket.com/news-alerts/north-carolina-mail-ballot-rejection-rates-keep-rising/)

**2. The Wake and Mecklenburg figures do not establish how many ballots the new rule caused to be rejected.**

The record treats 147 Wake County ballots and 259 Mecklenburg County ballots with unsealed inner envelopes as approximately 400 ballots that would have counted under the previous rule.

But the October 19 judicial opinion establishes that the 2024 policy also spoiled ballots when **both envelopes were unsealed**. It counted an unsealed inner envelope only when the outer envelope was sealed.

Neither county figure separates those two circumstances.

**Why it matters:** The 406 ballots establish the scale of current sealing-related rejections in those counties, not the incremental effect of the September 2026 change. Consequently, the evidence matrix cannot use all 406 to rule out the possibility that preexisting rejection practices account for some of those ballots.

Sources: [October 19 judicial opinion, pages 6–7](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-10-19-Opinion-and-order.pdf); [Wake County figures](https://www.democracydocket.com/news-alerts/gop-backed-ballot-rules-plague-north-carolina-voters-in-democratic-stronghold/); [Mecklenburg County figures](https://www.wfae.org/politics/2026-10-22/hundreds-of-mecklenburg-county-mail-in-ballots-rejected-under-new-rule)

**3. The court expressly rejected the premise that state law itself requires spoiling an unsealed ballot.**

The record establishes that North Carolina law requires ballots to be sealed in the container-return envelope. It does not establish that the statute mandates spoiling a ballot whenever that requirement is violated.

Judge Schroeder addressed that distinction directly on page 21 of his opinion. He agreed that the statute does **not** require an unsealed ballot to be spoiled. The challengers argued that the Board could instead have classified the defect as curable; the judge found their alleged injury traceable to the Board's decision.

He did not decide that their proposed cure was necessarily lawful.

**Why it matters:** Explanation I is stronger in the record than the court's findings support. The dispute is not simply whether officials are enforcing an unavoidable statutory consequence. It concerns the Board's choice of consequence for violating a statutory requirement.

Source: [October 19 judicial opinion, page 21](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-10-19-Opinion-and-order.pdf)

**4. A significant federal legal position from the original Republican lawsuit is absent from the lineage.**

On November 27, 2024, the U.S. Department of Justice filed a statement of interest in *Wasserberg v. North Carolina Board of Elections*, the litigation that ultimately produced the reconsideration agreement.

The department argued that the Civil Rights Act's Materiality Provision applies throughout the voting process, including mistakes involving ballot-secrecy envelopes. It further argued that rejecting a ballot over an immaterial error constitutes denial of the right to vote **even when the voter can submit a corrected ballot**.

That was an official federal position in the directly preceding litigation, not merely an argument reconstructed from the current plaintiffs' allegations. It was not a binding ruling, and the October 2026 judge relied on appellate decisions favoring a narrower interpretation.

**Why it matters:** The legal dispute did not begin with the September 2026 lawsuit. Federal civil-rights enforcement officials had previously advanced the opposing interpretation in the very case that precipitated the Board's reversal. That also supplies a substantive challenge to explanation D's premise that a replacement opportunity necessarily prevents a vote from being lost.

Source: [DOJ statement of interest, November 27, 2024](https://www.justice.gov/crt/media/1378696/dl)

**5. The record cannot substantiate the five-sentence story's claim that fewer than one-quarter of the approximately 400 affected voters had voted again.**

Wake County's 53 successful subsequent votes concern **all 277 spoiled ballots**, not specifically its 147 inner-envelope cases. Mecklenburg's 60 subsequent votes concern its 259 sealing cases.

Those are different populations. The actual number of Wake sealing-error voters who subsequently voted is not supplied.

The available figures permit as many as 113 subsequent votes among the 406 sealing cases—approximately 27.8%—but do not establish the actual proportion.

**Why it matters:** Whether affected voters successfully cast replacement ballots is central to Question 3. The record cannot determine that outcome for the specific group described in the story.

Sources: [Wake County reporting](https://www.democracydocket.com/news-alerts/gop-backed-ballot-rules-plague-north-carolina-voters-in-democratic-stronghold/); [Mecklenburg County reporting](https://www.wfae.org/politics/2026-10-22/hundreds-of-mecklenburg-county-mail-in-ballots-rejected-under-new-rule)

**6. The legal distinction between correcting an existing ballot and casting an entirely new ballot is missing.**

The September 4 memo permits a voter whose inner envelope appears to have been **opened and resealed**, within a properly sealed outer envelope, to submit an attestation and potentially preserve the original ballot.

A voter whose inner envelope arrives **unsealed** cannot use that procedure. The original ballot is spoiled, and the voter must cast another.

The challengers specifically attacked this distinction under the Equal Protection Clause. The Board defended it on evidentiary and chain-of-custody grounds; the judge found the plaintiffs unlikely to prevail on that theory at the preliminary-injunction stage.

**Why it matters:** The dispute is not only over technical errors versus election security. The Board permits one category of sealing problem to be corrected without another vote while requiring a completely new vote for another category. That is a distinct, tested legal dispute bearing directly on whether reissuance adequately protects voters.

Sources: [September 4 Board guidance](https://www.ncsbe.gov/blog/2021/06/11/numbered-memo-2021-03-absentee-container-return-envelope-deficiencies); [October 19 judicial opinion, pages 48–50](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-10-19-Opinion-and-order.pdf)

**7. The record's foundational chronology omits the earlier practice of spoiling unsealed ballot envelopes.**

The lineage begins its explanation of the envelope treatment with the two-envelope system introduced after photo ID requirements took effect in 2023.

But an October 30, 2020 federal court order reproduces the State Board's earlier guidance. Under that guidance, when North Carolina used a single container-return envelope, an envelope arriving unsealed was a **noncurable deficiency** requiring the ballot to be spoiled and reissued.

The same proceedings established statewide due-process protections requiring notice and an opportunity to be heard when certain absentee-ballot defects were identified. The court distinguished those protections from a general entitlement to cure every defect.

**Why it matters:** The 2026 treatment was not the first instance of North Carolina requiring replacement for an unsealed ballot envelope. What changed after 2023 was the treatment of two-envelope packages, including whether a sealed outer envelope could compensate for an unsealed inner one. The older ruling also explains the origins and limits of the notification-and-replacement system.

Source: [Democracy North Carolina v. State Board of Elections, October 30, 2020, pages 5–8](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/north-carolina/ncmdce/1%3A2020cv00457/85833/188/)

**8. The central statewide comparison still lacks a verified definition of what constitutes a rejected ballot.**

The record relies on 1,349 ballots and 3.1% in 2026 versus 1,829 and 2.6% in 2022, as relayed by State Board spokesperson Jason Tyson.

The Board's published data definitions, however, distinguish *Rejected*, *Spoiled* and *Spoiled – EV*. The last category covers absentee ballots spoiled because the voter instead voted early in person. The Board also states that its broader received-but-not-counted total includes multiple categories and overlaps with the early-voting spoil count.

The record has not established which statuses Tyson included in either year's numerator.

**Why it matters:** A ballot invalidated for an envelope defect is not equivalent to a ballot voluntarily superseded by an in-person vote. Without the underlying classification and matching extraction for both years, the record cannot independently verify that its headline comparison measures the same kind of rejection rather than an administrative spoilage rate containing other events.

This does **not** establish that Tyson's comparison is incorrect. It establishes that its meaning remains unaudited at the point where the story's principal conclusion depends on it.

Source: [North Carolina State Board of Elections, official absentee-ballot data definitions](https://www.ncsbe.gov/results-data/absentee-and-provisional-data/outstanding-ballots-data-report-summary)
