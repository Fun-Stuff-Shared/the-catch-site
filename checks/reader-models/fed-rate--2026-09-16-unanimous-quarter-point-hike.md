# Federal funds rate: September 16, 2026 unanimous quarter-point increase

## Entering

A stranger arrives knowing that the Fed unanimously raised rates by a quarter point, probably remembering that the new range tops out at 4 percent and that this was the first increase since 2023. The coverage makes it easy to read the move as a simple attempt to undo an oil-driven price shock and the 4.1 percent projection as a settled path for rates. They do not yet know what changed since July, what rate policy can and cannot change, or how much one increase reaches their own finances.

## Exiting

1. What happened: On September 16, the FOMC voted 12 to 0 to raise its target range by a quarter point to 3.75–4 percent, effective September 17. (`fomc-statement-2026-09-16`; `fomc-implementation-2026-09-16`)
2. Why it matters to someone who does not follow this subject: The move can quickly raise variable borrowing costs and improve some savings yields, though one quarter point is modest and a series would matter more. (`pbs-ap-fed-consumer-effects-2026-09-16`)
3. The easiest wrong reading, the one the headline or the coverage invites: The 4.1 percent median does not mean the committee decided to hold rates there through 2027 or promised its next move; the projections are individual, conditional judgments. (`cbs-fed-decision-2026-09-16`; `fomc-sep-2026-09-16`)
4. The one concept a stranger must hold to follow it, the mechanism: Higher rates cannot make oil cheaper; they restrain demand and credit so an energy-price shock is less likely to spread into other prices and expectations, and they work with a lag. (`fomc-press-conference-2026-09-16`; `fomc-longer-run-goals-2025`)
5. What changed: In July the committee held at 3.50–3.75 percent with three members seeking a hike; by September it was unanimous that a stronger economy, inflation that had not improved enough and a changed geopolitical outlook justified less accommodation, while the median projected 2026 rate rose from 3.8 to 4.1 percent. (`fomc-statement-2026-07-29`; `fomc-press-conference-2026-09-16`; `fomc-sep-2026-09-16`)
6. What is unresolved as of the newest dated record: The record does not settle how many more increases will follow, whether the energy shock will last, or how much households will ultimately feel; the chair gave no forward guidance, markets and analysts disagreed, and the minutes were not yet available on September 19. (`cnbc-warsh-followup-2026-09-18`; `fomc-calendar-2026-09-19`)
7. The next five questions a curious stranger asks, in order: What did the Fed do, and why now?; What can a rate hike change when oil helped drive inflation?; What changes for borrowers and savers?; Do the vote and projections mean more hikes are set?; How much does political pressure shape what comes next?
