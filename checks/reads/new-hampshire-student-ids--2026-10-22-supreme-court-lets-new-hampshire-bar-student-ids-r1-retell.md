**1. Five sentences**

- New Hampshire's HB 323 took student IDs off the list of IDs poll workers must accept, effective June 2.
- Separately, the Secretary of State told officials they couldn't even weigh a student ID as backup evidence of identity. A federal judge blocked that instruction, but the Supreme Court paused his order on October 22, with Justice Jackson dissenting.
- So on Nov. 3 a student ID won't prove identity, whether to register, vote absentee or check in.
- A registered student with no other listed ID can vote only if a local official personally knows them and signs an attestation. A same-day registrant can have their identity confirmed from state records.
- Many early reports said the Court approved a ban on student IDs, but the order ruled only that courts shouldn't change election rules this close to an election.

**2. What it's really about**

The Court ruled on timing, not on whether student IDs can be banned, but coverage and the state's statements blurred the two. Students end up in the same place either way.

**3. Questions I still had**

- What would a poll worker have done differently under the judge's order? "Weigh it as backup evidence" is never made concrete, so I can't tell what the Court actually took away from a student.
- What does "personally knows them" mean in practice, and who counts as a "local election official"? Is that realistic for a student at a school-town polling place?
- Why did vouching stop working for same-day registrants on Sept. 8?
- What does "The law has supervisors trained to use registration data" mean for a registered voter?
- If the law already excluded student IDs, what changed for voters between Oct. 21 and Oct. 23?
- What is the Coalition for Open Democracy, and who is behind it? Why might it lack standing?
- Why does the order record only Jackson's dissent? Did the other eight justices join it or not?
- Why does the Missouri map case matter here?
- Which specific other documents might a registered voter use? The story says the state gave courts two different answers but never says which one applies on Nov. 3.
