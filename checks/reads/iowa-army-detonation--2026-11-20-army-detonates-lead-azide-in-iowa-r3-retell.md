**1. Five sentences**

The Army has 68 drums of old, decaying lead azide, an explosive, in two bunkers at an Iowa ammunition plant. It plans to blow them up in place, saying it can't be moved safely. A federal appeals court paused EPA's permit for the blast after two environmental groups sued. The Army now says it will detonate by Nov. 24 anyway, under an emergency exemption that doesn't need the permit, because cold weather is coming. The blast will send up a lead-carrying plume. Illinois and Iowa officials and the groups say people got too little notice and the alternatives weren't properly weighed.

**2. What it's really about**

The Army is about to set off a lead-spreading blast on its own authority while the court that could stop it is still deciding, and the public has little notice and no firm numbers on how much lead they'll get.

**3. Questions I still had**

- Will the court rule before the blast, and does the Army's "emergency exclusion" actually hold up legally?
- When exactly is it: Nov. 23 or 24? The story says both.
- How much lead will people in nearby towns actually breathe or get on their property? The Army gave no numbers.
- Is the cold really a threat? The Army says yes, and a former EPA official says the bunkers have already seen similar cold.
- Why were the bunker blast doors removed and not replaceable? Who decided that?
- What are the new plume maps for the Monday/Tuesday weather? Only Friday's were drawn.
- Does the chemical alternative (MuniRem) work on this material, and what's in the Army's August and September analysis?
- Did the Army's Nov. 15 maps meet the permit rule, if the groups say people were told too late? The story doesn't settle it.
- What does "the Army expects less than all of it to get out" mean in practice? How much less?

**4. Sentences that sounded machine-written**

- "The notice gives the reason as cold: it says there is an immediate risk…" It announces the reason before stating it.
- "The notice says the Army intends to act 'by November 24,' and in one passage 'on November 24.'" This is fussy and explains something nobody asked.
- "…experts who question, among other things, whether waiting for the court would make the material more dangerous." "Among other things" is a hedge.
- "The plume maps the Army publishes are meant to show the worst case." It is stated flatly, then hedged: "EPA and the Army both say the maps are not necessarily what will happen."
- "The record that would show how the Army weighed the other methods is its analysis, sent to EPA in letters in August and September, which the groups say EPA has not made public." The construction is stiff and roundabout.
- "The Army published its first plume maps on Nov. 15, and the Iowa governor's office says it learned of the plan on Nov. 16." This repeats the table directly above it, so it sums up.
- "The court's Nov. 19 order is an administrative stay…" This appears twice in near-identical form under CBS and KCRG. It is also a checker's voice, and the headers ("overstated," "checks out, for the first maps") read like a grading rubric.
- "Peter Thorne… says hosing lead dust off cars and other surfaces moves it into the soil or into streams and ponds." This is fine, but it is the only line answering the Army's "hose that off" claim, and it reads as pasted in.
- The Catch heading "When did the Army publish its plume maps?" followed by a Telle quote about "communicating with stakeholders." The question doesn't match what follows, so the section announces a point it then doesn't land.
