**1. Five sentences**

The Justice Department sued Alaska on Oct. 22 to stop it from certifying its Senate race in one case. That case is when Sen. Dan Sullivan loses but would have won if every vote for a second candidate, Daniel J. Sullivan, had gone to him. If that happens, the department wants the result held while an investigation decides whether those voters meant to vote for the senator. Trump said the next day that he ordered the suit. The department's legal basis is one clause of a 1993 voter-registration law, and election lawyers call it a very weak argument. The suit matters only if the race is very close, and a judge will probably dismiss it before then.

**2. What it's about**

The Trump administration is building a legal route to hold up an election result that goes against a Republican, and the story argues the route is weak and narrower than most headlines said.

**3. Questions I still had**

- How would the investigation work? The story says it can't without breaking ballot secrecy, but never says what the department proposes or what the judge might allow.
- What happens to the Nov. 25 certification target and to the Jan. 3 seating of the Senate if a hold drags on?
- Who is Daniel J. Sullivan? Why did he run, and has anyone tied him to Peltola? The story gives only his denial and the "fake" label.
- What did the grand jury subpoenas turn up, if anything?
- Why does a rerun need only the senator to win? Data for Progress found a tie, and the story doesn't say whether a tie meets the test.
- What is the "extreme case" Blanche wouldn't rule out?
- Why are the California suit and the Supreme Court mail-ballot ruling here? They sit in a paragraph with no stated link to this case.
- Who decides what counts as a vote "meant" for the senator, and on what evidence?
- What does it mean that the senator's campaign said Peltola's voters put the other Sullivan on the ballot? It reads like an admission, but the story never says so.

**4. Sentences that sounded machine-written**

- "The federal effort began well before the filing." It announces what the next paragraph then shows.
- "The court is not the only place the result can be challenged."
- "The clause is the third of four purposes the law lists. The other three are to increase the number of eligible citizens who register…" This explains something nobody asked about.
- "Wrong. That is not the test." It is a blunt verdict, and the paragraph then restates the test for the third time.
- "3 other reports / Checks out. Each was checked the same way…" The three reports listed under it are each wrong on a date, a number or a label. The label contradicts its contents.
- "The suit comes into play only if the senator loses the ranked-choice count and would win a rerun with Daniel J. Sullivan's votes given to him." This is the fourth statement of the test, a summing-up.
- "The bodies of some reports gave both parts; headlines that said the incumbent 'narrowly loses' came closer, but describe a margin the complaint does not use." It is tangled and hard to parse. "Came closer, but" hedges both ways.
- "which suggests many of his votes came from her side, though it could not rule out that some conservatives picked him by mistake." This is stacked hedging.
- "The department reads that purpose as a duty the state owes, one that certifying a result shaped by confusion would break." The abstract phrasing makes the legal theory harder to follow, not easier.
- Headings that announce, such as "Meeting the test starts a hold" and "The fight over one clause".
