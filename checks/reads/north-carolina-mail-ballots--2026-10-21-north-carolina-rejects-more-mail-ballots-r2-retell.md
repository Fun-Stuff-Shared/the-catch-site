**1. Five sentences**

North Carolina's elections board told counties on Sept. 4 to throw out any mail ballot whose inner envelope arrives unsealed, even if the outer one is sealed. The rule passed 3–2 on party lines and reverses what the board decided in 2024. A federal judge declined on Oct. 19 to block it, mainly because voting had already started. Gov. Stein says the mail-ballot rejection rate has doubled since 2022. The board's own numbers show 3.1% against 2.6% at the same point in 2022, so it is up but has not doubled, and Stein's figure came from a volunteer count that Democracy Docket compared with the wrong baseline.

**2. What it's about**

It's about a governor's "twice as high" claim that doesn't hold up, and the real rise in rejections comes from a rule change nobody can count precisely.

**3. Questions I still had**

- How many ballots has the new rule actually thrown out statewide? The story says nobody knows, so I can't judge how big the real effect is.
- Why did the board reverse itself between Aug. 2024 and Feb. 2026? Did its membership change? The story never says why Republicans have the 3–2 majority.
- What happened between the Feb. 2 ruling and the Sept. 4 memo?
- Is 2.6% to 3.1% a big change or a small one? Common Cause calls it "statistically significant," but the story doesn't say whether that's right.
- Did Stein or his office respond to PolitiFact's rating? His post is still up, but the story doesn't say if they were asked.
- What did Democracy Docket's "updated" note actually change? The story says only that the wording moved from the state's 2022 rate to the nation's.
- How many of Wake's 277 rejections were for the envelope? The table gives 147 under one heading and then 277 under another.
- Guilford's director says sealing rejections are "about where it was." Doesn't that undercut the claim that the rule is causing a rise? The story leaves the two side by side.
- Why do voters keep sending back bad ballots four or five times? Is the new package confusing?

**4. Sentences that sound machine-written**

- "Both sides agree the rate is up." It's a one-line summing-up that adds nothing.
- "Read another way, as ballots the new rule threw out that the old one would have counted, the words could be true, but no statewide figure shows how many those are, because the Board's counts do not separate rejections by reason." It's a hedge stacked on a hedge, and I had to read it twice.
- "Not every one of those sealing rejections would have counted before, because the old rule also spoiled a ballot that came with both envelopes unsealed." It's a defensive caveat that's hard to follow.
- "His source compared the volunteers' 3% with the state's 2022 rate, as Stein did." This heading announces the finding, and "as Stein did" is confusing because the story later says the article's *current* version compares with the nation.
- "The source changed its comparison and did not say so." Another announcing heading.
- "He called his finding preliminary, not a decision on the merits." The "not X" construction explains what nobody asked.
- "A voter whose ballot is spoiled, which means set aside uncounted, can still vote if there is time". The mid-sentence definition interrupts the news.
- "The lawsuit goes on." It appears three times: the lede, the first section, and again in "What happens next."
- "Some voters get caught more than once." It's a bridge line that adds nothing before the Berg quote.
- "the Board says the rule follows the plain text of state law and adds a layer of security against tampering." This is boilerplate. The judge's finding that the statute doesn't require spoiling is the sharper fact, and it appears much earlier.
