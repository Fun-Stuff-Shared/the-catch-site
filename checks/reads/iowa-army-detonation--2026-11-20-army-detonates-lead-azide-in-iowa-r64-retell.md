**1. Five sentences**

The Army says it will blow up 68 drums of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant around 9 a.m. Wednesday, Nov. 25. Residents of Middletown and a 5-mile zone southwest of the plant are urged to leave. Trump said he'd hold the blast until an appeals court and Gov.-elect Sand agreed. The court then refused to block EPA's emergency permit, and the Army said it was going ahead. The Army says the explosive is too unstable to move and that waiting through the cold is more dangerous. Environmental groups say EPA never fully weighed safer alternatives or took public comment. The Army's own plume maps showed lead spreading far, and the new maps show dust above EPA limits over 27 to 148 square miles.

**2. What it's really about**

The Army says the only choice is a planned blast or an unplanned one, but it told the public late and hasn't shown that it weighed other methods.

**3. Questions I still had**

- How dangerous is this to the people in the dust area? The story gives EPA limits (100 and 5 micrograms per square foot) but never says what exposure at those levels does to a child.
- How many people live in the 27- and 148-square-mile areas, and in Illinois?
- Did Trump's condition get met? Sand's statement is quoted, but did he agree? The story doesn't say. Telle says "go," but Trump said "fully subject to" Sand.
- How much less lead do the "mitigated" maps assume than the earlier all-released maps? No comparison is given.
- Why were there four dates in a week (Nov. 20, 23, 24, 25)? What changed each time?
- What happened to the 20 years of storage before March? Who checked, and when?
- Why did the plume model go to EPA on Oct. 23 but the public maps come out on Nov. 15?
- What do people who can't leave do?
- What do the Army's alternatives analysis letters from August and September actually say?
- Does the Army's emergency exclusion make the permit and the court case beside the point?
- What does the Catch finally conclude? The section asks "When did the Army publish its plume maps?" and never answers it in a sentence.

**4. Sentences that sound machine-written**

- "Telle's post points to the court's order and quotes Sand's statement, then says the Army is proceeding." It describes a post instead of saying what happened.
- "The Army published its first plume maps on Nov. 15, and the Iowa governor's office says it learned of the plan on Nov. 16." This repeats the table directly above it.
- "EPA's rules treat an open blast as a way to get rid of explosive waste that cannot safely be disposed of another way, and say enclosed methods are preferred." It explains something nobody asked about.
- "The record that would show how the Army weighed the other methods is its analysis, sent to EPA in letters in August and September, which the groups say EPA has not made public." It's tangled.
- "The Army says levels of lead in the air may be elevated but well below the level at which EPA would require protective action." This is hedged twice over.
- "…which the maps say reflects deliberate steps such as detonating in a bunker with added countermeasures." It's bureaucratic echo.
- "The air map's colored area ends where the modeled concentration falls to 0.18 micrograms per cubic meter, which the map labels the smallest amount the Army's air samplers can detect." The reader can't use this.
- "FEMA is supporting the recommended relocation of Middletown residents…" Nobody talks like this. It's a recommendation, support for it, and an urging stacked together.
- "Its five criteria for choosing a method include how quickly the plant can get back to its work." It insinuates something without saying it.
- "The court said it acknowledges, and the parties agree, that the controlled detonation will result in harm to the public through environmental and health risks." It's stiff, and it hides the key fact.
- "CBS News Chicago… checks out, for the first maps" and "overstated." These are verdict labels attached to media notes, in the style of an announcement.
- The Catch heading, "When did the Army publish its plume maps?", is a question the section never answers.
