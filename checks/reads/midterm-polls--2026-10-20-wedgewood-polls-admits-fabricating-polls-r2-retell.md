**1. Five sentences**
- On Oct. 20 a polling firm called Wedgewood Polls said on X that most of its polls since June were made up. It called this an experiment, said one Nebraska poll was real, and posted it hours after FiftyPlusOne, a poll-averaging site, asked it to answer a fraud investigation.
- The New York Times, Decision Desk HQ and Nate Silver's forecast had used its polls, and outlets like USA Today, The Hill and Newsweek reported them. Most have since pulled them, but USA Today, The Hill (via AOL) and two poll sites still show them as of Oct. 24.
- Nobody knows who ran it. The firm gave different stories to different people, and its Boston address doesn't check out.
- Removing the polls barely changed the forecasts. The possible damage was to individual races: Mallory McMorrow says a poll putting her at 9% ended her Senate run, and a Wisconsin poll may have briefly cut a rival's betting odds.
- Silver and FiftyPlusOne's Morris think it was meant to move betting markets. Wedgewood says it was a "wake-up call." The FCC and a congresswoman are now asking about broadcasters that aired fake polls.

**2. What it's really about**
How easily a fake pollster got into the trackers, forecasts and newscasts that are supposed to filter it out, and how little has been cleaned up.

**4. Machine-sounding sentences**
- "Wedgewood told different people different things about itself." It announces what the paragraph is about.
- "Wedgewood said "most" of its data was made up, and named one poll, in Nebraska, as real." This repeats the first paragraph almost word for word.
- "…which holds in part for the primaries" and "does not hold." These verdicts read like a fact-check template rather than a person talking.
- "Silver says his forecasts were basically unchanged, though so many new polls are coming out that the effect is hard to isolate…" Hedge stacked on hedge.
- "The excuse has been used before:" It's editorializing, and it sums up before the example.
- "Why would anyone do this?" and "Did it change anything?" These headings are stock Q&A scaffolding. The second is answered with "Not the forecasts."
- "The people who caught it" This section is mostly about whether Wedgewood's claim was true.
- "Other possible motives" The first motive was never laid out as a section.
- "No one outside Wedgewood has yet shown that its Nebraska poll was real." It restates the story's earlier point as a closing line.
- "On Kalshi, a betting exchange, …" and "Kalshi, a betting exchange, says…" The same definition appears twice.
- "Two news outlets still had a Wedgewood poll in front of readers as of Oct. 24." This reads as a summary line before the table that says the same thing.

**Structure problems**
- The lede introduces "Mississippi," "FiftyPlusOne" and "raw data" before the reader knows why they matter.
- "The Catch" mixes two stories, USA Today's incomplete removal and the "left-leaning" claim, so I couldn't tell which one was the catch.
