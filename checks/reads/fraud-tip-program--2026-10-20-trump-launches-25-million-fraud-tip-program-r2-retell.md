**1. Five sentences**

- On October 20 the Justice Department launched a $25 million program that pays $10,000 to $30,000 to a person whose tip about health care or benefits fraud leads to criminal charges, if the department decides to pay.
- At the Oval Office event Trump said the payment is 1 percent of the fraud with no limit, that it isn't tied to criminal charges, and that he would be "the arbitrator."
- The written terms say something different on each point: a flat award per tip, charges as the trigger, and the Justice Department deciding with no appeal.
- Several outlets reported the 1 percent as the program's own rule, and several said it pays on conviction. Both come from Trump and a White House official, not from the terms.
- The White House also said YouTuber Nick Shirley would get $10,000 for his Minneapolis day care video. The charges he is credited with had started before the video, and the story never says whether he was paid.

**2. What it's really about**

The gap between what Trump and the White House said the program pays and what the Justice Department's written terms actually promise, and how the press repeated the spoken version.

**3. Questions I still had**

- Was Shirley actually paid? One source says he left with a $10,000 award, another says no check was handed over, and the story doesn't settle it.
- Does his $10,000 fall under this program at all? The program launched the day he was named, and his payout isn't in the terms.
- Did the video cause any charges? The timeline shows charges before it and a guilty plea three months before the program. The story never says outright that the video wasn't the cause.
- Where does "1 percent" come from? Is it a garbled version of the False Claims Act's 15 to 30 percent, or something Trump made up? The story doesn't say.
- Which law caps rewards at $3 million? It's called "the reward law the terms cite" and never named in the text, only in the source list.
- Is the person bound by the written terms, or can Trump override them by saying so? The story says paying 1 percent "would mean changing terms" but doesn't say who could change them.
- Who actually receives tips, Treasury or Justice? One paragraph says reports go to Treasury, another says Justice decides.
- Is the program taking tips now, and has anyone been paid?
- Why does the "Why a new program" section cite a directive from Colin McDonald? It reads as background to the speed argument, but the connection is thin.
- Is Vance's claim that "over 99 percent" of the fraud isn't covered by the False Claims Act true? It is stated and not checked.
- What are "special services" in the list of first-priority areas?

**4. Machine-sounding sentences**

- "The administration's own statements gave different answers on what triggers a payment: a conviction, an enforcement action, charges or recovered money." It announces what the table below it already shows.
- "Each stated a 1 percent payment as the program's own terms, in its own voice." It's stilted, and "in its own voice" repeats "as the program's own terms."
- "The Hill's line set the bar at 'a successful conviction,' and the Guardian described a program that..." This reads as scoring the outlets rather than telling the reader something.
- "Both match what a White House official said before the event, not the written terms." It's a summing-up line that restates the previous sentence.
- "The tipster's protection has limits:" It announces a list instead of stating the limit.
- "Trump's own example goes beyond what the reward law the terms cite allows." The unnamed "reward law" makes it hard to follow.
- "Paying Trump's 1 percent would mean changing terms that set each award at $10,000 to $30,000 per tip, and paying more than $3 million would also take a law other than the one they cite." It's a closing recap of points already made, and it's hard to read.
- "The 1 percent is Trump's, not the program's" and "The trigger is criminal charges, not a conviction." These headings repeat the headline and the "What the written rules say" section. The Catch adds nothing new.
- "with smaller or no awards in some cases the law sets out." It's hedging that tells the reader nothing.
- "The program is a pilot with an initial three-year term and could be expanded to other kinds of fraud." It's a generic, hedged "what happens next" that says nothing specific.
