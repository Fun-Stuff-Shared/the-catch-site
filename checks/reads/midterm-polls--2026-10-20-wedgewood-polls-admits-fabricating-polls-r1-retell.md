**1. Five sentences**

- On Oct. 20, a pollster called Wedgewood Polls said on X that most of its polls since June were made up. It called this an experiment, said only a Nebraska poll was real, and posted it hours after a poll-averaging site asked it to answer a fraud investigation.
- Its numbers had already gone into the New York Times's poll pages, Silver Bulletin and Decision Desk HQ averages, and stories in USA Today, The Hill and Newsweek.
- Most of those outlets have pulled the polls. USA Today says it removed every mention but still cites one in a Georgia live blog, and a couple of poll sites still carry them.
- Nobody knows who ran Wedgewood. Its backers are unconfirmed, the "experiment" claim is widely doubted, and the guesses at its motive are betting markets, boosting progressives, or discrediting polling.
- The forecasts barely moved, but one poll may have helped sink Mallory McMorrow's Michigan Senate bid, and Congress members and the FCC are now looking at it.

**2. What it's really about**

A fake pollster got into the poll averages and the news, and the cleanup is still incomplete. The story also can't show whether the fakes changed anything.

- Who ran Wedgewood? Who is the second name given to FiftyPlusOne? Is "Jack Sims" the head pollster Donnini talked to?
- Was the Nebraska poll real? It is the farthest from Silver's average (R 6.3), which is odd for the one poll said to be genuine.

- Did USA Today respond about the live blog?
- Does Newsweek's "retracted three, corrected others" count as having "corrected every" story?
- Why does Fox's Hispanic-voters-only figure matter? The sentence ends without saying.
- Is the "Public Polling Project (Sept. 21-31)" date in the USA Today quote a typo? September has 30 days.
- Did Wedgewood's fake Mississippi poll move anything, given that Senate Majority PAC says it didn't rely on it?

**4. Sentences that sound machine-written**

- "This story calls the polls fake on Wedgewood's own word, which leaves out only the Nebraska poll it calls real." (The story explaining its own method.)
- "Who ran Wedgewood, and whether its Nebraska poll was real, has not been confirmed." (Hedged summing-up that repeats later sections.)
- "None of the accounts Wedgewood gave of itself has been confirmed." (Repeats the previous paragraph's point.)
- "They read the experiment as a cover story." (Announces the paragraph's point before the quotes.)
- "Of the eight news outlets below, 6 have corrected every Wedgewood story found; USA Today and The Hill each still have one in circulation." (Announces the table's contents.)
- "Other motives, as others see them" (Heading that announces the section.)
- "PollingSource says it does not exclude pollsters." (Orphan line that explains something nobody asked.)
- "Fox's example was a figure for Hispanic voters only." (Dangling; the reader is left to work out why it matters.)
- "Margins are the leading party's lead, D for Democrat and R for Republican; 'leaned' is which party Wedgewood's margin favored compared with the average, and by how many points." (Reads like a definition block.)
