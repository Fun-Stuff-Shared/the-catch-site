**1. Five sentences**

1. On October 2 the G7 leaders agreed to release 100 million barrels of emergency oil over four months. They also promised not to ban energy exports to each other.
2. Trump had been threatening a U.S. diesel export ban. He dropped it after Europe agreed to the release.
3. The White House calls the deal 100 million barrels of new European diesel.
4. Europe, the IEA and JPMorgan say it is mostly the unfinished remainder of a 400 million-barrel pledge made in March. Europe's stocks released so far are well short of what was pledged.
5. The IEA board meets the week of October 12 to decide who releases what. Diesel is short because the Hormuz disruption and the loss of Russian supply have both hit it.

**2. What it's really about**

Whether the G7's headline is new oil or a repackaged March promise, and who gets to claim credit.

**3. Where I got lost**

- "That leaves more than 75 million: the country pledges the IEA listed in March add up to 426 million." The lede says about 100 million is left, and 400 minus 325 is 75. I couldn't tell which number was the real one until this sentence. Even then, I don't see why pledges would add up to more than the 400 headline.
- The table row "Country pledges listed by the IEA, March 19 · 426.0 million" has no explanation.
- "The IEA's statement does not say whether it counts these last U.S. barrels inside its 100 million." I didn't see why the reader should care.
- "returns it later with extra barrels, so its release comes as loan awards" was hard to follow. So was the table with "38.5 million not covered".
- "France told other governments privately it still had 8 million barrels to release". Later come "could release 2 million barrels of diesel on top" and "sell 10 million barrels". I couldn't tell how the three numbers relate, and the story says it doesn't know either.
- The Hormuz paragraph is a wall of figures: "7.6 million barrels a day... 13.1 million less", "just under 45 percent", "nearly 60 percent", "nearly three-quarters".
- "Yanbu", "Fujairah", "Houthis" and "LPG" arrive with little or no explanation.
- The paragraph that opens "Europe had already shifted its diesel imports away from Russia before the war" jumps to a 2023 ban, then Ukrainian strikes, then a Russian ban, then China. I forgot where it was heading.
- The prices table puts retail dollars per gallon next to crude dollars per barrel. Nothing tells me what to take from it.
- Later, "Brent crude futures, which had settled at $100.20" sits against the table's Brent at 113.96. I didn't know whether prices fell or the two measures differ.
- "The G7 call came out of two pushes, one from Paris and one from Washington." The Washington push is told later, and the section ends up in a different order.
- "U.S. midterm elections on November 3" appears suddenly as motive.
- "After the G7 call, EU officials agreed to hold back any release beyond March". This follows "Europe agrees" and reads as a contradiction until the next paragraph.
- The final paragraph is the biggest jump. Seven ships crossing Hormuz, Hurricane Isaias, an Iran offer to reopen Hormuz, a blockade "since July 14" and Trump on not attacking Iran are all new and unconnected to the stocks question.
- "Unlike in August, Middle East crude exports that week were back at their pre-war rate" contradicts the 45-percent-below picture and the "fewest ships" sentence before it.
- "The catch" is a heading, not a place I understood. The best sentence, JPMorgan's "does not represent 100 million barrels of new intervention", is buried inside it.
- The same points repeat. Germany's "no additional quantities" and its reading of "commitments already fulfilled" appear twice. The White House diesel claim appears four times.

**4. Questions left**

- What is the actual number still unreleased: 75, 100, or something else? Why do the pledges sum to 426?
- Is any of the 100 million new, or none?
- Will the final release be diesel or crude? Europe's stocks seem to be mostly crude, but I don't know.
- Does the U.S. barrel count toward the 100 million?
- What does France actually owe: 8, 10, or 10 plus 2?
- Did Trump really threaten a ban as leverage, or did Macron's version hold?
- How does the Hormuz news change any of this?
- Why would the White House claim "European diesel" if the IEA says otherwise?

**5. Machine-sounding sentences**

- "The statement names no amount of diesel and no country that will supply it." It announces an absence.
- "The IEA's statement does not say whether it counts these last U.S. barrels inside its 100 million."
- "It did not say whether the 10 million include the 8 million it still owed from March."
- "The G7 call came out of two pushes, one from Paris and one from Washington." It's a signpost.
- "The table below gives the weekly U.S. retail diesel price and two crude prices..." It announces the table.
- "None of the sources is named, and their accounts of the demand differ." It's hedging.
- "Germany's economy ministry reads 'commitments that have already been fulfilled' against the March obligations." The wording is stilted.
- "Europe is further behind, by Washington's account." This is the reflex "by X's account" hedge.
- Headings like "The catch", "What Washington asked of Europe, and the denials" and "Who has offered more than March, and who has said no" read as labels, not as an article's voice.
