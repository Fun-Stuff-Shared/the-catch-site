**1. Five sentences**
- Two days after U.S. forces seized Maduro, Energy Secretary Wright phoned Vitol trader John Addison and asked if Vitol wanted to market Venezuela's oil. No tender was held, and Vitol and Trafigura had Treasury licenses by January 9.
- Addison is a gasoline trader, not a crude specialist. He gave $300,000 to the super PAC behind Vance's 2022 run, and Vance had vouched for him to Wright, an official confirmed.
- The two firms have made hundreds of millions of dollars and handled 61% of the money in the U.S.-controlled account. For two weeks after the trade opened to others, PDVSA still sold only to firms with individual licenses.
- A Reuters source says Vance and Addison met at a ski club a week before the raid and discussed selling the oil. Both deny it, and flight records put only their planes at the same airport.
- The Energy Department says it picked Vitol because it's the biggest trader. Democrats want testimony, and the KPMG audit shows most of the money went to bolívar-propping dollar auctions.

**2. What it's really about**
Whether a political donor's connection, rather than a competitive process, decided who got to profit from Venezuela's oil, and the answer is "plausibly, but nobody has shown it."

**4. Machine-sounding sentences**
- "By Vitol's own account, the man Wright called could not do the deal himself." It restates the sentence before it.
- "By Rubio's account, the oil the traders were brought in to move was stuck because of the U.S. blockade." It repeats the Rubio quote and Bloomberg point just above it.
- "In Addison's telling, the sales were more than a trade: the better the price, the more leverage Trump would have over Venezuela." It explains a quote the reader just read.
- "The call itself is not in dispute; why it went to Addison is." It's a tidy summing-up line.
- "The post published a deal arranged a week before the attack. What Reuters found is a recommendation an official dates at least a year earlier, and a pre-raid meeting that rests on one secondhand account both sides deny." It's a recap of the section above it.
- "Both are right, counting different things" is a neat announce-the-resolution line.
- "On the money, the post is loose rather than wrong." and "The timing is where it fails." Both are scaffolding that announces what comes next.
- "The administration's case for the two traders was speed. Through September they still paid in 61%..." This repeats the 61% figure from the top, and "still" asserts a point without making it.
- "In Libya any U.S. person could apply to trade the oil, and Iraq's first sale went by tender among 52 bidders. In Venezuela, Wright offered the job by phone, and bidding was skipped, Reuters reports." This paragraph and the table after it say the same thing, and the paragraph itself repeats details from its own first sentence.
- The explanations section verdicts, for example "Size fits the choice of Vitol, but Vitol itself says... The Energy Department's account and Addison's both stand, and they disagree." These are hedged both-sides lines that say little.
- "The flight records place the planes, not the men." It's punchy, but it's the third time the story makes this point.
