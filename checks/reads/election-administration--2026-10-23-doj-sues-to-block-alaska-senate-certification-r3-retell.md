**1. Five sentences**
- Alaska's Senate race has two candidates named Dan Sullivan: the Republican incumbent, and a former teacher who registered Republican and filed to run, but is printed on the ballot with no party.
- Republicans say the teacher is a Democratic plant meant to split the senator's vote. He says he ran on his own, and Alaska's courts kept him on the ballot.
- On Oct. 22 the Justice Department sued Alaska to stop it certifying the result if the senator loses but would have won had all the teacher's votes gone to him. It wants an investigation into whether those votes were meant for the senator, and Trump said the next day that he ordered the suit.
- Election-law experts call the legal theory very weak, and one expects a quick dismissal. The state is reviewing the complaint, and no emergency request has been made.
- Early voting is under way, the election is Nov. 3, and the state targets Nov. 25 for certification.

**2. What it's really about**
The administration is testing whether the federal government can hold up a Senate result it dislikes on the theory of voter confusion, using a statute that doesn't clearly give it that power.

**4. Sentences that sounded machine-written**
- "Three smaller errors do not change the account of the suit." This is announcing and reassuring.
- "Meeting the test would not make the senator the winner." It's defensive framing, written to head off a misreading.
- "That moves fewer votes than the suit's test, which gives the senator every vote cast for Daniel J. Sullivan, and the test asks whether the senator would have won, which a tie is not." It's stacked and needs a reread.
- "Besides the headlines that dropped the second condition, one report got the test itself wrong, turning it into a sum of votes." The "What the coverage got wrong" section is media criticism, not story, and it explains something I didn't ask about.
- "The test counts every one of his votes as the senator's, whatever the voter meant." It restates the test a fourth time.
- "Michael Morley of Florida State says the worry behind it is real: concern about a bad-faith sham candidate. He also calls this suit frivolous…" It's hedged on both sides, which reads like balance by formula.
- "The department says the listing is not enough." It's a filler transition.
- "Meeting the test would…" and the Catch box repeat the opening paragraph's "if the senator loses but would have won…" almost word for word.
