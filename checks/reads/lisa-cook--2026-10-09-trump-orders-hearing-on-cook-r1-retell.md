**1. Five sentences**

Trump has ordered Fed Governor Lisa Cook to a closed White House hearing on November 5. Three officials he appointed will question her about two 2021 mortgages, then recommend whether he has cause to fire her. The White House says she called both houses her main residence, which can mean a lower rate. She says the Atlanta form was an inadvertent oversight, and she hasn't been charged. The Supreme Court blocked his first firing in June because she got no chance to answer. He then sent written notice in August, and this hearing is the next step. His decision has no deadline, and if he fires her she can sue.

**2. What it's really about**

Whether Trump can use a procedure that looks fair to remove a Fed governor he calls an "automatic no vote" on rates.

**3. Questions I still had**

- Why did Trump add a live hearing after the written exchange? The story says the memo gives no reason. I wanted to know whether anyone else offered one.
- Did she actually get a lower rate? The story says neither side claims she did, so it's unclear what the harm is.
- Was the Ann Arbor mortgage disputed, or only Atlanta?
- Trump says she's an "automatic no vote," but she has voted with the majority every time. What does he mean? I had to infer he means rate cuts.
- Why does it matter that the hearing falls two days after the midterms? The story states the date and leaves the point unspoken.
- Why is her chairing the financial stability committee mentioned? Nothing uses it.
- Will she attend? What happens if she doesn't?
- Who is Judge Cobb, and what is the lawsuit's status? The "joint report" on November 6 appears without setup.
- The story says the Fed "raised rates 12 to 0" while Trump wants 1%. I wasn't told what rates are now.
- What does "the catch" section title refer to? It reads as media corrections, not a catch in the story.

**4. Sentences that sounded machine-written**

- "Both sides agree that she signed both forms and that she must have a chance to answer before any firing. The fight is over whether what she signed is cause." It sums up and then restates the summary.
- "Until then she keeps her seat and her vote." It's a closing tidy-up.
- "Some documents back part of that." It announces what the next sentences do.
- "This is Trump's second attempt to remove her." It's a signpost, and the story has already said so.
- "On October 10 a reporter asked Trump why he formed the committee, and he gave two reasons." It announces the quote instead of letting it speak.
- "The printout is not the loan." It's a brisk explainer for something the next sentence already shows.
- "Cook denies wrongdoing, has not been charged with a crime, and remains a governor." It's a hedge stacked as a triplet.
- "The law gives governors 14-year terms and lets a president remove one only 'for cause.' It does not say what cause is." This repeats the "for cause" point from a few paragraphs earlier.
- The Supreme Court's holding appears three times: once in "Why a hearing now," again under "What the courts have said," and again in "The catch." Each retelling reads like a fresh summary.
- "Is a mortgage form reason to fire her?" and "Is this about interest rates?" Both are rhetorical headings that pose the question for the reader.
