**1. Five sentences**
- The Justice Department says about 20,000 California voters are likely noncitizens. It asked a federal appeals court on Oct. 23 to make the state set aside their mail ballots until their citizenship is confirmed.
- The trial judge hasn't ruled and hears the case Oct. 27. DOJ went to the appeals court anyway, arguing his silence amounted to a denial.
- DOJ's lead prosecutor in Los Angeles called the list "verified," but the two federal officials who built it swore only that the people on it are "highly likely" or "reasonably believed" to be noncitizens.
- Los Angeles County checked 200 names and found 112 had never been mailed a ballot because their registrations were canceled or inactive. DOJ also charged 28 green-card holders from the list and says that proves the list is accurate.
- The list was built from a voter roll DOJ's own filings say USCIS had since May, but it was delivered Oct. 13, after ballots went out. Critics read that as groundwork for challenging results.

**2. What it's really about**
DOJ is asking to hold thousands of ballots based on a list whose own authors say it's unconfirmed, and the timing makes the request look more like a post-election-challenge setup than a fix for a real problem.

**3. Questions I still had**
- How many of the 20,000 are actually noncitizens? Nobody says, and the story never settles whether "likely" means 60% or 95%.
- Why did the county check only 200 names, and how were they chosen? Could the result be off by a lot either way?
- The 200 names split 112 and 86. What happened to the other two?
- If 22 of the 28 charged are accused of voting, why are only six charged with voting as a noncitizen? What are the others charged with?
- Does 18 counties or 57 counties received the letters? The lede gives one number and the next paragraph gives another. Which is right?
- Which judge's "voluminous and unreliable" finding is this, and does it apply to this list? The story drops it without saying.
- What does Weber's office say about never receiving the list, when the county got it and DOJ says California "refused to look"?
- What did the Ninth Circuit do? The story ends before its noon deadline today.
- What is the 25.8% vs. 38.9% birthplace figure supposed to show? I couldn't tell which one matters.

**4. Sentences that sounded machine-written**
- "The charges are accusations." Everyone knows that, and the sentence is just there to protect the writer.
- "No filing says how many of the 20,000 were among the 81,336 close matches." This announces a gap instead of reporting a fact.
- "And in Los Angeles County's check, 112 of the 200 people had been sent no ballot at all." It repeats what the reader already learned and works as a summing-up.
- "On other days Essayli used the softer words himself." This sets up the quotes instead of just giving them.
- "Essayli calls the 20,000 verified. The sworn declarations say 'highly likely' and 'reasonably believe.'" The antithesis is too tidy. It reads like a prepared contrast.
- "DOJ's case for acting now is that…" and "California's case" are section-label constructions. They read like a template, not like reporting.
- "What the coverage got wrong / ABC News overstated the county's birthplace figure." This is inside-baseball media criticism, and the difference between 40% and 38.9% is small. The block reads as announced thoroughness.
- Table and caption lines like "The list's numbers, by who reported them" and "Dated steps in the filings, letters and DOJ's releases" explain what the chart is, which the chart already shows.

**Smaller problems**
- "Bill Essayli, the first assistant U.S. attorney in Los Angeles warned" is missing a comma.
- In "describe the people on its list," the word "its" is ambiguous.
- The timeline lists "Oct. 23: 48-hour letters" after "Oct. 23: DOJ asks the Ninth Circuit," so the order is unclear.
