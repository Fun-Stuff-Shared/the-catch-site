**1. Five sentences**
- Commerce issued a rule on Nov. 16 requiring a U.S. license to ship advanced AI chips to foreign data centers that give Chinese firms more than 12 million TPP of computing. The same rule requires a license for any such chips going to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, with an exception for U.S. end users.
- Chinese firms can't buy the top chips directly, so they rent them from data centers in Southeast Asia. The rule is meant to close that route, and data centers already serving them have 90 days to get a license.
- Biden's version was never enforced under Trump, and the replacement stalled. Then Nvidia's CEO resigned over an affair with a former executive of Megaspeed, a data center company under smuggling investigation, and Treasury publicly blamed Commerce. The rule came three days later.
- Commerce says Treasury and the NSC wrote it, and Politico says Bessent pushed it past Lutnick.
- Much of the coverage called it a ban, or said it took effect immediately. It is a license requirement that covers only data centers abroad, so Chinese firms can still rent chips in the U.S.

**2. What it's really about**
The rule is weaker than the headlines said, and it came from a fight inside the administration rather than from Commerce's own plan.

**4. Machine-sounding sentences**
- "It requires licenses rather than banning the rentals, and it does not reach data centers inside the United States." It repeats the subhead and the Catch section.
- "Chip sellers and data centers must check who their customers are and who owns them." This is a generic compliance line with no specifics.
- "Then the Megaspeed affair broke." This is announcing, a drumroll for a paragraph that follows.
- "The rule came three days later." This sums up, but the heading below says "four days" for the same gap ("four days after Jensen Huang stepped down"), so the two disagree.
- "Where the rule does apply, it asks for a license, not a halt." This is a tidy contrast that repeats the previous paragraph.
- "Three reports on the day the rule came out got its timing or its status wrong." This announces the table below it.
- "The scandal came just before the rule; the rule itself was planned long before." This is a summing-up verdict in neat balance.
- "No public record shows whether the letters moved it." and "No public record shows why the rule was issued that day." These are the same hedge used twice.
- "Wrong date." / "Mislabeled ." / "Wrong ." The labels have stray gaps and read as stamped verdicts. "Mislabeled ." and "Wrong ." look like missing words.
- "Who wanted it to go further" and "Who wrote it" are label-style headings. They are fine, but the section after "Who wrote it" opens with "But Commerce's announcement says...", which restates what the previous paragraph's last line set up.
- "Every explanation, tested" and "What the coverage got right and wrong" are claims the sections don't quite earn. Only three reports are listed, and all three are wrong.

Also a flat factual conflict: the lede says "about 800 of Nvidia's H100 chips," but the 12 million TPP figure is never tied to a per-chip number the reader can check.
