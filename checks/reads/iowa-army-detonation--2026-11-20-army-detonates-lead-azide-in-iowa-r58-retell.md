**1. Five sentences**

- Trump posted on Nov. 23 that he had told the military not to blow up the 68 drums of lead azide in Iowa until the appeals court agreed, and only if Gov.-elect Sand agreed too.
- That same day the court lifted its pause on EPA's emergency permit and denied the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club a stay. It said they hadn't shown they would likely win.
- That night Acting Army Secretary Telle said the blast "will occur this Wednesday morning," Nov. 25. He also said every agency involved backs it and that both governors say the call belongs to the experts.
- The Army says the old explosive has crystallized and could go off on its own in the cold. It plans to detonate it in place, which sends up a lead plume. Its worst-case air maps ran about 100 miles at first and about 60 after an update. The groups say the Army didn't weigh safer options or take public comment.
- The Army told people to stay inside and to hose off surfaces, and a Penn... sorry, a University of Iowa professor says hosing just moves the lead into soil and water.

**2. What it's really about**

The Army is blowing up a hazardous stockpile on a short, shifting schedule, and the public and neighboring states were told late, with fuzzy information.

**3. Questions I still had**

- Did Sand agree? Trump's condition was "fully subject to" Sand, and Sand's reply doesn't say yes or no. Is Trump's hold still in force?
- What is the Army's "emergency exclusion" that lets it skip the permit? Does the court ruling matter if the Army doesn't need the permit?
- What did the briefings tell Sand, and what did the experts tell the Army?
- Why did the bath evaporate? Who was responsible for checking it over 20 years?
- Have updated maps for the Nov. 25 weather been published? What lead levels does the Army expect in towns?
- What are the "specific procedures" for cleanup?
- Was the dust area in the first maps really that much smaller than the air area? The only source is the groups' reading.
- Which date was real: Nov. 20, 23, 24 or 25? The story gives four, and the reasons for each move are scattered.
- The Catch heading asks when the Army published its plume maps. The answer, Nov. 15, comes in a table. Then the quote about "immediately" communicating in March reads as a contradiction nobody states outright.

**4. Sentences that sounded machine-written**

- "The Army published its first plume maps on Nov. 15, and the Iowa governor's office says it learned of the plan on Nov. 16." It repeats the table directly above it.
- "Air reach as The New York Times reported the Army's maps; dust areas by the environmental groups' reading of them." This is source-admission ceremony in a table caption.
- "In its Nov. 21 notice, the government argues that waiting is the greater danger:" It announces the quote that follows.
- "The court said the Army is not a defendant in the case and that it does not opine on that plan." It's hedged, and it leaves the reader unsure what the plan changes.
- "It says the cleaning instructions should include specific procedures, but does not give them." This is true but reads like filler.
- "The roughly 100 miles reported for the Army's first maps was lead in the air. The air map's edge is the smallest amount the Army's samplers can detect, and the maps assume all the lead is released." It explains a correction nobody asked for.
- The headings "The Catch" with "When did the Army publish its plume maps?" and "What the coverage got right and wrong" announce structure rather than say anything.
- Verdict labels like "checks out, for the first maps" and "overstated" read like grading rubrics.
- "The same day, the groups asked the court to accept sworn statements, including from experts who question whether waiting for the court would make the material more dangerous." It's dropped into the date paragraph with no payoff, since the ruling never says how the court treated them.

One error in my own summary: the last sentence of item 1 starts with "Penn..." and then restarts. Read it as "the Army told people to stay inside and hose off surfaces, and a University of Iowa professor says hosing moves the lead into soil and water."
