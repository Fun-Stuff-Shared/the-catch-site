**1. Five sentences**

The Army has 68 drums of old lead azide, an unstable explosive, in two bunkers at an Iowa ammunition plant. It plans to blow them up in place, which sends lead dust into the air for miles. The blast was set for Friday but moved to Monday after Iowa and Illinois officials and the governor asked for a delay, and after two environmental groups sued. The Army's plume maps shrank between Nov. 15 and Nov. 18, and the Army has told residents to stay inside and hose off surfaces afterward. The groups say EPA didn't properly consider safer methods and took no public comment, while the Army and EPA say the material can't be moved.

**2. What it's really about**

The Army is about to release lead over a wide area with late, shifting public information, and nobody on either side disputes the explosive is dangerous.

**3. Questions I still had**

- What are the actual health risks of the lead at the modeled levels? The Army gave no numbers, and I don't know what "temporary and reversible discomfort" means in practice.
- How many people live inside the plume or the dust area?
- Will the court rule before Monday?
- What happens if the temperature drops before the Army can blast?
- Does the Monday evacuation near the two parks still apply?
- Why did the maps shrink from 100 miles to 60 so quickly? Did the Army change its assumptions or its method?
- Why did the 20-year-old storage bath get no inspection until March?
- Is the Army's March claim ("immediately began communicating") false or only generous? The table implies it but never says so.
- What is MuniRem, and would it work on this material?
- Why does the lede say 9,700 pounds "by the count in a letter" from officials? Does the Army give a different number?
- Why did EPA's Iowa office not tell Illinois?

**4. Sentences that sounded machine-written**

- "Even the groups' side allows:" announces the quote and sounds like a setup.
- "The Army's spokesperson, Justine Barati, told Radio Iowa it was simpler than that." is a wry framing the reader didn't need before the quote.
- "Telle says the Army warned the area from March. Here is what the Army's own records show." announces the table.
- "Those results are what will show where the lead actually landed." is a summing-up line.
- "The record that would show how the Army weighed the other methods is its analysis, sent to EPA in letters in August and September, which the groups say EPA has not made public." is clumsy and circular.
- "The roughly 100 miles reported for the Army's first maps was lead in the air. The air map's edge is the smallest amount the Army's samplers can detect, and the maps assume all the lead is released." is explaining a rebuttal nobody asked for.
- "By the environmental groups' reading, the settling dust covered a far smaller area, as the map table above shows." has a hedge plus a pointer back to the table.
- "The Intercept, Nov. 19: overstated" and "CBS News Chicago, Nov. 18: checks out, for the first maps" are report-card headings, not reporting.
- "It says the cleaning instructions should include specific procedures, but does not give them." is stilted.
- "The Army says levels of lead in the air may be elevated but well below the level at which EPA would require protective action." stacks hedges.
