**1. Five sentences**

- The Army has 68 drums (about 9,700 lb) of lead azide, an explosive that degraded in storage at an Iowa ammunition plant. It plans to blow them up in place, because it says they can't be moved.
- The blast was set for Nov. 20 and then pushed back. Iowa's governor and Illinois officials asked for a delay, environmental groups sued, and an appeals court paused EPA's emergency permit on Nov. 19.
- The government now says the Army will detonate by Nov. 24 anyway, under a separate emergency exclusion that doesn't need the permit. It argues that cold weather makes an uncontrolled explosion the bigger danger.
- The Army's maps show a lead plume that could travel far (100 miles in the first maps, 60 in the updated ones). The Army says the levels will be low, while the groups and their experts doubt that waiting is more dangerous and say safer methods weren't properly weighed.
- The Army told the public late, and as of Nov. 22 it had still given no date and no updated plume modeling.

**2. What it's really about**

The Army sat on a decaying explosive, told the public late, and now says an emergency lets it skip a court's pause.

**3. Questions I still had**

- What is the Catch? The section opens with "When did the Army publish its plume maps?" and never answers it in one plain line. I assume it's the gap between Telle's "immediately began communicating" and a first map on Nov. 15, but the story doesn't say so.
- Telle said Monday was the latest the Army could wait, but the date is now Tuesday, Nov. 24. Why did that deadline move?
- What does lead at that level do to a person? "Temporary and reversible discomfort" is the only health detail, and the Army gave no numbers for nearby towns.
- Does the emergency exclusion legally let the Army ignore the court's pause? The story never says what the court or a legal expert thinks.
- Is Thomas's cold-weather argument right? He says the material has already been through similar cold. The story doesn't say what the Army or EPA answer.
- Why is 7–22 days of chemical treatment "unacceptable risk" when the material is also said to be about to blow up on its own?
- What happened between March and October? The inspection was in March but the public announcement came in October.
- What do the table rows mean? Dust "more than 40 miles long" and "about 25 miles long" are unexplained, and the story doesn't say whether that's good or bad.
- Testing only starts inside the "heavier" dust level. What about people outside it?
- The Nov. 17 plume maps (source 9) and the Nov. 18 "updated" maps both appear. Which maps are current?
- Who decides if the court rules after the blast has happened?
