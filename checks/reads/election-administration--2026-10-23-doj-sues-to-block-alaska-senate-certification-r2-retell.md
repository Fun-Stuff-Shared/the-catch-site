**1. Five sentences**
- The Justice Department sued Alaska on Oct. 22 to stop it from certifying its Senate result in one case: Sen. Dan Sullivan loses, but would have won if every vote for a second candidate, Daniel J. Sullivan, had gone to him.
- If that happens, the department wants the result held while it investigates whether those voters meant to vote for the senator.
- The department says the two similar names will confuse voters. Alaska's elections division removed the second Sullivan from the ballot, but state courts put him back. He then drew 2.5% in the August primary.
- Trump said he ordered the suit. Election-law experts say the voter-registration law it relies on doesn't support it, and they expect it to be dismissed.
- Nothing is urgent yet: there is no emergency request, early voting is under way, and the election is Nov. 3.

**2. What it's really about**
The federal government is trying to build a legal route to contest a Senate loss before it happens, using a statute that probably doesn't cover it.

**3. Questions I still had**
- Why would a voter-registration law cover vote counting at all? The article says only that the department points to one clause, and it never explains the connection.
- Can a hold on certification start before Nov. 3? Has Judge Gleason set a schedule or hearing?
- What happens if the state certifies anyway?
- How would the investigation find out what voters "meant" if ballots are secret?
- Was Daniel J. Sullivan really a plant? The story gives the department's charge, the state courts' rejection of it, and the Anchorage Press analysis, but doesn't tell me who recruited him.
- What is an "exhausted ballot," and why did the poll's numbers shift when those ballots went to the senator?
- Blanche's quote, "I'm not trying to not have an election certified," is a double negative. What did he mean?
- Why is the California suit, or the Supreme Court mail-ballot ruling, in this story?
- Muller says "a wide Democratic win or a narrow Republican one could make it moot." Why would a narrow Republican win do that?
- Who would settle a close loss if the suit fails? Morley's line about the Senate judging its own members comes at the very end and is easy to miss.

**4. Sentences that sounded machine-written**
- "Much of the first coverage described the suit as blocking certification if Sullivan loses, if a Democrat wins, or if Daniel J. Sullivan stays on the ballot, or as blocking the vote." It lists wrong versions and makes me parse them.
- "A loss that still stands in that hypothetical count does not meet the test, and what the suit asks to stop is certification, pending an investigation." It is dense and restates the point.
- "Meeting it would not make the senator the winner." It's a correction of something I never believed.
- "Beyond the headlines that dropped the second condition, one report got the test itself wrong, turning it into a sum of votes." It's media-scoring, and the whole "What the coverage got wrong" section is too.
- "Three smaller errors do not change the account of the suit." It is announcing, and it says nothing a reader needs.
- "The clause is the third of four purposes the law lists. The other three are..." This is explanation nobody asked for.
- "Trump's stated aim was different from the suit's." It's a summing-up line that could be cut.
- "That is how Derek Muller of Notre Dame reads it: he said the suit would likely only kick in if Republicans narrowly lost." Its "it" is unclear, and it comes after a sentence about the CBS and Washington Post reports.
- "The department's reading" and "The critics' reading." These are label-style subheads. The first section is two sentences, and the critics' section stacks four attributions.
- "maximize differentiation." It's bureaucratic jargon. The story quotes it without saying what it means.
- "The Catch / 'If Republican Loses'." This is a quoted heading with no speaker.
