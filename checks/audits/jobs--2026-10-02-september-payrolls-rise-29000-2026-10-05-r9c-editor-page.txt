The jobs report · September 2026
Payrolls rose by an estimated 29,000 in September, a change too small for the survey to tell from none
Economists surveyed by Reuters expected payrolls to rise by 90,000. The Bureau of Labor Statistics said payrolls and the 4.2 percent unemployment rate both changed little. July and August together are 60,000 lower than previously reported.

What happened
The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Friday, October 2, 2026, that payroll employment rose by 29,000 in September and the unemployment rate was 4.2 percent, and said both changed little.
Private payrolls rose by 46,000 and government payrolls fell by 17,000.
The bureau also revised the two prior months down by 60,000 together: August from a gain of 162,000 to 133,000, and July from a gain of 21,000 to a loss of 10,000.
The establishment survey counts jobs on employer payrolls. The household survey counts people, and individuals are counted only once. A jobs number and a people number can move differently in the same month. The payroll count comes from the first survey, and the unemployment rate from the second.
The Federal Reserve's two goals are maximum employment and price stability. Its primary means of adjusting the stance of monetary policy is through changes in the target range for the federal funds rate. On September 16 it raised that range by 1/4 percentage point in support of those goals. This report is the employment reading before that committee meets on October 27-28; the next one, for October, is scheduled for November 6. On prices, the personal consumption expenditures price index for August, whose annual change is how the committee measures inflation, was up 3.4 percent from a year earlier. This report put average hourly earnings up 3.0 percent over the past 12 months.
The catch
USA Today headlined the release “September jobs report shows slowdown in hiring, unemployment ticks up”. The bureau's table shows the rounded unemployment rate up from 4.1 to 4.2, and its summary says payrolls and that rate both “changed little”, which describes the size of the move. The 29,000 is a net change in payroll jobs, not a count of hires, and it is under about 122,000, the monthly change the bureau says is statistically significant. Hires, “all additions to the payroll” during the month, are counted in a separate survey, and September's count is scheduled for November 3.
By the household survey's estimates, employment rose by 406,000, which is under the 650,000 that survey needs before a change is statistically significant. The labor force, the employed plus the unemployed, rose by 485,000. The unemployment rate is the number unemployed as a percent of the labor force. The labor force grew by more than employment did, so the count of unemployed rose by 78,000 and the rate went from 4.1 to 4.2 percent.
Most of that tenth is rounding. Before rounding, the rate “increased slightly from 4.141% to 4.175%”, the St. Louis Fed wrote on October 2, a rise of about 0.03 point. The rate has stayed between 4.1 percent and 4.3 percent since March.
The bureau named one group whose rate increased: Black workers, from 6.0 percent in August to 7.0 percent in September. A year earlier it was 7.6 percent.

September's 29,000 followed August's revised 133,000. The bureau's 45,000 a month is its average over the prior 12 months, and September is lower. The Associated Press's 68,000 a month is 2026 so far, and its 9,700 is 2025. The three averages cover different months, which is why they are not the same pace.
What we do not know yet
The payroll figures for August and September are preliminary: the bureau says they rest on incomplete returns from the employers it surveys. The release states no cause for the 29,000 change. Whether hiring slowed is not known yet: hires are counted separately, August's hires were 5.2 million, and September's are scheduled for November 3.
