Immigration detention · October 1, 2026 · updated 2026-10-03
The Supreme Court agreed to decide whether people already in the country who were never admitted must be held without a bond hearing
The October 1 order is not a ruling and not a count of people. If the detention statute applies, a person has no opportunity for release on bond, and the government's memorandum allows release only by parole. Under the bond statute the government may release someone.
156 days From the Second Circuit's April 28 decision to the grant
The grant
What happened
On October 1, 2026, the Supreme Court granted review of whether the detention statute requires holding, during removal proceedings, people who, like Ricardo Aparecido Barbosa da Cunha, are in the United States and were not admitted. The October 1 order grants review and does not decide the case. The bond statute is the statute under which the government may release someone.
For about thirty years, by the Second Circuit's account, people present without admission could ask for bond. In July 2025 the government said the detention statute covers them instead. On September 5, 2025, the Board of Immigration Appeals said immigration judges lack authority to grant them bond. On September 26, 2025, officers arrested Barbosa da Cunha while he was driving to work in Norwood, Massachusetts; an immigration judge denied him a bond hearing, a district court ordered a bond hearing or release within ten days, and an immigration judge released him on bond after finding no danger and no flight risk. On April 28, 2026, the Second Circuit ruled the other way from the Board in Barbosa da Cunha's case. On October 1 the Supreme Court agreed to decide the question.
The detention statute uses two descriptions of the person, an applicant for admission and an alien seeking admission. It tells an immigration officer to detain that person for a removal proceeding when the officer decides the person seeking admission is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted.
Detention statute · 2024 compilation · Inspection of applicants for admission
in the case of an alien who is an applicant for admission, if the examining immigration officer determines that an alien seeking admission is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted, the alien shall be detained
Government Publishing Office
The Second Circuit held that Barbosa da Cunha may be an applicant for admission but is not seeking admission, because he is not requesting lawful entry after inspection and authorization; he was already living here. So the court held that the detention statute does not apply to someone in the United States after entering without inspection and admission who was not apprehended while entering the country or shortly thereafter. The Second Circuit affirmed the district court, which had required a bond hearing or release. The hearing did not itself require release. He has never been arrested for or charged with a crime. Under the Department of Homeland Security's July 10 memorandum, an applicant for admission may not be released from custody except by parole. That memorandum says the only people eligible for release on bond are people already admitted.
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit · April 28, 2026 · Opinion
Petitioner may be an “applicant for admission,” but he is not “seeking admission.”
Barbosa da Cunha
Barbosa da Cunha entered around 2005, lived in the United States for more than twenty years, applied for asylum in 2016, and held a work permit. If the government wins, people in his situation cannot be released on bond: the Second Circuit wrote that if the detention statute applies, he "must be detained during the removal process with no opportunity for release on bond". Under the Department of Homeland Security's July 10 memorandum, release from custody is then possible only by parole. By the grant the dispute had reached every numbered federal appeals court: on September 10 the Fourth Circuit wrote that the First, Second, Third, Sixth, Seventh, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits had concluded that the bond statute applies to noncitizens inside the country, like the petitioners in those appeals, who must be given a bond hearing, and that the Fifth and Eighth Circuits had ruled that the detention statute applies to all noncitizens who have not been admitted.
The government's petition asked the Court to wait on this case. It said the Court should hold this petition pending another petition, Lopez-Campos, which it called a "better vehicle" for resolving whether holding those people is lawful, and then dispose of this petition as appropriate. The October 1 order granted this petition.
The coverage
The catch
The count CBS News wrote that the Court would consider whether the administration can detain tens of thousands of immigrants during removal proceedings without bond hearings. That count is not the question the Court granted. On September 10 the Fourth Circuit wrote that district courts sided with the petitioners in over twenty thousand individual cases. That figure counts cases, not people held. None of the court or agency records states how many people are held under the government's reading.
The grant The Associated Press wrote that the Court agreed to review a policy denying bond hearings.
The notice Customs and Border Protection's public notice describes mandatory detention for applicants for admission the department encounters for the first time after they fail to lawfully present for inspection at a port of entry. The July 10 memorandum says the first encounter counts regardless of when or where it happens. Barbosa da Cunha was arrested driving to work in Norwood, years after he entered.

On March 6, 1997, a Federal Register rule said that people present without having been admitted or paroled would be eligible for bond and bond redetermination. The Second Circuit describes the executive practice that followed as thirty years old. In July 2025 the government changed that practice and said the detention statute, rather than the bond statute, is the detention authority for every applicant for admission. On September 5, 2025, the Board of Immigration Appeals said immigration judges lack authority to grant bond to people present in the United States without admission. On April 28, 2026, the Second Circuit held the opposite for someone already present who was not apprehended while entering the country or shortly thereafter, and it affirmed. On September 25 the full court refused rehearing. Judge Steven J. Menashi dissented and argued that the detention statute applies and that Barbosa da Cunha must be detained without a bond hearing. Judge Matthew A. Schwartz voted to deny rehearing and wrote that he agreed with that dissent. On October 1 the Supreme Court granted review.

Open questions
What we do not know yet
This case has a grant date of October 1 and no argument day on the list of cases set for argument. The question the Court accepted is the statute. The government's petition says a petition from the Sixth Circuit in Lopez-Campos also presents whether applying the detention statute comports with due process. The Second Circuit said it need not rely on constitutional avoidance, the practice of reading a statute so as not to decide a constitutional problem. The petition says the Second Circuit did not reach a standalone holding on the constitutionality of detaining him without a bond hearing. Two related petitions, one from the Fifth Circuit in Buenrostro-Mendez and one from the Sixth Circuit in Lopez-Campos, have not been granted.
