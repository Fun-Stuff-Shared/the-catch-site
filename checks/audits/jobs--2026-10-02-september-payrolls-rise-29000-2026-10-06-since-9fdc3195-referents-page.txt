The jobs report · September 2026
Payrolls rose by an estimated 29,000 in September, a change too small for the survey to tell from none
Economists surveyed by Reuters expected payrolls to rise by 90,000. The Bureau of Labor Statistics said payrolls and the 4.2 percent unemployment rate both changed little. July and August together are 60,000 lower than previously reported.

What happened
The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Friday, October 2, 2026, that payroll employment rose by 29,000 in September and the unemployment rate was 4.2 percent, and said both changed little.
Private payrolls rose by 46,000 and government payrolls fell by 17,000, with 10,600 of that in local government outside education.
Kevin Hassett, the director of the National Economic Council, told reporters outside the White House on October 2 that “private sector employment is still booming.” His account of the jobs numbers was “What's happened is because we've reduced federal employment by 300,000 workers, then the numbers, uh, are a little bit lower if you don't account for that.” He also said that “50% of the job creation was hiring federal government workers” over “the last two years,” and he did not name the months.
Federal payrolls were 3,009,000 in December 2024 and 2,682,000 in September 2026, a decline of 327,000. September's own federal change was a drop of 1,000, from 2,683,000 in August. The 1,000 is September, so that longer decline does not account for this month's payroll change. He gave the period as “over the last two years under Joe Biden,” the president, with no months, and January 2023 to January 2025 are the last two years of that term. In those months federal payrolls rose 130,000 while total payrolls rose 3,492,000, about 3.7 percent of the gain, not half.
The bureau also revised the two prior months down by 60,000 together: August from a gain of 162,000 to 133,000, and July from a gain of 21,000 to a loss of 10,000.
The establishment survey counts jobs on employer payrolls, and the household survey counts people. The payroll figure comes from the first and the unemployment rate from the second, so the two can move differently in one month.
The Federal Reserve's two goals are maximum employment and price stability. On September 16 the Federal Open Market Committee raised the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point. The committee's goals statement says it “considers a wide range of indicators” in assessing maximum employment. The committee raised the target range on September 16, this report came out October 2, and the committee meets October 27-28. The report is employment data available before that meeting, and the October decision is not known.
Reuters wrote that the slowdown was “almost taking another interest rate hike from the Federal Reserve this month off the table”. The same dispatch said financial markets initially lowered bets on a rate hike at the October 27-28 meeting to 13 percent, then lifted them to about 23 percent. That later figure was little changed from Thursday, CME Group's FedWatch tool showed.
The next employment report, for October, is scheduled for November 6. On prices, the personal consumption expenditures price index for August, whose annual change is how the committee measures inflation, was up 3.4 percent from a year earlier. This report put average hourly earnings up 3.0 percent over the past 12 months.
Chris Phelan, chair of the President's Council of Economic Advisers, argued that the central bank's decision to raise rates last month was a mistake because inflation is already coming down. The Federal Open Market Committee's September 16 statement says “Inflation remains elevated.” Philip N. Jefferson, a vice chair of the Federal Reserve Board, said on October 1 that he supported that decision because he saw it as the appropriate policy to pursue the Fed's dual mandate. He also said he sees “economic activity and labor market conditions remaining broadly solid while inflation remains above our target.”
The catch
USA Today headlined the release “September jobs report shows slowdown in hiring, unemployment ticks up”. The 29,000 is a net change in payroll jobs, not a count of hires, and it is under about 122,000, the monthly change the bureau says is statistically significant. Hires, “all additions to the payroll” during the month, are counted in a separate survey.
The rounded unemployment rate rose from 4.1 to 4.2. Beside that rise, the bureau's summary says payrolls and that rate both “changed little,” its words for a change its surveys cannot distinguish.
By the household survey's estimates, employment rose by 406,000, which is under the 650,000 that survey needs before a change is statistically significant. The labor force, the employed plus the unemployed, rose by 485,000. The unemployment rate is the number unemployed as a percent of the labor force. The labor force grew by more than employment did, so the count of unemployed rose by 78,000 and the rate went from 4.1 to 4.2 percent.
Most of that tenth is rounding. Before rounding, the rate “increased slightly from 4.141% to 4.175%”, the St. Louis Fed wrote on October 2, a rise of about 0.03 point. The rate has stayed between 4.1 percent and 4.3 percent since March.
The bureau named one group whose rate increased: Black workers, from 6.0 percent in August to 7.0 percent in September. A year earlier it was 7.6 percent.

September's 29,000 followed August's revised 133,000. It is below the bureau's average monthly gain of 45,000 over the prior 12 months. The bureau's three-month average is 51,000 a month for the three months through September 2026, against 23,000 for the three months through September 2025. The bureau's payroll series rose by about 9,700 jobs a month in 2025, from December 2024 to December 2025, and by about 68,000 a month through September 2026.
Break-even employment growth, a Federal Reserve note dated April 2 said, is “the number of jobs that must be added to the economy each month to keep the unemployment rate steady while still absorbing the growth in the potential labor force.” The note says that monthly pace rose to 155,000 in 2023-24 because of a surge in net immigration, then fell to an estimated 85,000 in 2025 as net immigration slowed. It says the 2026 pace could require less than 10,000 new jobs a month, and that the estimates are inherently uncertain. A low break-even means fewer jobs keep the unemployment rate steady. It does not mean hiring is healthy.
The Dallas Fed wrote on March 31 that net unauthorized immigration plummeted after early 2024, with net outflows beginning in February 2025. Christopher J. Waller, a governor of the Federal Reserve Board, said on February 23 that “close to zero net job creation over 2025 indicates a weak, and fragile job market.”
Reuters said economists estimated that the economy needs to create roughly 50,000 jobs per month to keep up with growth in the working-age population. Chris Phelan, chair of the President's Council of Economic Advisers, calculated that the economy needs to add only about 40,000 jobs per month to keep the unemployment rate at its current level. The Associated Press wrote that “the break-even point could now be as low as zero jobs a month, down from 150,000 a year or two ago”.
What we do not know yet
The payroll figures for August and September are preliminary: the bureau says they rest on incomplete returns from the employers it surveys. The release states no cause for the 29,000 change. Reuters wrote that economists noted payrolls “have a tendency to underperform when the Labor Day holiday falls relatively late in September, as was the case this year.” The bureau's count of hires for September, in a separate report scheduled for November 3, has not been published, and the 29,000 is a net payroll change, not a count of hires. The Associated Press wrote that a measure of gross hiring “has been stuck in a rut for more than two years.” The hires rate was 4.2 percent in August 2022, 3.3 percent in August 2024, and 3.3 percent in August 2026. August's hires were 5.2 million. The September count is scheduled for November 3.
