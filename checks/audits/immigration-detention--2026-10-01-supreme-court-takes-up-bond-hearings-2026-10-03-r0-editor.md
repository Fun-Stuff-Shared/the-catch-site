## First read

The page told me that the Supreme Court will decide whether people already living in the United States without admission must be detained through removal proceedings, after a long bond practice and a split among courts.

At the end, I still wanted to know who would lose a bond hearing if the government wins, exactly what changed in 2025, and whether the case had moved beyond the grant.

I first lost the thread at: “The Second Circuit held that Barbosa da Cunha may be an applicant for admission and is not seeking admission, and held that the detention statute does not apply to someone in the United States after entering without inspection and admission who was not apprehended while entering the country or shortly thereafter.”

## The story

The record supports this story: a 2025 change in detention practice led to the denial of a bond hearing for a man arrested years after entry; the Second Circuit held that he could seek bond, other appeals courts split over the statute, and on October 1 the Supreme Court agreed to decide that statutory question. The [Customs memorandum](/Volumes/4/GitHub/the-catch-site-wt-detention/data/sources/immigration-detention/cbp-detention-memo-2025-07-10.txt) says “section 235 of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), rather than section 236, is the applicable immigration detention authority for all applicants for admission.” The [order](/Volumes/4/GitHub/the-catch-site-wt-detention/data/sources/immigration-detention/scotus-order-list-2026-10-01.txt) says “The petitions for writs of certiorari are granted.”

The page has the acts but makes the reader assemble their order: the changed practice, the denied hearing and bond release, the appellate ruling, the widened split, then the grant. No single passage near the top gives that sequence with dates. The opening act is clear; the consequence and reach arrive late or by inference. The unresolved statutory ruling is clear, while the absence of an argument date and the separate due process question sit inside a crowded procedural paragraph.

## The top

The first story sentence says what happened. The headline survives the easiest wrong reading because “agreed to decide” does not claim the Court approved detention. It does not tell a cold reader that *bond hearings* are at stake. The dek opens with two corrections and describes an unnamed person; the full name arrives in the lead, so no one is named before introduction. “Detention statute,” “bond statute,” and “were not admitted” do work before the page explains them. **The top fails the cold-reader test despite its accurate first sentence.**
