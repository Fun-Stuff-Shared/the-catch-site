The jobs report · September 2026
Payrolls rose by an estimated 29,000 in September, a change too small for the survey to tell from none
Economists surveyed by Reuters expected payrolls to rise by 90,000. The Bureau of Labor Statistics said payrolls and the 4.2 percent unemployment rate both changed little. July and August together are 60,000 lower than previously reported.

What happened
On Friday, October 2, 2026, payroll employment rose by an estimated 29,000 in September and the unemployment rate was 4.2 percent. Both changed little.
Private payrolls rose by 46,000 and government payrolls fell by 17,000, with 10,600 of that in local government outside education.
Federal job cuts do not account for September's payroll number. Kevin Hassett, the director of the National Economic Council, argued on October 2 that federal job cuts make the total look weaker than private hiring, which he called still booming. Federal payrolls are down about 327,000 since December 2024, but only about 1,000 of that came in September from the August level, so the cuts do not account for September's payroll change. He said half of job creation was federal hiring “over the last two years under Joe Biden,” the president, and he did not name the months. From January 2023 to January 2025, that share of the payroll gain is about 4 percent.

July and August were revised down by 60,000 together: August from a gain of 162,000 to 133,000, and July from a gain of 21,000 to a loss of 10,000.
The payroll figure counts jobs on employer payrolls, and the unemployment rate counts people. The two counts come from different surveys, so they can move differently in one month.
Maximum employment and price stability are the Federal Reserve's goals, and price stability is inflation at 2 percent. Maximum employment is judged from a wide range of indicators. On September 16 the Federal Open Market Committee raised the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point, to 3-3/4 to 4 percent. This report came out October 2, the committee meets October 27-28, and the October decision is not known. Inflation “remains elevated.” Chris Phelan, chair of the President's Council of Economic Advisers, called the September increase a mistake because inflation is already coming down. Philip N. Jefferson, a vice chair of the Federal Reserve Board, defended it on October 1 because economic activity and the labor market looked broadly solid while inflation remained above that goal.
Bets on a rate increase at the October 27-28 meeting fell to 13 percent at first, then rose to about 23 percent, little changed from Thursday. One account of the slowdown had it “almost taking another interest rate hike from the Federal Reserve this month off the table”.
The next employment report, for October, is scheduled for November 6. August inflation, measured by the annual change in the personal consumption expenditures price index, was up 3.4 percent from a year earlier, and average hourly earnings were up 3.0 percent over the past 12 months.
The catch
USA Today headlined the release “September jobs report shows slowdown in hiring, unemployment ticks up”. The 29,000 is a net change in payroll jobs, not a count of hires, and it is under about 122,000, the monthly change counted as statistically significant.
The rounded unemployment rate rose from 4.1 to 4.2. Beside that rise, payrolls and the rate both “changed little,” the words for a change the surveys cannot distinguish.
By the household survey's estimates, employment rose by 406,000, which is under the 650,000 that survey needs before a change is statistically significant. The labor force, the employed plus the unemployed, rose by 485,000. The unemployment rate is the number unemployed as a percent of the labor force. The labor force grew by more than employment did, so the count of unemployed rose by 78,000 and the rate went from 4.1 to 4.2 percent.
Most of that tenth is rounding. A St. Louis Fed note dated October 2 says “More precise data show the rate increased slightly from 4.141% to 4.175%.” That is a rise of about 0.03 point. The rate has stayed between 4.1 percent and 4.3 percent since March.
The unemployment rate for Black workers rose from 6.0 percent in August to 7.0 percent in September. A year earlier it was 7.6 percent. The overall rate moved by a tenth.

2026 has averaged about 68,000 jobs a month through September, against about 9,700 a month in 2025, counted from December 2024.
September's 29,000 is below the average monthly gain of 45,000 over the prior 12 months, and below surveys that expected about 90,000. The unemployment rate, at 4.2 percent, has stayed between 4.1 percent and 4.3 percent since March.
Break-even is the monthly job gain that holds the unemployment rate steady as the potential labor force grows. The estimate fell from about 155,000 a month in 2023-24 to about 85,000 in 2025 as net immigration slowed. In 2026 it could be under 10,000 a month, after that same slowing. Net unauthorized immigration fell after early 2024, and outflows began in February 2025. A low break-even means fewer jobs hold the unemployment rate steady, not that hiring is healthy. Christopher J. Waller, a governor of the Federal Reserve Board, said on February 23 that “close to zero net job creation over 2025 indicates a weak, and fragile job market.” The estimates are uncertain.

What we do not know yet
The payroll figures for August and September are still preliminary. They rest on incomplete returns from the employers surveyed. The release states no cause for the 29,000 change. Economists noted that payrolls “have a tendency to underperform when the Labor Day holiday falls relatively late in September, as was the case this year.”
September's 29,000 is a net change in payroll jobs, not a count of hires. August's hires changed little, at 5.2 million, and September's count comes out November 3. Hiring has run at a low rate for more than two years, at 3.3 percent in August 2026 and in August 2024, against 4.2 percent in August 2022.
