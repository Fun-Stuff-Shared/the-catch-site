## 8\. Reconstructed Story

Before 1996, detention treatment largely followed physical entry: a person who had entered and was placed in deportation proceedings could seek bond, while someone seeking entry at the border was generally detained. Congress rewrote the system in 1996 around lawful admission and unified exclusion and deportation into removal proceedings, but the legislative history supplies arguments to both sides of the present detention dispute. A 1997 rule expressly preserved bond eligibility for people already present without admission, and that remained executive practice for roughly thirty years.

DHS changed course in July 2025 and concluded that section 235 requires mandatory detention for applicants for admission even when their first encounter with immigration authorities occurs years after an unlawful entry. The Board adopted that interpretation. Barbosa da Cunha, who had lived in the United States for more than twenty years, was arrested while driving to work and denied a bond hearing under that position. A district court ordered a hearing or release, and an immigration judge released him after finding no danger or flight risk.

The courts then divided over the statutory text. The Second Circuit held that a person like Barbosa da Cunha can be statutorily deemed an “applicant for admission” without presently “seeking admission,” making the bond statute applicable. Nine circuits ultimately reached the bond-hearing side of the split, while the Fifth and Eighth Circuits adopted the government's reading.

On October 1 the Supreme Court granted Rhoney to resolve that statutory question. It has not decided the merits. The due-process question remains on a separate track involving Lopez-Campos and the Genalo litigation.
