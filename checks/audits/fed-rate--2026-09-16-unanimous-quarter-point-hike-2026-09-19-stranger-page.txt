The federal funds rate · September 16, 2026
Fed raises rates a quarter point on a unanimous vote
The quarter-point increase cannot lower oil prices. It targets demand and the spread of the energy shock, with the quickest effects reaching variable-rate borrowers and savers.

The decision
What happened
The Federal Open Market Committee voted 12–0 to raise its target range by one-quarter percentage point, to 3.75–4.00 percent.
record
The new range took effect September 17. The Fed also raised the rate paid on reserve balances to 3.90 percent and the primary-credit rate to 4 percent.
The first increase since 2023 Upper limit of the target range
The upper bound fell in six steps from September 2024 through December 2025, then rose a quarter point effective September 17, 2026.
Seven weeks earlier, the committee had held the range at 3.50–3.75 percent by a 9–3 vote. The July minutes say most participants wanted more information before acting, while several already favored a quarter-point increase.
By late August, Chair Kevin Warsh had set a public test: underlying inflation had to move toward the Fed's goal “clearly and at sufficient speed.” The next official CPI release showed gasoline up 3.9 percent in August, accounting for more than one-third of the monthly increase, while core CPI was 2.4 percent over the year.
Growth was not uniformly fast: real GDP increased at a 1.5 percent annual rate in the second quarter, down from 2.1 percent in the first. Private domestic final sales, consumer spending plus private fixed investment, grew 4.2 percent in the same quarter.
Warsh highlighted three considerations in explaining the September decision: the economy had strengthened, his judgment that summer inflation trends had not passed the test remained, and the committee's geopolitical assessment had changed. The September statement described resilient domestic spending, robust capital investment and elevated inflation. Warsh described the increase as removing “a dose of accommodation” so financial and credit conditions would be more consistent with the Fed's objectives.

The mechanism
What a rate hike can change
The Fed raises the cost of borrowing to slow consumer and business spending, reducing demand.
The Associated Press traced the oil and gasoline shock to the war with Iran, which was in its seventh month by the day after the decision.
A rate increase cannot set the price of oil or gasoline. Warsh said the Fed cannot affect an individual price; it can try to stop a relative-price shock from broadening into second- and third-order effects across the economy.
record
The August CPI increase was partly an energy-price event: gasoline supplied more than a third of the monthly rise. Warsh framed the Fed's task as preventing that relative-price shock from producing second- and third-order effects across the economy.
CBO described the route by which the shock could spread: lower oil and gas shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, disruption to refining and Red Sea shipping, and then higher transport costs across goods and services. It estimated that energy added 2.3 percentage points to the annualized second-quarter PCE inflation rate and that core PCE inflation would stay elevated longer as energy costs passed into nonenergy prices.
The effect is delayed. The Fed's strategy statement says monetary policy influences activity, employment and prices with a lag, which creates a risk that an energy shock fades before tighter credit has fully slowed the economy.
Borrowers and savers
Who feels it
Credit-card rates can respond quickly because most cards carry variable rates tied to banks' prime rate. Auto loans and adjustable-rate mortgages can also become more expensive, while savings accounts and certificates of deposit may pay more.
checked
Fixed mortgage rates are different: they tend to follow the 10-year Treasury yield, not the Fed's rate directly. Homeowners with fixed loans do not have their existing rate reset by this decision.
Consumer-finance analyst Matt Schulz told the Associated Press that one quarter-point increase would not have a large effect. He said the larger burden would come if several increases stack up over time.
Warsh said persistent inflation falls especially hard on people without financial assets, home equity or retirement-plan equity who live from paycheck to paycheck. For them, the benefit of restoring stable prices is not an investment return but protection of real take-home pay.
The signal beyond the vote
The projections
The median projected federal funds rate for the end of 2026 rose from 3.8 percent in June to 4.1 percent in September. For 2027, the median rose from 3.6 percent in June to 4.1 percent in September.
Figure 2 shows several individual end-of-year rate endpoints around the median, not a meeting-by-meeting path. Each circle is one participant's judgment of the appropriate year-end midpoint or target level. Warsh did not submit a projection of his own.
The same projection table put median PCE inflation at 3.7 percent for 2026, up from 3.6 percent in June, and at 2.3 percent for 2027. It does not reach 2 percent until 2029.
The projection document defines the dots as participants' individual assessments of appropriate monetary policy. Warsh said his practice is not to give forward guidance or prejudge future actions.

The catch
The 2027 median was 4.1 percent.
CBS wrote that policymakers expected to hold rates steady throughout 2027. The record says something narrower: the median of individual projections was 4.1 percent in both years, about half of participants projected a steady rate next year, and the chair declined to give forward guidance.
One vote, eighteen projections.
Twelve committee members voted on the rate increase. Eighteen participants submitted projections. The vote applied to the September action; the projection record contains several paths after it.
The White House and the central bank
The pressure around the decision
About two hours after the announcement, The Hill reported that President Donald Trump demanded rates of 1 percent or less and called for the Fed to lower them fast.
checked
The next morning, DealBook reported that Trump said he had told Warsh to vote with the board because the result would not change, and described the board as “very hostile.” The report also quoted Trump calling it “a very tough board.”
Warsh described central-bank independence as “a two-way street” and said trade and fiscal policymakers should stay in their lane.
The dispute was also about what a supply shock calls for. Before the meeting, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent argued that central banks traditionally do not raise rates after a supply shock unless second- or third-order effects appear, and said core inflation remained restrained.
September 17 and 18
What happened next
The next day, the 10-year Treasury yield slipped. The Associated Press described that as a likely sign that investors' inflation worries had eased, while noting that mortgage rates and other long-term borrowing costs are not directly controlled by the Fed.
checked
By September 18, CNBC reported that market-implied odds of another increase in October had risen to about 58 percent from 42 percent a week earlier. Goldman Sachs expected an October increase, Bank of America expected increases in October and December, and one strategist said the move was not the start of an aggressive new tightening cycle.

Open questions
What we do not know yet
Whether another increase follows this one. The projections are individual assessments of appropriate monetary policy, and Warsh declined to prejudge the next decision.
How long the energy shock lasts. Monetary policy works with a lag, and the Associated Press described the risk that the gas-price shock could be over before higher borrowing costs started to slow the economy.
How much households ultimately pay. The Associated Press analysis says several increases would stack up over time, while fixed mortgage rates can move differently from the policy rate.
What the next inflation and growth vintages show. BEA scheduled August PCE and the annual national-account updates, including GDP revisions, for September 30.
What the September deliberations add. The official calendar says regular-meeting minutes are released three weeks after the decision; its September minutes slot was blank in the September 19 capture.
