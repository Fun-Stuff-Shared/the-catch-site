Immigration detention · October 1, 2026 · updated 2026-10-05
The Supreme Court agreed to decide whether people already in the country who were never admitted must be held without a bond hearing
The Court's October 1 order takes the case and decides nothing yet. Since July 2025 the government has said people arrested inside the country who had entered without inspection must be held, and the appeals courts have split.
The grant
What happened
On October 1, 2026, the Supreme Court agreed to decide whether the government must hold, while their removal cases are pending, people who are living in the United States and were never admitted. Admitted means the lawful entry of a person into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer. The order takes the case; it does not decide it. The detention statute deems a person present in the United States who has not been admitted an applicant for admission.
The bond statute says the government may keep an arrested person in custody or may release that person on bond. A hearing is a chance at release, not release itself. An immigration judge found that Ricardo Aparecido Barbosa da Cunha presents no danger to persons or property and no risk of flight, and released him on bond. If the government wins, people in his situation cannot be released on bond under the detention statute. The Second Circuit wrote that if the detention statute applies, he "must be detained during the removal process with no opportunity for release on bond".
For about thirty years, by the Second Circuit's account, people who entered without inspection could ask for bond. Entered without inspection, in a 1997 rule, means present without having been admitted or paroled. The practice did not cover a person caught while entering or shortly afterward, or a person held on a criminal or terrorism ground. On July 10, 2025, a memorandum from the commissioner of Customs and Border Protection said the detention statute covers them instead. On September 5, 2025, the Board of Immigration Appeals said immigration judges lack authority to grant them bond. On September 26, 2025, officers arrested him while he was driving to work in Norwood, Massachusetts. An immigration judge denied him a bond hearing. A district court ordered a bond hearing or release within ten days. On April 28, 2026, the Second Circuit ruled the other way from the Board.
The detention statute uses two descriptions of the person, an applicant for admission and an alien seeking admission. It tells an immigration officer to detain that person for a removal proceeding when the officer decides the person seeking admission is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted.
Detention statute · 2024 compilation · Inspection of applicants for admission
in the case of an alien who is an applicant for admission, if the examining immigration officer determines that an alien seeking admission is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted, the alien shall be detained
Government Publishing Office
Before 1996, the procedures for detaining and expelling a person depended largely on whether the person had physically entered the United States. Someone who entered illegally without being caught was eligible for bond. Someone who presented for inspection at a port of entry was to be detained unless clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to enter, or unless granted parole. In 1996 Congress enacted the removal and detention provisions this case concerns. The Justice Department's petition says Congress acted to correct that difference, between people who had entered illegally and people who presented for inspection at a port of entry. Congress replaced the defined term entry with admission. Members of Congress described lawful admission, rather than physical entry, as the pivotal factor. The government contends that Barbosa da Cunha is subject to mandatory detention because he is an applicant for admission who is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted. It argues that "the ordinary meanings of the phrases 'applicant for admission' and 'seeking admission' are the same."
In his August 21 response to that petition, he says that in the detention provisions Congress largely kept the earlier scheme. People arrested inside the country, including "those who had entered without admission," remained eligible for release on bond, and people apprehended at the border continued to be detained without the possibility of bond.
The Second Circuit held that he may be an applicant for admission but is not seeking admission, because he is not requesting lawful entry after inspection and authorization. He was already living here. The court held that the detention statute does not apply to someone already in the United States who entered without inspection and was not caught while entering or shortly afterward. It affirmed the district court's order, which had required a bond hearing or release. Under the July 10 memorandum, an applicant for admission may not be released from custody except by parole, and the parole statute allows that release "only on a case-by-case basis for urgent humanitarian reasons or significant public benefit".

Opinion in his case
He entered around 2005, lived in the United States for more than twenty years, applied for asylum in 2016, and held a work permit. He has never been arrested for or charged with a crime. Eight appeals courts held that the bond statute applies to noncitizens inside the country, like the people who brought those cases, and that they must be given a bond hearing: the First, Second, Third, Sixth, Seventh, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits. On September 10, 2026, the Fourth Circuit joined them. The Fifth and Eighth Circuits held that the detention statute applies to all noncitizens who have not been admitted, and that they must be detained without bond.
The government's petition asked the Court to wait on this case. It said the Court should hold this petition pending another petition, Lopez-Campos, which it called a "better vehicle" for resolving whether holding those people is lawful, and then dispose of this petition as appropriate. The October 1 order granted this petition.
The catch
CBS's tens of thousands
CBS News wrote that the Court would consider whether the administration can detain tens of thousands of immigrants during removal proceedings without bond hearings. No court or agency record gives the number of people detained because of the July 2025 change. The Fourth Circuit says district courts sided with the petitioners in over twenty thousand individual cases, which counts court cases, not people in detention.
Who the policy reaches
Customs and Border Protection's public notice describes mandatory detention for applicants for admission the Department of Homeland Security encounters for the first time after they fail to lawfully present for inspection at a port of entry. The July 10 memorandum says the first encounter counts "regardless of when or where that first encounter occurs". So the policy reaches a person arrested inside the country years after entering: Barbosa da Cunha was arrested driving to work in Norwood more than twenty years after he entered.

On September 25, 2026, the full Second Circuit refused to rehear the case. Judge Steven J. Menashi dissented, arguing that the detention statute applies and that Barbosa da Cunha must be detained without a bond hearing. Judge Matthew A. Schwartz voted against rehearing while writing that he agreed with the dissent's analysis. He wrote that mere substantive disagreement with the panel opinion does not warrant the time and effort of full court rehearing.
Open questions
What we do not know yet
No day for argument has been set. The Court agreed to decide what the detention statute requires, not whether holding someone without a bond hearing violates the Constitution's guarantee of due process. The government's petition says Lopez-Campos, from the Sixth Circuit, also presents that due-process question. Genalo v. G.M., a related constitutional case set for argument on October 13, was dismissed on September 11. On September 21 the government filed a new petition, Genalo v. D.C., with a response due October 21, and Barbosa da Cunha's lawyer wrote that it presents the same constitutional issues as the dismissed case. On September 14 and September 25 that lawyer asked the Court to grant only the statutory question in this case and in Buenrostro-Mendez. As of the October 5 dockets, the Court had not granted or denied Lopez-Campos or Genalo v. D.C. The October 3 docket in Buenrostro-Mendez, from the Fifth Circuit, listed no grant or denial.
