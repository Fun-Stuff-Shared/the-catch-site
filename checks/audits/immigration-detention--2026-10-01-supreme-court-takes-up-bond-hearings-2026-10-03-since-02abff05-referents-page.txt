The grant
What happened
On October 1, 2026, the Supreme Court granted review of whether the detention statute requires holding, during removal proceedings, people who, like Ricardo Aparecido Barbosa da Cunha, are in the United States and were not admitted. The October 1 order grants review and does not decide the case. The bond statute is the statute under which the government may release someone.
The detention statute uses two descriptions of the person, an applicant for admission and an alien seeking admission. It tells an immigration officer to detain that person for a removal proceeding when the officer decides the person seeking admission is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted.
Detention statute · 2024 compilation · Inspection of applicants for admission
in the case of an alien who is an applicant for admission, if the examining immigration officer determines that an alien seeking admission is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted, the alien shall be detained
Government Publishing Office
The Second Circuit held that Barbosa da Cunha may be an applicant for admission and is not seeking admission, and held that the detention statute does not apply to someone in the United States after entering without inspection and admission who was not apprehended while entering the country or shortly thereafter. The Second Circuit affirmed the district court, which had required a bond hearing or release. A bond hearing is a hearing on release. Release in his case followed a finding that he was not a danger and not a flight risk. The hearing did not itself require release. He has never been arrested for or charged with a crime. The Second Circuit's April 28 opinion calls Barbosa da Cunha the petitioner. Under the Department of Homeland Security's July 10 memorandum, an applicant for admission may not be released from custody except by parole. That memorandum says the only people eligible for release on bond are people already admitted.
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit · April 28, 2026 · Opinion
Petitioner may be an “applicant for admission,” but he is not “seeking admission.”
Barbosa da Cunha
Barbosa da Cunha entered around 2005, lived in the United States for more than twenty years, applied for asylum in 2016, and held a work permit. On September 26, 2025, officers arrested him while he was driving to work in Norwood, Massachusetts. An immigration judge denied a bond hearing. A district court then ordered a bond hearing or release within ten days, and an immigration judge released him on bond after finding no danger and no flight risk. The question the government put to the Court is whether the detention statute requires holding people in that situation for the removal proceedings. Its memorandum says an applicant for admission may not be released except by parole. By the grant, the dispute was no longer his case alone, or only the Fifth Circuit and the Eighth Circuit, the two appeals courts whose divided panels the April opinion said had agreed with the government.
The government's petition asked the Court to wait on this case. It said the Court should hold this petition pending another petition, Lopez-Campos, which it called a "better vehicle" for resolving whether holding those people is lawful, and then dispose of this petition as appropriate. The October 1 order granted this petition.
In July 2025 the government changed the executive practice of bond for people present without having been admitted or paroled. The Second Circuit describes it as executive practice over thirty years.

On March 6, 1997, a Federal Register rule said that people present without having been admitted or paroled would be eligible for bond and bond redetermination. The Second Circuit describes the executive practice that followed as thirty years old. In July 2025 the government changed that practice and said the detention statute, rather than the bond statute, is the detention authority for every applicant for admission. On September 5, 2025, the Board of Immigration Appeals said immigration judges lack authority to grant bond to people present in the United States without admission. On April 28, 2026, the Second Circuit held the opposite for someone already present who was not apprehended while entering the country or shortly thereafter, and it affirmed. On September 25 the full court refused rehearing. Judge Steven J. Menashi dissented and argued that the detention statute applies and that Barbosa da Cunha must be detained without a bond hearing. Judge Matthew A. Schwartz voted to deny rehearing and wrote that he agreed with that dissent. On October 1 the Supreme Court granted review.

What we do not know yet
This case has a grant date of October 1 and no argument day on the list of cases set for argument. The October 2 docket's last disposition is the grant, and later filings go through the electronic system. The question the Court accepted is the statute. The government's petition says a petition from the Sixth Circuit in Lopez-Campos also presents whether applying the detention statute comports with due process. The Second Circuit said it need not rely on constitutional avoidance, the practice of reading a statute so as not to decide a constitutional problem. The petition says the Second Circuit did not reach a standalone holding on the constitutionality of detaining him without a bond hearing. A petition from the Fifth Circuit in Buenrostro-Mendez was distributed for the September 28 conference, then rescheduled, and those proceedings list no grant. The Sixth Circuit petition was distributed for that same conference, and its proceedings list no grant.
The October 2 docket lists a response filed on August 21 and a reply filed on September 4, and that page does not link either document. The Justice Department file is the petition, not the response or the reply.
