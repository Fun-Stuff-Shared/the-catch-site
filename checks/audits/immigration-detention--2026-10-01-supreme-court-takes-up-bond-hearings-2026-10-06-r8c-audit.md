## 8\. Reconstructed Story

Before 1996, detention treatment largely turned on physical entry: people who had entered and were placed in deportation proceedings could seek bond, while people seeking entry at the border were generally detained. Congress replaced that entry framework with admission in 1996, but the legislative history does not unambiguously resolve this detention dispute. One report made lawful admission pivotal to status, while the conference report described the new §1226(a) as restating existing bond authority. The 1997 implementing rule expressly preserved bond eligibility for people present without admission.

That remained the executive practice for roughly thirty years. In July 2025 the government changed course, treating such people as subject to mandatory detention under §1225\. The BIA adopted that reading. Barbosa da Cunha, who had lived in the country for more than twenty years, was arrested driving to work, denied a bond hearing under the new interpretation, then obtained release after federal habeas relief and an immigration judge's finding that he posed neither danger nor flight risk.

The courts then split. Nine circuits ultimately applied the bond statute to interior residents in this position; the Fifth and Eighth Circuits applied mandatory detention. The Second Circuit's textual hinge was that Barbosa da Cunha could be an “applicant for admission” without presently “seeking admission.”

On October 1 the Supreme Court granted Rhoney to decide that statutory dispute. It has not decided the merits. The due-process question remains on a separate track involving Lopez-Campos and the Genalo litigation.
