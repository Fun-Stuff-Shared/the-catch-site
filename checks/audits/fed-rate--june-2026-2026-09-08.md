# Audit result

The story is materially incomplete. Its central frame treats June as the start of a hawkish split, but that disagreement was already public in April. It also misstates a major SEP number, describes participant projections as the committee’s view, imports May inflation data that was released after the meeting, and omits July minutes available before its stated revision check.

I read all 39,895 bytes of the built page. The manifest contains two text pins and zero video pins, so the applicable video-frame denominator was zero. The audit took about 21 minutes. It is bounded only around first-party historical market snapshots and some outlet revision histories, not around the primary policy record.

## Findings, ranked by materiality

### 1. HIGH | The unemployment projection is wrong

**Layer:** Verification

- **Page bytes:** [index.html](/Volumes/4/GitHub/the-catch-site/dist/events/fed-rate/june-2026/index.html:1), bytes `11548-11612`: `Unemployment rate, 2026 Q4</td><td>4.0%</td><td>4.2%`
- The same page later contradicts the table at bytes `21435-21567`: `4.3%, slightly down from the 4.4% projected in March`.
- **Record bytes:** [fomcprojtabl20260617.txt](/Volumes/4/GitHub/the-catch-site/data/sources/fomcprojtabl20260617.txt:353), bytes `10020-10052`: `Unemployment rate\n4.3\n4.3\n4.2\n4.2`; bytes `10134-10165`: `March projection\n4.4\n4.3\n4.2\n4.2`. The [official SEP table](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcprojtabl20260617.htm) reports the same figures.

**Correct account:** The 2026 Q4 median fell from 4.4 percent in March to 4.3 percent in June. It did not rise from 4.0 to 4.2. This is a direct factual error in a centerpiece table.

### 2. HIGH | The headline converts participant forecasts into a committee position and overstates their conviction

**Layers:** Verification and completeness discovery

- **Page bytes:** bytes `2381-2457`: `Warsh&#39;s first unanimous vote, with half the committee penciling in a hike`; bytes `10226-10274`: `The unanimous vote was not the day's real signal.`
- **Record bytes:** [SEP pin](/Volumes/4/GitHub/the-catch-site/data/sources/fomcprojtabl20260617.txt:453), bytes `12254-12348`: `Eighteen participants submitted information...`; its dot distribution at bytes `14920-14980` is 9 above the current midpoint, 8 at it, and 1 below it.
- The [June press-conference transcript](https://www.federalreserve.gov/mediacenter/files/FOMCpresconf20260617.pdf) says participants did not feel bound by the dots and Warsh “didn’t hear tons of conviction.”
- The separate [discount-rate minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/monetary20260714a1.pdf) show all twelve Reserve Bank boards had requested retaining the 3.75 percent primary credit rate, and the Board expressed no sentiment for changing it.

**Correct account:** Eighteen of nineteen meeting participants submitted individual, conditional projections. Nine projected a higher year-end rate, eight projected the current midpoint, and one projected a cut. The formal twelve-member committee unanimously held. The dots reveal a divided distribution, not “half the committee” deciding or confidently intending to hike. The page itself correctly faults Fox Business for making essentially this category error.

### 3. HIGH | The inflation claim leaks post-meeting data into the meeting-time record

**Layer:** Verification

- **Page bytes:** bytes `15751-15876` claim `63 consecutive months`; bytes `15908-15964` call May’s 4.1 percent reading the `latest pre-meeting reading`.
- **Record bytes:** [June minutes pin](/Volumes/4/GitHub/the-catch-site/data/sources/fomcminutes20260617.txt:319), bytes `14366-14523` contain `was 3.8 percent in April`; bytes `15406-15482` say staff `estimated` May inflation at 4.1 percent.
- BEA did not release May PCE until June 25, eight days after the meeting. The [May release](https://www.bea.gov/index.php/news/2026/personal-income-and-outlays-may-2026) identifies June 25 as its publication date.

**Correct account:**

- At the June 17 meeting: the latest published PCE reading was April at 3.8 percent, making the reported above-target streak 62 months. May 4.1 percent was a staff estimate.
- By the page’s August 23 check: June had been reported at 3.7 percent, extending the streak to 64 months.
- As of September 8: [BEA reports July PCE at 3.7 percent](https://bea.gov/data/personal-consumption-expenditures-price-index), extending the streak to 65 months.

The 63-month calculation is arithmetically valid through May, but its temporal label is false and its endpoint was already stale on the page’s stated check date.

### 4. HIGH | The story begins the policy split too late

**Layer:** Completeness discovery

- **Page bytes:** bytes `17108-17196`: `The hawkish undercurrent visible in June's dots and minutes became formal six weeks later`; bytes `17456-17502`: `June's unanimity was the calm before that split`.
- **Underlying record:** The [April 29 FOMC statement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260429a.htm) records Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan voting against the action because they opposed its easing bias, although they supported holding the rate.
- The [April minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20260429.htm) say a majority believed firming could become appropriate if inflation persisted.

**Correct account:** The same three officials who voted for a July hike had already registered formal dissents in April over the policy-path language. June temporarily reunited them around a hold after the easing bias was removed. July changed the object of their dissent from communications to the current rate action. June was not the beginning of the split or simply “calm before” it.

### 5. HIGH | The forward chronology stops despite a major record already being available

**Layer:** Completeness discovery

- **Page bytes:** bytes `32170-32239`: `No changes are recorded in this view. Changes last checked 2026-08-23.`
- **Record:** The Fed released the [July minutes on August 19](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260819a.htm), four days before that check.
- Those [July minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20260729.htm) say several participants favored an immediate increase, many expected tightening if inflation did not decline, and the participants’ overall economic assessments were little changed from June.
- Kashkari’s [own dissent statement](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/article/2026/statement-on-my-fomc-dissent) explains that he preferred incremental tightening to reduce the risk of entrenched inflation.

**Correct account:** July confirmed a broader conditional tightening camp, while most participants still preferred to wait for information. The dissenters’ reasons, the majority’s wait-and-see rationale, and the fact that economic assessments were “little changed” are necessary to explain what changed and what did not. The omission cannot be excused by publication timing.

The page is also no longer current. Warsh’s [August 28 speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/warsh20260828a.htm) prioritized inflation but committed “to a discipline, not to a decision.” Waller’s [September 3 speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/waller20260903a.htm) was explicitly conditional: hold if disinflation continued, consider a hike if inflation came in hot. The [August jobs report](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm) subsequently showed 162,000 jobs added and 4.1 percent unemployment.

### 9. MEDIUM | The percentile is computed correctly, but its policy interpretation is not

**Layer:** Verification

- **Page bytes:** bytes `8973-9042`: `48th percentile`; bytes `9144-9189`: `Not a high-rate regime by historical standards`; bytes `9946-10014`: `prior cycles treated as ordinary`.
- Recalculation from the pinned DFEDTAR and DFEDTARU series produced 15,970 daily observations, a midpoint percentile of 48.494 percent, mean 3.8225, and median 4.0.

**Correct account:** The arithmetic is reproducible. The interpretation is not established by that calculation because it:

- compares a pre-2008 point target with the post-2008 target range’s upper bound;
- calendar-weights long holding periods;
- begins in September 1982, excluding the earlier Volcker period while speaking broadly of “historical standards”;
- compares nominal rates without contemporaneous inflation, neutral-rate estimates, or the real policy stance.

The June minutes themselves show disagreement over restrictiveness, and Warsh described policy transmission as uneven. The statistic supports “middle of this particular nominal series,” not “ordinary” in an economic or household sense.

## Reconstructed story

The policy disagreement preceded Warsh’s first meeting. On April 29, the Fed held at 3.50-3.75 percent. Miran wanted a cut. Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan supported the hold but formally dissented from language implying an easing bias. April’s minutes show a majority already believed persistent inflation could require firming.

On June 17, Warsh’s first FOMC meeting produced a genuine 12-0 consensus on the current action. The statement was shortened and the easing bias was removed, which resolved the April communications dispute for that meeting. All twelve Reserve Bank boards had separately supported retaining the primary credit rate.

The SEP showed disagreement about the path, not a committee decision. Eighteen of nineteen participants submitted projections. Nine placed the year-end rate above the current midpoint, eight at it, and one below it. The median moved from 3.4 to 3.8 percent. Warsh submitted no projection and explicitly warned that the dots were provisional and lacked strong conviction. The correct unemployment forecast moved from 4.4 to 4.3 percent.

At the meeting, the latest published PCE inflation figure was April’s 3.8 percent. Staff estimated May at 4.1 percent. The actual May release came afterward. The June minutes consequently support both sides of the policy frame: a few saw a present case for raising rates, while many participants described conditional paths in which inflation could recede without a hike.

On July 29, the Fed again held, this time 9-3. The same three officials who had opposed April’s easing language now wanted a quarter-point increase. The July minutes show that several favored an immediate hike and many expected tightening if inflation failed to decline, but most preferred to wait for more evidence.

As of September 8, the target remains 3.50-3.75 percent. July PCE inflation is 3.7 percent, core PCE is 3.3 percent, and August unemployment is 4.1 percent. Warsh remains inflation-focused but has not committed to a September action. Waller has publicly described a conditional choice between another hold and a hike. The current story is therefore a persistent, previously visible split over inflation risks and timing, not a June consensus concealing a cleanly emerging hike decision.

Attempts to disprove this reconstruction failed:

- Treating the dots as a majority decision fails against the participant denominator and Warsh’s own caveat.
- Treating July as a new split fails against the April dissents.
- Treating May PCE as meeting-time evidence fails against BEA’s June 25 release date.
- Treating June as broadly hawkish fails against the unanimous FOMC action, the discount-rate proceedings, and the 8 hold plus 1 cut projections.
