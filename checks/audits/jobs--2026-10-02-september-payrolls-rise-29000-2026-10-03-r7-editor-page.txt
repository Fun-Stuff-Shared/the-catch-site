The jobs report · September 2026
Payrolls rose by an estimated 29,000 in September, a change too small for the survey to tell from none
Economists surveyed by Reuters expected payrolls to rise by 90,000. The Bureau of Labor Statistics said payrolls and the 4.2 percent unemployment rate both changed little. July and August together are 60,000 lower than previously reported.

What happened
The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Friday, October 2, 2026, that payroll employment rose by 29,000 in September and the unemployment rate was 4.2 percent, and said both changed little.
Private payrolls rose by 46,000 and government payrolls fell by 17,000.
The bureau also revised the two prior months down by 60,000 together: August from a gain of 162,000 to 133,000, and July from a gain of 21,000 to a loss of 10,000.
The establishment survey counts jobs on employer payrolls, and employees appearing on more than one payroll are counted separately. The household survey counts people, and individuals are counted only once. A jobs number and a people number can move differently in the same month. The payroll count comes from the first survey, and the unemployment rate from the second.
The Federal Reserve's two goals are maximum employment and price stability, and on September 16 it raised its target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point. This is the last monthly jobs report before its committee meets on October 27-28; the next one, for October, is scheduled for November 6. On prices, the personal consumption expenditures price index for August was up 3.4 percent from a year earlier, and this report put average hourly earnings up 3.0 percent over the past 12 months.
The catch
USA Today headlined the release “September jobs report shows slowdown in hiring, unemployment ticks up”. Neither half is a change the bureau itself reports: its summary says payrolls and the unemployment rate both “changed little”. On hiring, the report does not count hires. Its 29,000 is the net change in payroll jobs, and the bureau says a monthly change has to be about 122,000 before it is statistically significant, that is, larger than the survey's margin of error. Hires, “all additions to the payroll” during the month, are counted in a separate survey, and September's count is scheduled for November 3.
By the household survey's estimates, employment rose by 406,000, which is under the 650,000 that survey needs before a change is statistically significant. The labor force, the employed plus the unemployed, rose by 485,000. The unemployment rate is the number unemployed as a percent of the labor force; the labor force grew by more than employment did, so the count of unemployed rose by 78,000 and the rate went from 4.1 to 4.2 percent.
Most of that tenth is rounding. Before rounding, the rate “increased slightly from 4.141% to 4.175%”, the St. Louis Fed wrote on October 2, a rise of about 0.03 point. The rate has stayed between 4.1 percent and 4.3 percent since March.
The bureau named one group whose rate increased: Black workers, from 6.0 percent in August to 7.0 percent in September. A year earlier it was 7.6 percent.

September's 29,000 followed August's revised 133,000. It is below the 45,000 a month the bureau reports for the prior 12 months and below the 68,000 a month the Associated Press counts for 2026 so far, and above the 9,700 a month the Associated Press counts for 2025.
What we do not know yet
The payroll figures for August and September are preliminary: the bureau says they rest on incomplete returns from the employers it surveys. Whether hiring slowed is not known yet. Hires are counted in the bureau's Job Openings and Labor Turnover survey; the newest count is August's, 5.2 million, and September's is scheduled for November 3.
