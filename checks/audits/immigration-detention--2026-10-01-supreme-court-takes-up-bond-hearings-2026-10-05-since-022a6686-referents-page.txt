Immigration detention · October 1, 2026 · updated 2026-10-03
The Supreme Court agreed to decide whether people already in the country who were never admitted must be held without a bond hearing
The Court's October 1 order takes the case and decides nothing yet. Since July 2025 the government has said people arrested inside the country who had entered without inspection must be held, and the appeals courts have split.
The grant
What happened
On October 1, 2026, the Supreme Court agreed to decide whether the government must hold, while their removal cases are pending, people who are living in the United States and were never admitted. The order takes the case; it does not decide it. The detention statute deems a person present in the United States who has not been admitted an applicant for admission. The bond statute says the government "may release" a person on bond.
For about thirty years, by the Second Circuit's account, people who entered without inspection and were not admitted or paroled, and who were not apprehended while entering or shortly afterward or held on a criminal or terrorism ground, could ask for bond. On July 10, 2025, a memorandum from the commissioner of Customs and Border Protection said the detention statute covers them instead. On September 5, 2025, the Board of Immigration Appeals said immigration judges lack authority to grant them bond. On September 26, 2025, officers arrested Ricardo Aparecido Barbosa da Cunha while he was driving to work in Norwood, Massachusetts. An immigration judge denied him a bond hearing. A district court ordered a bond hearing or release within ten days. An immigration judge released him on bond after finding no danger and no flight risk. On April 28, 2026, the Second Circuit ruled the other way from the Board in Barbosa da Cunha's case.
The detention statute uses two descriptions of the person, an applicant for admission and an alien seeking admission. It tells an immigration officer to detain that person for a removal proceeding when the officer decides the person seeking admission is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted.
Detention statute · 2024 compilation · Inspection of applicants for admission
in the case of an alien who is an applicant for admission, if the examining immigration officer determines that an alien seeking admission is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted, the alien shall be detained
Government Publishing Office
Before 1996, the procedures for detaining and expelling a person depended largely on whether the person had physically entered the United States. Someone who entered illegally without being caught was eligible for bond. Someone who presented for inspection at a port of entry was to be detained unless clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to enter, or unless granted parole. In 1996 Congress enacted the removal and detention provisions this case concerns. It acted to correct that disparity and replaced the defined term entry with admission: the lawful entry of a person into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer. Members of Congress described lawful admission, rather than physical entry, as the pivotal factor. The government contends that Barbosa da Cunha is subject to mandatory detention because he is an applicant for admission who is not clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to be admitted. It argues that "the ordinary meanings of the phrases 'applicant for admission' and 'seeking admission' are the same."
The Second Circuit held that Barbosa da Cunha may be an applicant for admission but is not seeking admission, because he is not requesting lawful entry after inspection and authorization; he was already living here. So the court held that the detention statute does not apply to someone in the United States after entering without inspection and admission who was not apprehended while entering the country or shortly thereafter. The Second Circuit affirmed the district court, which had required a bond hearing or release. Under the July 10 memorandum, an applicant for admission may not be released from custody except by parole. Parole is a separate power. The parole statute lets the government release a person "only on a case-by-case basis for urgent humanitarian reasons or significant public benefit". The July 10 memorandum says the only people eligible for release on bond are people already admitted.

Petitioner may be an “applicant for admission,” but he is not “seeking admission.”
Barbosa da Cunha
Barbosa da Cunha entered around 2005, lived in the United States for more than twenty years, applied for asylum in 2016, and held a work permit. He has never been arrested for or charged with a crime. If the government wins, people in his situation cannot be released on bond under the detention statute. The Second Circuit wrote that if the detention statute applies, he "must be detained during the removal process with no opportunity for release on bond". The question the Court agreed to decide is whether the detention statute requires that detention. Whether the Constitution separately requires a hearing is not part of the question. The Fourth Circuit names eight courts, the First, Second, Third, Sixth, Seventh, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh, that held the bond statute applies to noncitizens inside the country, like the people who filed the cases, and that they must be given a bond hearing. On September 10, 2026, that court joined them. The Fifth and the Eighth held that the detention statute applies to all noncitizens who have not been admitted, and that they must be detained without bond.
The government's petition asked the Court to wait on this case. It said the Court should hold this petition pending another petition, Lopez-Campos, which it called a "better vehicle" for resolving whether holding those people is lawful, and then dispose of this petition as appropriate. The October 1 order granted this petition.
The catch
CBS's tens of thousands
CBS News wrote that the Court would consider whether the administration can detain tens of thousands of immigrants during removal proceedings without bond hearings. The October 1 order, the Supreme Court's docket for this case, the Second Circuit's opinion, the Board of Immigration Appeals decision, and the July 10 Customs memorandum do not give a precise count of people held under the government's reading. The Fourth Circuit says district courts sided with the petitioners in over twenty thousand individual cases, which counts court cases, not people in detention.
Who the policy reaches
Customs and Border Protection's public notice describes mandatory detention for applicants for admission the Department of Homeland Security encounters for the first time after they fail to lawfully present for inspection at a port of entry. The July 10 memorandum says the first encounter counts "regardless of when or where that first encounter occurs". So the policy reaches a person arrested inside the country years after entering: Barbosa da Cunha was arrested driving to work in Norwood more than twenty years after he entered.

On September 25, 2026, the full Second Circuit refused to rehear the case. Judge Steven J. Menashi dissented, arguing that the detention statute applies and that Barbosa da Cunha must be detained without a bond hearing. Judge Matthew A. Schwartz voted against rehearing while writing that he agreed with the dissent's analysis. He wrote that mere substantive disagreement with the panel opinion does not warrant the time and effort of full court rehearing.
Open questions
What we do not know yet
No argument date has been set. The Court took one question, what the detention statute means. Whether holding someone without a bond hearing violates the Constitution's guarantee of due process is not part of it; the government's petition says a second case, Lopez-Campos from the Sixth Circuit, raises that question as well. The Court had not granted or denied review in that case or in a third, Buenrostro-Mendez from the Fifth Circuit, as of October 3.
