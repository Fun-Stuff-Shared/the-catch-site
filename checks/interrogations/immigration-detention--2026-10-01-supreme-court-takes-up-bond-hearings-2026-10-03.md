The page is a completeness review of the October 1 Supreme Court grant on mandatory immigration detention. I'll read the audit method, then check the grant, the statutes, and the reaction the page leaves out.The page stays inside the grant, the Second Circuit opinion, and three news write-ups. I'm checking the petition, the Second Circuit facts, and whether the government actually wanted a different case.The page is right that the October 1 order only grants review of a statutory question. It misses the fight over which case the Court would take, who is actually detained where, and the policy memo that produced this arrest. No White House post or prediction-market contract on this petition turned up; the Kalshi contract below is a different detention case.

1. The government asked the Court to defer this petition, not to decide it. Solicitor General D. John Sauer’s July 23, 2026 petition says the Court “should hold this petition pending the disposition of the petition in Lopez-Campos” because that Sixth Circuit case “is a better vehicle” and “squarely presents” both the statute and due process (https://www.justice.gov/d9/2026-07/barbosa_da_cunha_hold_pet_-_final.pdf, filed 2026-07-23). The page’s line that the government “asked the Court to hold that the detention statute requires holding” people reads as the opposite request. Amy Howe reported on October 1 that the Court “took up the issue, but not the administration’s preferred case,” Putra v. Lopez-Campos (https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/10/court-agrees-to-hear-arguments-in-important-immigration-case-religious-zoning-dispute/, 2026-10-01).

2. Philip L. Rhoney is not a private petitioner. He is Acting Field Office Director of ICE’s Buffalo office, substituted for his predecessor under Supreme Court Rule 35.3; Sauer is counsel of record (same petition, parties page). Respondent’s counsel on the docket are Michael K.T. Tan of the ACLU and Matthew H. Adams; the ACLU’s October 1 release also names the New York Civil Liberties Union and Paul Dwyer, and quotes Tan, Amy Belsher, and Dwyer (https://www.aclu.org/press-releases/aclu-comment-on-supreme-court-agreeing-to-hear-challenge-over-trump-mandatory-detention-policy, 2026-10-01). Without that, the caption reads as one man suing another.

3. The grant does not pause the split, and the page never says who is on which side. As of the Fourth Circuit’s September 10, 2026 decision, nine circuits had rejected the policy (First, Second, Third, Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, Ninth, Tenth, Eleventh) and two had accepted it (Fifth, Buenrostro-Mendez; Eighth, Avila, a 2–1 panel) (https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/trumps-mandatory-detention-policy-rejected-by-fourth-circuit, 2026-09-10). The July petition already listed Second, Sixth, Tenth, and Eleventh against the government, so “the two appeals courts the April opinion named” is stale by the petition the page itself cites. Until a decision, bond depends on where ICE holds the person. The October 1 order grants review; it is not a stay.

4. The arrest-and-release sequence in the record is not “a judge released him.” ICE took him at a traffic stop in Norwood on an administrative warrant that cited the bond statute, § 1226; the immigration judge then denied a bond hearing under § 1225(b)(2)(A); he filed habeas in Massachusetts the day he was detained; the case moved to the Western District of New York after ICE transferred him to Buffalo; Judge Meredith A. Vacca ordered a bond hearing within ten days or release; the hearing was October 28, 2025, and he was released October 29 (petition, pp. 8–10; district docket Barbosa Da Cunha v. Freden, No. 6:25-cv-06532, https://dockets.justia.com/docket/new-york/nywdce/6:2025cv06532/157692). The Second Circuit opinion also says the government concedes he is neither a danger nor a flight risk, that his asylum application has been pending since 2016, and that he lives in Massachusetts with his wife and two U.S.-citizen children, owns his home, and runs a small construction business (https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/25-3141/25-3141-2026-04-28.html, opinion of 2026-04-28). The petition says he entered around 2004 or 2005, not simply “around 2005,” and charges inadmissibility under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(a)(6)(A)(i) and § 1182(a)(7)(A)(i)(I).

5. The policy that governed this ICE arrest is Acting ICE Director Todd M. Lyons’s July 8, 2025 “Interim Guidance Regarding Detention Authority for Applicants for Admission,” which the petition quotes: applicants for admission “may not be released from ICE custody except by parole” under § 1182(d)(5), and bond is limited to people admitted and then charged with deportability (petition, p. 8). The page treats the July 10 CBP memo as the department’s reading and never names Lyons or that memo (AILA copy notice: https://www.aila.org/library/ice-memo-interim-guidance-regarding-detention-authority-for-applications-for-admission, 2025-07-08; tracking summary: https://immpolicytracking.org/policies/ice-issues-memo-eliminating-bond-hearings-for-undocumented-immigrants). That summary also says DHS is not treating earlier releases as parole, so “the only way out of custody is parole” is the rule for people then in custody, not a roundup of everyone already released. Parole is not admission, and the policy is being applied to people whose parole ended, not only to entries without inspection.

6. Due process is the dispute the grant sidesteps, and the page only gestures at it. The petition says the Second Circuit did not decide the constitutional claim and used due process only as a backup if the statute were ambiguous, while calling the due-process concern “grave.” A Fifth Circuit panel had held that detention under § 1225(b)(2)(A) for more than ninety days required a bond hearing; the full court vacated that decision on July 10, 2026 (Sosnava Rodriguez v. Ortega, petition footnote, p. 13). The district court found a due-process violation and then did not need to rest on it. Counsel’s September 25, 2026 letter asked that if the Court granted the separate case Genalo v. D.C., No. 26-379, it grant only the statutory question in this case and in Buenrostro-Mendez (https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/26/26-43/425720/20260925165931280_2026.09.25%20Letter%20re%20DC.pdf, 2026-09-25). “It need not rely on constitutional avoidance” buries that the constitutional cases are still live and that the government said this petition was the worse vehicle for them.

7. The page starts the law in 1997 and skips the cases and the statute Congress actually wrote. The petition’s argument runs through the 1996 Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act, Jennings v. Rodriguez, 583 U.S. 281 (2018), Nielsen v. Preap, and DHS v. Thuraissigiam. It also says the 1997 preamble the page quotes was contradicted by 8 C.F.R. § 235.3(b)(1)(ii), which said § 1225(b)(2)(A) did apply to some people already physically present after an unlawful entry, even while bond hearings continued (petition, p. 7). “Long-settled practice” is the courts’ account of the hearings; it is not an uncontested account of the regulation. The Laken Riley Act, Pub. L. No. 119-1 (2025), is a different mandatory-detention statute, § 1226(c), for people with specified criminal charges. A reader who hears “mandatory detention” this year will mix the two, and a win or loss here does not decide Laken Riley detentions.

8. The count the page knocks down is not the only count, and the denominators differ. Politico’s Kyle Cheney reported on October 1 that district judges had rejected the policy more than 20,000 times, including a majority of Trump appointees, across more than 460 judges, and that the appeals courts were 9–2 (https://www.politico.com/news/2026/10/01/supreme-court-trump-ice-detention-01103024, 2026-10-01; his post the same day, https://x.com/kyledcheney/status/2105653691474751672). The sortable table, current through September 1, 2026, lists 21,718 rulings, for and against (https://www.politico.com/interactives/2026/mandatory-detention/cases-table/). Those are rulings, not people held. Judge José A. Cabranes’s concurrence, in the opinion the page already uses, says the government’s reading would mean Congress ordered detention of “millions” and that nobody noticed for thirty years (CourtListener text of the rehearing discussion of that concurrence: https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10983473/barbosa-da-cunha-v-freden/). The page is right that “tens of thousands” is not the question granted. It never says what would replace it, or that “millions” is the class who could be covered and “20,000” is habeas outcomes.

9. The agency and the lawyers answered the grant; the page quotes neither. A DHS spokesperson told Newsweek on October 1: “DHS is glad that the Supreme Court will hear arguments regarding mandatory detention,” and said the policy “put the nail in the coffin” for the prior administration’s release practices (https://www.newsweek.com/trump-immigration-detention-case-supreme-court-12511961, 2026-10-01). On September 25, Senators Durbin and Padilla introduced a bill that would say border mandatory detention does not apply to people arrested in the interior and would require periodic detention hearings (https://www.durbin.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/durbin-padilla-introduce-new-legislation-to-prevent-unlawful-immigration-arrests-and-detentions, 2026-09-25). Earlier, on March 25, 2026, Attorney General Bondi called the Eighth Circuit’s ruling a “massive court victory against activist judges” (Politico, https://www.politico.com/news/2026/03/25/mandatory-detention-ruling-8th-circuit-00844386, 2026-03-25).

10. Matter of Yajure Hurtado did more than restate the July memo. The Board applied § 1225(b) to a Venezuelan man who had entered without inspection in November 2022 and had been granted temporary protected status, and it reversed the Board’s own recent precedent that had treated this custody as § 1226(a), including Matter of Q. Li and Matter of Akhmedov (Yale Law Journal essay, https://yalelawjournal.org/essay/welcome-to-the-trump-administrations-board-of-immigration-appeals-the-immigrant-always-loses-forum, 2026-09-25). A reader who has only the 1997 preamble thinks the Board broke a regulation. It also broke its own 2025 decisions.

11. Monday is still part of this conference. Howe wrote that further orders from the September 28 conference are due Monday, October 5, 2026, at 9:30 a.m. Eastern, before the term opens that morning (same SCOTUSblog piece, 2026-10-01). “No grant” on the Lopez-Campos and Buenrostro-Mendez dockets as of October 3 is not a denial; those petitions can still be held, denied, or relisted on that list. The same week, on September 29, the Court granted a stay and certiorari before judgment in a different immigration case, No. 26-426, on third-country removals, set for December (https://www.supremecourt.gov/docket/docketfiles/html/public/26a406.html, order of 2026-09-29). Argument in Rhoney is not set. Reporters say early 2027, with a decision expected by the end of the term; that is their forecast, not an order.

12. One sentence states the government’s theory as the statute. “The detention statute tells an immigration officer to detain a person for a removal proceeding when the officer decides that person is not clearly entitled to be admitted” drops “seeking admission,” which is the clause the case is about. The page quotes the clause later, but that earlier sentence is the reading the Second Circuit rejected. Separately, the only live market found is not this case: Kalshi’s contract resolves on whether the Court, in Genalo v. Black, requires a bond hearing for unreasonably prolonged detention under § 1226(c), not on § 1225(b)(2)(A) (https://kalshi.com/markets/kxscotuscase/supreme-court-decisions/kxscotuscase-genbla28).
