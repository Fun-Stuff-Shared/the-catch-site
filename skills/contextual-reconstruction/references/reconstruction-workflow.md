# Reconstruction Workflow

Use the parts that fit the subject, but preserve the distinctions between observation, cause, history, analogy, remedy overlap, propagation, and consequence.

## 1. Define the subject and current state

Identify the event, decision, system, institution, policy, claim, or trend being reconstructed. Establish scope, jurisdiction, timeframe, relevant actors, and the observable condition now.

Separate:

- direct observations;
- operational symptoms;
- official or interested-party explanations;
- supported causal findings;
- allegations and inference;
- unresolved facts.

Move from vague labels toward the affected system or mechanism. “Flights were cancelled” is an outcome; “flight-data-processing capacity degraded” identifies a more useful operational layer.

## 2. Build the event/state timeline

Trace meaningful state changes, not merely article publication times:

- earliest observable signal;
- internal or public acknowledgment;
- escalation;
- intervention or mitigation;
- recovery or present state;
- later revisions to the explanation.

Preserve timestamps, timezone, source, and whether each timestamp describes the event, observation, publication, or later update.

## 3. Reconstruct how the situation developed

Search backward for precursor conditions and decisions:

- earlier incidents and near misses;
- audits, investigations, citations, and litigation;
- regulations, legislation, standards, and oversight;
- maintenance, architecture, procurement, vendor, staffing, budget, or leadership choices;
- known vulnerabilities and warnings;
- capital projects and remediation programs;
- earlier promises, deadlines, and status claims;
- environmental, market, political, or institutional pressures.

Distinguish background that explains the system from evidence that directly caused the event.

## 4. Find meaningful analogues

Search for comparable incidents or cases at several levels:

- same component or exact failure mode;
- same system or architecture;
- same failure class or control objective;
- same institution or regulatory regime;
- similar propagation pattern;
- comparable downstream consequences.

For each analogue, state what is genuinely comparable, what differs, and what inference—if any—it supports. Do not treat analogy as proof.

## 5. Build a warning/remedy crosswalk

For every material earlier finding, recommendation, regulation, or promised improvement, determine:

- the problem identified;
- the exact remedy or control objective;
- owner and accountable institution;
- scope, affected system/component/process, and exclusions;
- promised and actual implementation dates;
- reported implementation status and evidence for it;
- whether the present situation falls within its functional scope;
- whether it was meant to prevent, detect, contain, recover from, or govern the relevant failure;
- whether the present evidence suggests nonimplementation, incomplete scope, ineffective design, operating failure, degraded recovery, or an unrelated failure mode.

Do not demand an identical bug. A resilience recommendation may be relevant because it was intended to prevent a single fault from creating system-wide consequences. Conversely, shared system proximity alone does not establish that the remedy failed.

Use direct labels:

- Directly implicated
- Functionally overlapping
- Potentially relevant
- Superficially similar
- Outside scope
- Insufficient evidence

## 6. Trace causal structure and propagation

Build the most defensible chain from initiating condition to wider consequences. Separate:

- background susceptibility;
- trigger;
- failed or absent control;
- containment boundary;
- operational response;
- network or institutional propagation;
- first-order outcome;
- second-order and persistent effects.

For each link, classify it as established, strongly supported, plausible, disputed, or unknown. Test alternate paths and avoid collapsing “happened before” into “caused.”

## 7. Map consequences

Investigate effects on relevant groups: operators, users or citizens, employees, suppliers, adjacent infrastructure, regulators, markets, government, legal exposure, budgets, politics, policy, and future system design.

Separate immediate effects, second-order propagation, and longer-term institutional or policy consequences. Quantify scale, denominator, duration, geography, and distribution when the data permits.

## 8. Interrogate explanations and evidence

Compare official language with technical records, logs, notices, filings, incident reports, status pages, operational data, expert analysis, employee or professional observations, and contemporaneous user evidence.

For early public signals, classify provenance:

- official;
- operator or employee;
- domain expert;
- affected professional;
- eyewitness or user;
- unsupported speculation.

Use weak signals as leads, not established facts. Trace claims to their earliest available source and identify common source lineages.

## 9. Reconstruct and discover the story

Set aside headlines and organize the subject into:

- Established
- Strongly supported
- Plausible
- Disputed
- Unknown
- Ruled out or contradicted

Only then identify candidate stories. Possible forms include the immediate event, failed or incomplete remediation, ignored warning, regulatory blind spot, cascading systems weakness, mismatch between compliance and real resilience, institutional incentive, or overlooked downstream consequence.

Rank candidate stories by evidence, materiality, novelty, affected scale, and what remains unproven. Do not manufacture a hidden story when the ordinary explanation best fits the evidence.

## 10. Adversarial pass and stopping rule

Ask:

- What alternative mechanism could explain the same observations?
- Which causal links rest on timing, analogy, or one interested source?
- What evidence would disprove the leading reconstruction?
- Which actors, records, prior incidents, or adjacent systems remain unchecked?
- Did a “completed” remedy address design, implementation, operation, or only documentation?
- Did the search overfit to an identical past incident and miss the same failure class?
- Did the search overgeneralize a similar failure into a false connection?
- What would a skeptical domain expert search for?

Investigate the highest-value unresolved avenues. Stop when additional readily discoverable evidence is unlikely to materially change the reconstruction, or clearly bound the result and identify the evidence needed next.
