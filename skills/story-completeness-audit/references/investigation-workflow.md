# Investigation Workflow

Use this reference for substantive completeness audits. Adapt depth to the stakes and size of the supplied story, but preserve the distinct verification, discovery, and reconstruction layers.

## 1. Map the supplied frame

Record the article’s apparent starting point and endpoint; included and excluded actors; temporal, geographic, jurisdictional, legal, and dataset scope; evidence classes; thesis; implied causal mechanism; and conclusion.

Ask why the account begins and ends there, who has agency outside its frame, and what records should exist if it is complete.

## 2. Build a material claim inventory

Extract claims whose failure or qualification could change a reasonable reader’s understanding. Include facts, dates, numbers, quotations, sequence, causality, comparisons, superlatives, policy or legal descriptions, responsibility, motive, intent, and words such as “blocked,” “approved,” “opposed,” “only,” “never,” or “first.”

For each important claim retain:

- exact wording and normalized proposition;
- named entities and time/geographic scope;
- cited evidence;
- passage-support status: supported, partially supported, unsupported, or ambiguous;
- broader-record status: confirmed, likely, disputed, uncertain, contradicted, or unverifiable;
- confidence and materiality.

Do not confuse “the source said P” with “P is true.”

## 3. Find the best evidence

Prefer original primary records, contemporaneous official records, direct transcripts or media, high-quality firsthand reporting, independent secondary reporting, analysis, then aggregation. Treat official records as proof of what an institution recorded or said, not automatic proof of every underlying assertion.

Look for legislation, amendments, roll calls, committee material, court dockets and rulings, regulatory notices, contracts, filings, audits, datasets, meeting minutes, testimony, procurement records, and archived versions.

## 4. Search outside the frame

Generate searches independently of the article’s source list and vocabulary. Use actor names, institutions, docket or bill identifiers, dates, locations, alternate descriptions, opposing terminology, historical names, and connected proceedings.

### Backward search

Find precursor events, earlier votes or drafts, prior litigation or investigations, historical baselines, previous stated positions, longstanding disputes, and earlier record versions. Determine whether a knowledgeable account would begin earlier.

### Forward search

Find later votes, implementation, appeals, settlements, rulings, investigations, revised figures, corrections, enforcement, disclosures, reversals, confirmations, and downstream effects. Ask what happened next and whether it changes the original interpretation.

### Horizontal search

Find parallel cases, companion legislation, adjacent agencies or jurisdictions, other involved companies, underlying funding, lobbying, oversight, audits, methodological documentation, and relevant datasets. Determine whether the account isolates an event from the system that produced it.

## 5. Test common omission classes

- **Missing actors:** decision-makers, counterparties, regulators, courts, implementing bodies, contractors, oversight bodies, affected groups, other chambers, or prior administrations.
- **Missing records:** logically expected primary records the article did not cite.
- **Procedural context:** final versus interim, substantive versus procedural, proposal versus rule, complaint versus ruling, cloture versus passage, preliminary versus final.
- **Denominators and baselines:** raw count versus rate, base values, population, historical comparison, inflation, seasonality, opportunity count, or expected frequency.
- **Counterevidence:** stronger or conflicting records, competing data, corrections, methodological criticism, exceptions, or evidence shifting responsibility.
- **Alternative explanations:** plausible mechanisms with actual evidentiary support; do not manufacture balance.
- **Scope limitations:** time period, jurisdiction, population, dataset, sample, procedural stage, or measurement method.
- **Temporal integrity:** publication and update times, archived versions, corrections, headline/body changes, and whether later evidence is being treated as contemporaneously known.

## 6. Analyze provenance and contradictions

Cluster sources by evidentiary lineage. Multiple domains derived from one wire story, press release, filing, anonymous source, official statement, or dataset count as one lineage for corroboration.

Normalize propositions before comparing them. Classify new assertions as duplicate, support, refinement, update, contradiction, or no match.

Before declaring contradiction, reconcile time period, procedural stage, units, denominator, population, geography, preliminary versus final, gross versus net, nominal versus real, calendar versus fiscal year, and prediction versus observed result.

Treat absence claims separately. Record the corpus, time range, query strategy, unavailable records, expected evidence if the event occurred, and confidence in coverage. Failure to find evidence is not itself proof of absence.

## 7. Reconstruct independently

Set aside the article’s framing and organize the evidence into Established, Strongly supported, Disputed, Unknown, and Contradicted.

Build a chronology of meaningful state changes rather than a list of publication dates. Compare this reconstruction with the article and identify where scope, causality, responsibility, severity, novelty, or confidence differs.

For overstated claims, write the strongest bounded version the evidence supports. Use: **Episode → direct evidence → contextual mechanism → counterevidence → bounded claim**.

## 8. Adversarial completeness pass

Generate a fresh list of what has not been checked, independent of the article’s structure. Consider other government bodies, courts, local and historical reporting, archives, inspector-general material, corporate or campaign-finance filings, procurement records, FOIA releases, hearing transcripts, opposition filings, and methodological documentation.

Attempt to disprove the reconstruction. Ask what evidence would make it wrong, which assumptions were trusted implicitly, what terms a serious dissenter would use, whether repetition masqueraded as corroboration, whether confirming evidence ended the search, whether the selected dates are convenient, and which expected records remain unchecked.

Investigate the most promising unresolved avenues. Stop when no readily discoverable high-probability avenue is likely to materially change the result, or clearly label the audit’s limits.

## Materiality scale

- **Critical:** Changes the central conclusion or makes a material statement false.
- **Major:** Substantially changes interpretation, responsibility, causality, chronology, or confidence.
- **Moderate:** Context a well-informed reader would reasonably expect.
- **Minor:** Useful nuance that does not materially change the story.
- **Cosmetic:** Precision or presentation only.
